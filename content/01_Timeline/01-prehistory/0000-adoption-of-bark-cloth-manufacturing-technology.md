## Background

Long before the integration of the Pearl River Delta into imperial Chinese administrations, the coastal and island regions of modern-day Hong Kong (香港) supported Neolithic communities. Archaeological excavations across various coastal sites have yielded artifacts that point to a sophisticated maritime and littoral adaptation. While organic materials rarely survive the acidic soils of southern China, durable stone implements recovered from these strata provide critical insights into the daily lives, subsistence strategies, and technologies of these ancient populations.

## What happened

Around **c. 3000 BCE**, archaeological evidence indicates the introduction and adoption of bark cloth manufacturing technology. The defining artifacts of this technological shift are grooved stone beaters (石拍). These specialized implements were used by early inhabitants to pound the inner bark of specific trees, breaking down the fibers to create a flexible, pliable material suitable for garments and other utilitarian uses. Sites such as Yung Long (榕朗) on Lamma Island (南丫島) and other coastal shell-midden locations have produced stone tools bearing characteristic grooved patterns, which experimental archaeology links directly to the production of bark cloth found across the broader Austronesian and southern Chinese coastal spheres.

## Significance

The adoption of bark cloth manufacturing represents a major milestone in the technological and cultural history of early Hong Kong. It demonstrates that prehistoric inhabitants possessed specialized craft knowledge well beyond basic stone-tool knapping, adapting their material culture to the subtropical environment. Furthermore, the presence of bark-cloth beaters offers potential technological links to wider prehistoric exchange networks and cultural connections spanning the South China Sea and Southeast Asia, reflecting a shared regional heritage long before written records began.
