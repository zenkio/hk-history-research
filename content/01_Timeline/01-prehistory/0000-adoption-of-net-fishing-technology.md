## Background
During the middle **Neolithic period**, the coastal regions of the Pearl River Delta began to see increased human activity. Archaeological evidence from sites across Hong Kong—most notably at **Sha Ha (沙下)** in Sai Kung and **Sham Wan (深灣)** on Lamma Island—indicates that early settlers relied on a combination of foraging, hunting, and primitive fishing. As sea levels stabilized after the post-glacial transgression, these communities developed specialized tools to exploit the rich marine resources of the South China Sea.

## What happened
Around **4000 BCE**, the archaeological record shows a marked increase in the presence of ceramic net sinkers. These artifacts, typically made from fired clay, were perforated to allow attachment to netting materials such as vegetable fibers. By attaching these weights to the bottom of nets, ancient fishers could sink their equipment deeper into the water column and maintain its position against tidal currents. This technological adaptation allowed for the capture of a greater variety and volume of fish compared to earlier spear-fishing or hand-collection methods.

## Significance
The widespread adoption of net fishing signifies a transition toward a more sedentary, resource-focused maritime lifestyle. The ability to catch fish in higher quantities suggests that early inhabitants may have possessed techniques for preserving their catch, such as sun-drying or salting, which would be necessary to survive periods of low productivity. These findings place Hong Kong within a broader network of Southeast Asian maritime cultures that prioritized coastal resource management. While these ceramic artifacts provide a clear technological baseline, researchers continue to debate the extent to which these early communities also engaged in incipient agriculture, as the archaeological record for that period is characterized by a heavy reliance on shellfish and marine fauna.

## Perspectives

- **Mainland Chinese Archaeology**: Mainland scholars often categorize these findings as part of the broader development of the prehistoric Lingnan (嶺南) culture. They argue that these technological advancements reflect the progressive evolution of southern Chinese populations within the context of the Neolithic transition.
- **Hong Kong Local Scholarship**: Local researchers focus on the specific adaptation to the island topography of the region. They emphasize the unique role that maritime mobility played in the development of prehistoric Hong Kong identity, distinct from inland continental patterns.
