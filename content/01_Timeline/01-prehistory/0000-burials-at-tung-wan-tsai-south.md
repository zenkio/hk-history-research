## Background

The coastal regions of southern China and the Pearl River Delta, including the islands of present-day Hong Kong, possess a rich archaeological record stretching back several millennia. During the late Neolithic period, around **c. 2000 BCE**, these coastal areas were inhabited by communities whose subsistence strategies relied heavily on maritime resources, fishing, and rudimentary agriculture. Archaeological sites throughout the territory, particularly on islands such as Lamma Island (南丫島), have yielded significant artifacts including stone tools, pottery, and burial remains that illuminate the daily lives and mortuary practices of these early peoples.

## What happened

The recovery of skeletal remains from this period is relatively rare in Hong Kong due to the acidic nature of the local soil, which tends to accelerate the decomposition of organic matter. The finds at Tung Wan Tsai South included human bones preserved alongside distinctive pottery styles and stone implements characteristic of the regional late Neolithic and early Bronze Age cultures of the Lingnan (嶺南) region.

## Significance

The discoveries at Tung Wan Tsai South provide invaluable physical and genetic evidence regarding the prehistoric populations of Hong Kong and their connections to broader populations in the Pearl River Delta and Southeast Asia. Osteological and potential ancient DNA analyses help researchers understand the biological affinities, health status, and migration patterns of early maritime communities in southern China. While written historical records for Hong Kong only emerge much later, sites like Tung Wan Tsai South allow historians and archaeologists to reconstruct the deep-time human history of the region before the integration of the area into the imperial Chinese state during the Qin dynasty.
