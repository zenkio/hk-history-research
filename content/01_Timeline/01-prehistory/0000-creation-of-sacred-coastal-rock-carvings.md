## Background

During the middle of the second millennium BCE, the coastal areas of present-day Hong Kong were inhabited by communities practicing a maritime-oriented lifestyle, associated by archaeologists with the broader Bronze Age cultures of the Lingnan region. While perishable materials have mostly vanished from the archaeological record, durable evidence of their presence and belief systems remains etched into the coastal stone.

## What happened

Around **c. 1300 BCE**, local inhabitants incised abstract geometric designs, spirals, and stylized animal forms onto exposed vertical rock faces near the high-water mark. Sites containing these features, such as those found on **Cheung Chau** (長洲) and **Po Toi Island** (蒲台島), required careful placement along the shoreline, accessible primarily by water. The creators used percussion and abrasion techniques on hard igneous rock, such as granite, to produce grooves that have survived thousands of years of weathering and tidal action.

## Significance

The rock carvings represent some of the earliest tangible evidence of human ritual expression in the territory. Because the carvings are situated near the sea, modern scholars generally interpret them as sites of maritime worship, possibly intended to appease sea deities, ensure safe voyages, or secure abundant fishing yields in a coastal environment. They provide critical insight into the symbolic world and technological capabilities of prehistoric populations in the Pearl River Delta before the area was incorporated into the centralized empires of the north.

## Perspectives

- **Archaeological and Academic Scholarship**: Modern archaeologists view the carvings as functional ritual sites created by Bronze Age coastal communities. They compare these motifs to similar discoveries across the Lingnan region to map prehistoric migration and cultural exchange.
- **Local New Territories Communities and Folklore**: Local oral traditions often attribute ancient physical anomalies and stone markings to legendary figures, dragons, or ancient pirates. Over generations, these coastal sites became integrated into local folk memory as places imbued with spiritual power.
