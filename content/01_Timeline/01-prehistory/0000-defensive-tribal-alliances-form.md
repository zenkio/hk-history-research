## Background
Prior to the incorporation of the Lingnan region into the centralized Chinese empire, the coastal and riverine areas of modern-day Hong Kong and Guangdong were inhabited by diverse communities collectively referred to in early Chinese texts as the **Baiyue** (百越) peoples. Archaeological evidence from sites across the territory indicates a complex network of maritime and agricultural communities practicing bronze and stone tool technologies. By **c. 250 BCE**, the northward expansion and military consolidation of the Warring States—particularly the state of Chu and later the Qin—began to destabilize regional trade networks and exert direct territorial pressure on the south.

## What happened
Facing external encroachment from the north, fragmented tribal groups and clan lineages in the Pearl River Delta are believed to have forged broader defensive alliances. While direct written records from the indigenous perspective are entirely absent, archaeological indicators such as standardized pottery styles, shared weapon typologies, and fortified hilltop settlements suggest increased regional cooperation. These coalitions likely aimed to secure vital trade routes, pool military resources, and coordinate resistance or negotiation strategies against the advancing northern forces. This period marks a critical transition from isolated clan-based societies toward larger, more cohesive regional confederations.

## Significance
These early alliances represent a crucial chapter in the pre-imperial history of Hong Kong, demonstrating that the region's inhabitants were active agents responding to macro-historical shifts rather than passive observers. The consolidation of the **Baiyue** tribes laid the groundwork for the more organized resistance later encountered by the armies of **Qin Shi Huang** (秦始皇) during their conquest of Lingnan. Understanding this era highlights the indigenous roots of the Pearl River Delta's population before the imposition of imperial administration and Han dynasty migration.

## Perspectives

- **Later Imperial and Nationalist Historiography**: Traditional Chinese historical frameworks often view the southward expansion of northern states and the subsequent pacification of the Baiyue through the lens of civilizing missions and the inevitable unification of diverse territories under a central Chinese hegemony.
- **Modern Regional and Indigenous Scholarship**: Contemporary local and archaeological perspectives emphasize the agency, advanced social organization, and maritime capabilities of the indigenous Baiyue tribes, framing these alliances as acts of self-defense against external imperialist aggression.
