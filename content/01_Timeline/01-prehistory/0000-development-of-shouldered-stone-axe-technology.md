## Background

Before the widespread adoption of specialized stone tools, early inhabitants of the Pearl River Delta region relied on simpler pebble tools and unshouldered axes. Archaeological excavations across various sites in the territory have yielded lithic artifacts that illustrate a gradual technological evolution during the Bronze Age and late Neolithic periods. While precise dating remains a subject of ongoing archaeological debate, stratigraphic evidence suggests that regional craft industries were becoming increasingly sophisticated.

## What happened

Around **c. 1500 BCE**, the production and use of shouldered stone axes (有肩石斧) became prevalent among local communities. This distinct tool design features a narrowed upper portion—the "shoulder"—which facilitated more secure hafting onto wooden handles. Archaeological findings from coastal and island sites reveal that these axes were meticulously shaped by chipping, pecking, and grinding local stones such as quartz and volcanic rock. The improved hafting method provided greater leverage and structural stability, reducing the likelihood that the stone head would dislodge during heavy impact.

## Significance

The technological shift toward shouldered axes had profound implications for daily life and material culture in the region. Improved woodworking efficiency enabled local communities to clear dense forests more effectively for agriculture and settlement expansion. Furthermore, the enhanced cutting power allowed builders to work with heavier timber, facilitating the construction of sturdier shelters and improved watercraft. This technological milestone reflects broader regional exchange networks and a shared technological horizon across coastal South China during the second millennium BCE.
