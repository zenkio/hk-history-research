## Background
During the mid-Neolithic period, roughly between **4500 and 4000 BCE**, the coastal regions of the Pearl River Delta underwent significant environmental changes as sea levels stabilized. The inhabitants of these regions, often associated with the **Sham Wan (深灣)** and **Tai Wan (大灣)** cultures, relied heavily on the abundant marine life provided by the surrounding waters. Archaeological excavations have uncovered dense concentrations of shell middens and stone tools, suggesting a lifestyle deeply integrated with maritime ecosystems.

## What happened
Evidence from sites across Lantau Island and Lamma Island reveals that these communities moved beyond mere subsistence foraging. Researchers have identified specialized activity areas designated for the processing of fish and shellfish. These sites typically feature large quantities of stone net sinkers, specialized scrapers, and ceramic vessels, which archaeologists interpret as tools for cleaning, drying, and salting aquatic products. By establishing these semi-permanent processing stations, early settlers were able to generate a surplus of preserved food, likely to mitigate seasonal fluctuations in resource availability.

## Significance
The development of these specialized sites signifies a transition toward more complex communal food management. The ability to preserve marine protein suggests the existence of rudimentary storage systems, which allowed these prehistoric groups to support larger or more stable populations. This technological evolution marks a critical chapter in the regional history, illustrating the ingenuity of early coastal communities in adapting to the unique environmental constraints and opportunities of the South China coast.

## Perspectives

- **Mainland Chinese Archaeological Tradition**: Academic perspectives originating from the mainland often contextualize these findings within the broader development of the Neolithic cultures of the Lingnan region, viewing them as integral to the evolution of southern Chinese sedentary society.
- **Colonial-Era British Archaeology**: Earlier colonial-era scholars frequently approached these sites through the lens of diffusionism, debating the extent to which these developments were indigenous innovations versus influences brought from northern Neolithic centers.
