## Background
Prior to **c. 800 BCE**, prehistoric communities inhabiting the coastal areas and islands of present-day Hong Kong primarily utilized stone tools, pottery, and occasionally simpler bronze ornaments or implements. These early inhabitants participated in coastal exchange networks spanning the Lingnan region (嶺南). Over centuries, metallurgical knowledge gradually diffused southward from the Central Plains and the middle and lower Yangtze River valleys, interacting with indigenous geometric pottery cultures.

## What happened
During the mid-to-late Bronze Age, approximately around **c. 800 BCE**, advanced metal artifacts began to appear in the regional archaeological record. Most notable among these finds are intricately cast bronze ge-halberds (戈) and sturdy socketed axes. Sites such as Lam Tsuen (林村) and other coastal locations in the New Territories have yielded fragments or complete specimens of these weapons. Unlike earlier decorative items, these objects required advanced casting techniques, precise mold-making, and controlled alloy proportions, indicating established metallurgical workshops either locally or through robust regional trade networks.

## Significance
The sudden appearance of advanced weaponry carries profound implications for the social structure of prehistoric communities in the Pearl River Delta. Historians and archaeologists generally suggest that these artifacts point toward growing social stratification, as the possession of rare, high-status metal weapons likely concentrated power in the hands of emerging elites or chieftains. Furthermore, the functional nature of ge-halberds—designed specifically for warfare rather than domestic labor—strongly implies an increase in localized tribal conflicts, competition over arable land, and the defense of key maritime trade routes.

## Perspectives

- **Mainland Chinese / Lingnan Archaeology**: Scholars on the mainland often view these developments as part of the broader integration and southern expansion of Bronze Age cultures influenced by the greater Chinese civilization, reflecting a shared regional trajectory within the Baiyue (百越) cultural sphere.
- **Colonial-Era & Early Western Scholarship**: Early British colonial antiquarians and mid-20th-century archaeologists tended to categorize these finds within a diffuse 'Southeast Asian' or maritime trade network model, treating the region as an isolated periphery influenced by external migrations and seafaring traders.
- **Local Hong Kong Historians**: Local researchers frequently emphasize the indigenous agency of coastal inhabitants, arguing that local communities actively adapted imported technologies to suit their maritime environment rather than simply absorbing external influences.
