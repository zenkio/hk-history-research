## Background

Archaeological excavations across the Pearl River Delta, including coastal and island sites within modern-day Hong Kong, have yielded human remains dating to the Neolithic period. These findings provide critical material evidence regarding the prehistoric inhabitants of southern China. Among the recovered artifacts and skeletal remains, dental modification practices have emerged as a distinct area of study for understanding the social structures of ancient coastal communities.

## What happened

Around **2000 BCE**, the deliberate extraction of specific teeth—most commonly the upper lateral incisors—became a widespread custom among youth in the region. This practice of ritual tooth ablation was performed not for therapeutic reasons, but as a significant physiological and social rite of passage. Anthropological interpretations suggest that the procedure marked the transition from childhood to adulthood, binding young individuals more closely to their specific clan or tribal network through shared bodily modification and endurance.

## Significance

As a physical and permanent alteration, tooth ablation served as a visual marker of tribal identity and maturity within prehistoric society. It demonstrates a complex cultural uniformity and communication network spanning the coastal regions of southern China and Southeast Asia during the Neolithic era. Furthermore, the presence of these dental practices offers contemporary researchers a tangible method for tracing migration patterns, social stratification, and cultural affinities among early populations before the advent of written records in the region.

## Perspectives

- **Archaeological and Anthropological Scholarship**: Modern scholars generally interpret ritual tooth ablation as a key indicator of regional cultural integration and social maturation processes among Neolithic coastal communities in southern China. Comparisons with contemporaneous sites across Southeast Asia suggest widespread shared traditions rather than isolated local developments.
- **Local New Territories Communities and Tradition**: While oral histories of indigenous New Territories families focus primarily on later imperial-era migrations and lineage establishment, the physical heritage recovered from local soil connects modern inhabitants to an ancient lineage of maritime and coastal adaptation that stretches back millennia.
