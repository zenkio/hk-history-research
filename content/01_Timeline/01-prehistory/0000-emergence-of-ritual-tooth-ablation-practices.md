## Background
During the middle to late Neolithic period, the coastal regions of Southern China, including the modern territory of **Hong Kong** (香港), were inhabited by maritime communities often associated with the **Geometric Pottery Culture** (幾何印紋陶文化). Archaeological excavations, particularly at sites like **Sha Ha** (沙下) in Sai Kung and **Yung Long** (涌浪) on Lantau Island, have uncovered skeletal remains that suggest a sophisticated understanding of bodily modification. These early inhabitants lived in small, semi-sedentary groups that relied heavily on marine resources and early forms of primitive agriculture.

## What happened
Around **1000 BCE**, archaeological evidence indicates the emergence of **ritual tooth ablation**, a practice where specific teeth—typically the lateral incisors—were intentionally extracted from healthy young adults. Unlike modern dental procedures performed for clinical necessity, these removals were symmetric and occurred during a specific phase of physical development, often during late adolescence. Physical anthropologists have observed that the healing of the bone tissue in these specimens suggests the procedure was conducted with care, indicating it was a standardized rite of passage rather than a haphazard injury.

## Significance
Ritual tooth ablation serves as vital biological evidence of a shared social identity among early coastal communities. Scholars suggest that the uniformity of the practice across different geographical sites in the Pearl River Delta points to a cohesive cultural network that facilitated exchange and common ritual frameworks. The act of modification likely served as a visual marker of status, adulthood, or tribal affiliation within these early societies, helping to maintain group cohesion. While the exact theological or social motivations remain obscured by time, the intentionality of the practice highlights a complex societal structure that extended beyond mere subsistence, reflecting deeper metaphysical concerns or formalized stages of community participation.

## Perspectives

- **Archaeological Scholarship**: Contemporary researchers primarily view tooth ablation as a key diagnostic trait for defining the cultural boundaries and migrations of Neolithic peoples in Southern China. This perspective emphasizes biological and archaeological data to map the spread of Austronesian or Baiyue (百越) ancestral groups.
- **Local New Territories Traditions**: Some local oral traditions and ethnographic interpretations view these ancient skeletal markers as part of a deep, indigenous history that precedes the arrival of later dynastic influences. This view emphasizes the distinctiveness of the coastal culture, framing it as an independent evolutionary branch of early civilization.
