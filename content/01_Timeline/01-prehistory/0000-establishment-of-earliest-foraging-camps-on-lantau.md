## Background
During the early Holocene epoch, approximately **6000 BCE**, the coastal landscape of what is now Hong Kong underwent significant changes due to rising post-glacial sea levels. The previously connected landmasses of the Pearl River Delta began to submerge, creating the rugged, island-studded geography recognized today. Archaeological evidence from sites on **Lantau Island (大嶼山)**, particularly along the coastlines, suggests that these environmental shifts forced nomadic hunter-gatherer groups to adapt their subsistence strategies to a maritime-oriented ecosystem.

## What happened
Excavations have uncovered lithic assemblages—predominantly stone tools and quartz flakes—consistent with seasonal foraging camps. These sites were likely occupied temporarily by small, mobile bands who exploited the rich marine resources of the South China Sea. While these camps indicate a clear human presence, the lack of permanent structures or burial remains from this specific period makes it difficult to definitively identify these groups' long-term migration patterns or their precise linguistic or ethnic origins. It is widely accepted among archaeologists that these groups relied on a mix of foraging for wild tubers, shellfish harvesting, and rudimentary fishing.

## Significance
The identification of these camps is pivotal for understanding the earliest human adaptation in the South China coastal region. These sites serve as the foundational evidence for the "pre-neolithic" presence in Hong Kong. The transition from terrestrial to maritime subsistence during this period is a critical focus for scholarship, as it mirrors broader trends seen across the East Asian coast. The persistence of these foraging patterns suggests a long-standing cultural adaptation to the littoral environment that would eventually evolve into the more sedentary coastal societies identified in later Neolithic archaeological horizons.

## Perspectives

- **Archaeological Scholarship**: Mainstream archaeological consensus posits that these camps represent highly mobile, low-density populations responding to climate-driven environmental changes. Some scholars hypothesize that these groups migrated from inland northern areas as sea levels stabilized, though this remains a point of academic debate.
- **Local New Territories Community Identity**: For some local communities, the presence of these early sites reinforces the historical continuity of the region as a distinct coastal entity separate from the northern agrarian heartland. This perspective often emphasizes the unique "maritime culture" (海洋文化) that has defined the region's inhabitants for millennia.
