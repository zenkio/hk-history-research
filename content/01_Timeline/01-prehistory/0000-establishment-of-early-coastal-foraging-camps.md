## Background
During the early Holocene epoch, approximately **6000 to 5000 BCE**, the rise in global sea levels transformed the low-lying valleys of the Pearl River Delta into the intricate coastline now characterizing Hong Kong. Archaeological evidence suggests that early human inhabitants were drawn to the region’s diverse marine ecosystems. These populations, often categorized by archaeologists under the 'Sham Wan culture' or related Neolithic precursors, operated as mobile foragers rather than sedentary agriculturalists.

## What happened
Archaeological surveys have uncovered evidence of shell middens—accumulations of mollusk shells discarded by humans—and stone tool workshops near protected bays, such as those found at **Sham Wan (深灣)** on Lamma Island. These sites served as seasonal camps. While the exact duration of these occupations remains uncertain, the presence of polished stone axes and chipped pebble tools indicates a sophisticated adaptation to the littoral environment. These groups likely utilized the abundant estuaries and rocky outcrops to gather shellfish, fish, and coastal flora. The transition from transient occupation to more permanent settlement remains a subject of ongoing debate in regional archaeology, as evidence for continuous habitation before the mid-Holocene is fragmented.

## Significance
The establishment of these foraging camps marks the beginning of intensive human exploitation of Hong Kong's marine biodiversity. By utilizing the sheltered bays and natural harbours, these early settlers laid the foundation for the maritime orientation that would define the region's history for millennia. These sites provide a critical window into the survival strategies of coastal communities that thrived long before the integration of the region into the administrative structures of the mainland dynasties. Understanding these early camps is essential for reconstructing the movement of prehistoric populations along the South China coast.

## Perspectives

- **Archaeological & Academic Community**: Most researchers emphasize the scientific evidence of shell middens as proof of high-mobility foraging strategies linked to the broader Pearl River Delta maritime culture. They maintain that the cultural development in Hong Kong followed a gradual evolution driven by environmental adaptation rather than sudden external influence.
- **Nationalist Narratives (Historical Context)**: Some historical interpretations from the mainland emphasize these early settlements as part of a continuous, indigenous development within the broader context of Chinese civilization. This view seeks to place Hong Kong’s prehistoric inhabitants within the early evolutionary arc of the South China region's ancestral populations.
