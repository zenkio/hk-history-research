## Background
During the **Neolithic period**, specifically between **-3000 and -2000**, the coastal regions of the Pearl River Delta (珠江三角洲) were inhabited by Neolithic communities characterized by their reliance on the sea. Archaeological evidence, primarily excavations of stone tools and pottery shards found across various islands, indicates that these early inhabitants possessed advanced maritime navigation capabilities. These communities are often categorized under the broader **Geomantic Stone Culture** or related coastal Neolithic traditions.

## What happened
The discovery of stone adzes, rings, and quartz ornaments in diverse island locations—such as **Sham Wan (深灣)** on Lamma Island—points to a distribution pattern that cannot be explained by local resources alone. Geochemical analysis of stone tool materials indicates that the lithic sources were often non-local, originating from the mainland. This suggests that organized, regional trade networks were established between **-3000 and -2000**, facilitating the exchange of raw materials and finished goods between the islands and the mainland. These networks allowed for the sharing of technological innovations in tool production and, likely, cultural practices.

## Significance
The existence of these trade networks challenges earlier colonial-era depictions of Hong Kong as a 'barren rock' prior to the British arrival. Instead, these networks demonstrate that the region served as a hub of human activity and economic interaction for millennia. Whether these interactions were sporadic or part of a formalized, long-term trade structure remains a subject of ongoing archaeological investigation. These early maritime activities laid the foundation for the region's long history as a coastal gateway, connecting the Pearl River Delta to broader regional influences.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Scholarship**: Early British historians often emphasized the lack of significant central governance or urban development in the region before the 19th century. They tended to characterize pre-colonial Hong Kong as a collection of isolated fishing villages rather than part of an integrated, sophisticated regional economic system.
- **Later Archaeological and Academic Scholarship**: Contemporary researchers focus on the material evidence of trade and cultural exchange, arguing that Hong Kong was never truly isolated. They emphasize that the region functioned as an integral part of a complex, interconnected coastal network that thrived long before the establishment of imperial administrative centers.
