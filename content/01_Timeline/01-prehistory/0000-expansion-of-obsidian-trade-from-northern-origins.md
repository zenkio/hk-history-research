## Background
During the mid-to-late second millennium BCE, the coastal regions of the Lingnan area were inhabited by maritime-oriented Neolithic societies. Archaeological excavations, particularly at sites like **Sha Ha (沙下)** in **Sai Kung (西貢)**, have revealed a complex material culture characterized by geometric stamped pottery and sophisticated stone tool production. While local stone resources were abundant, the discovery of materials not native to the immediate environment provides evidence of external contact.

## What happened
Between approximately **1500 BCE and 1200 BCE**, prehistoric inhabitants of the Hong Kong region began utilizing obsidian, a volcanic glass highly valued for its ability to hold a razor-sharp edge. Geological analysis indicates that the chemical composition of this obsidian does not match local volcanic sources, which are primarily acidic or basaltic in ways that do not produce high-quality, translucent obsidian. Current scholarship suggests this material originated from volcanic sources much further north, potentially reaching the Pearl River Delta via complex maritime or coastal riverine trade routes. This suggests that the early settlers were not isolated, but rather nodes within a broader network of exchange that facilitated the movement of prestige goods and raw materials across significant distances.

## Significance
The presence of this non-indigenous material serves as a key indicator of the reach of early long-distance exchange routes. The movement of obsidian challenges older models of prehistoric Hong Kong as an isolated backwater. Instead, it positions the region as an active participant in a regional economic system that linked coastal southern China with northern volcanic regions. Whether this trade was driven by systematic barter, gift exchange between coastal tribes, or the migration of small groups carrying their own materials remains a subject of ongoing archaeological debate. The obsidian serves as a material bridge, proving that even in the second millennium BCE, the inhabitants of the Hong Kong territory were integrated into the wider currents of prehistoric East Asian development.

## Perspectives

- **Later scholarship and archaeological consensus**: Most modern researchers interpret the obsidian findings as evidence of a highly mobile, seafaring society that actively participated in regional trade. They emphasize that the material presence constitutes a 'prehistoric signature' of long-distance economic connectivity that transcended local boundaries.
- **Local and New Territories community perspectives**: Some local community voices emphasize the significance of these finds as proof of deep-rooted, long-standing indigenous cultural history in Hong Kong. They often frame this as evidence of an autonomous maritime identity that existed long before the influence of northern administrative states.
