## Background
Long before the imperial unification of China, the coastal regions surrounding the Pearl River Delta (珠江三角洲) supported indigenous communities with maritime-oriented economies. Archaeological findings across various islands and coastal areas in Hong Kong indicate that local inhabitants engaged in fishing, gathering, and basic agriculture, establishing the foundation for broader regional contact.

## What happened
Around **500 BCE**, archaeological evidence suggests an intensification of exchange networks across the Pearl River Delta. Coastal settlements in present-day Hong Kong participated in the circulation of distinctive hard geometric pottery, polished stone tools, and ornaments fashioned from materials such as quartz and jade. These goods moved along maritime and riverine routes, connecting local communities with wider populations in present-day Guangdong and beyond. Salt, dried marine products, and locally manufactured items likely formed the basis of these transactions.

## Significance
The expansion of trade around **500 BCE** demonstrates that early Hong Kong was not an isolated frontier, but an active participant in coastal exchange systems. This period reflects the development of specialized craft production, such as advanced pottery firing and stone carving, and highlights the maritime orientation of early regional societies before the formal southward expansion of the Qin empire.

## Perspectives

- **Later Scholarship**: Modern archaeologists and historians view this era through the material culture of the Bronze Age transition, emphasizing regional trade networks that connected coastal Baiyue (百越) populations independently of central dynastic control.
- **Local New Territories Communities**: Local oral traditions and community histories often view the ancient inhabitants of the coastal areas as ancestral maritime pioneers whose survival skills and navigational knowledge laid the groundwork for later generations of fishermen and salt-producers.
