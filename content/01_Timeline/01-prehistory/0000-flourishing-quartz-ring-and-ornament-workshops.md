## Background

Before the formal incorporation of the region into imperial Chinese administration during the Qin dynasty, the coastal areas of present-day Hong Kong and the Pearl River Delta supported indigenous societies with advanced lithic technologies. Archaeological excavations across several coastal and island sites, most notably **Sham Wan** (深灣) on **Lamma Island** (南丫島), have unearthed significant material culture dating to the second millennium BCE. These discoveries indicate that prehistoric inhabitants possessed sophisticated skills in working with hard stones, particularly quartz.

## What happened

Around **c. 1200 BCE**, archaeological evidence suggests a marked increase in localized craft specialization. Artisans established dedicated workshops focused on fashioning highly prized quartz rings, **slit-discs** (zhuer, 玦), and other personal adornments. The manufacturing process required advanced techniques involving sawing, drilling, and abrasive polishing to shape quartz—a mineral with a high degree of hardness on the Mohs scale. Raw materials appear to have been sourced locally or traded within regional coastal networks, and the finished items were distributed throughout the Pearl River Delta, pointing to active exchange systems.

## Significance

The flourishing of quartz ring workshops challenges older historiographical models that viewed prehistoric coastal South China as culturally isolated or purely peripheral to the Central Plains civilizations of ancient China. Instead, the evidence highlights a distinct regional maritime culture characterized by high levels of technical expertise and specialized craft production. These artifacts provide crucial insights into the social stratification, aesthetic values, and trade networks of early societies inhabiting the South China coast long before written historical records documented the region.

## Perspectives

- **Later Archaeological Scholarship**: Modern archaeologists and academic researchers emphasize that these workshops demonstrate indigenous technological sophistication and complex regional trade networks independent of northern Chinese dynastic influence. They view the standardized production of quartz ornaments as evidence of early social stratification and specialized labor division within coastal communities.
- **Traditional Historiography**: Traditional Chinese dynastic accounts largely overlook pre-Imperial southern coastal societies, categorizing the indigenous populations broadly as the **Baiyue** (百越) peoples without detailing specific local manufacturing traditions. Consequently, official imperial histories focus primarily on later southward expansions rather than the internal economic and technological developments of these early indigenous workshops.
