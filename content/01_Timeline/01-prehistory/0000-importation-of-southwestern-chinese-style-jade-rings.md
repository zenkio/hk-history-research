## Background
During the middle to late **Warring States period (戰國時期)**, the territory comprising modern-day Hong Kong and the wider Pearl River Delta was inhabited by indigenous communities often referred to in ancient texts as the **Baiyue (百越)** peoples. Traditionally viewed by early colonial-era antiquarians as isolated and culturally stagnant, modern archaeological excavations have fundamentally revised this perspective, revealing sophisticated maritime and riverine trading networks that linked the south China coast with inland political centers.

## What happened
Around **c. 400 BCE**, distinctive jade artifacts—specifically slit rings known as **jue (玦)** crafted in styles characteristic of southwestern China, notably the **Sichuan (四川)** region—began appearing in local archaeological contexts alongside indigenous geometric-stamped pottery and local stone tools. These ornaments, manufactured hundreds of kilometers away from the Lingnan region, point to long-distance acquisition rather than local emulation. The presence of these specialized prestige goods indicates that local elites or community leaders were actively participating in inter-regional exchange systems, trading coastal or forest products for high-status luxury goods originating from the upper Yangtze River valley.

## Significance
The importation of southwestern-style jade rings challenges simplistic historical narratives of early Hong Kong as a peripheral backwater untouched by broader historical currents. Instead, it highlights the existence of complex trade routes that bypassed central dynastic control, connecting the South China Sea coastline directly with inland southwestern polities via river systems and coastal navigation. These artifacts serve as tangible evidence of cultural diffusion and economic interdependence well before the formal incorporation of the region into the imperial Chinese state under the **Qin dynasty (秦朝)** in the late 3rd century BCE.

## Perspectives

- **Later Archaeological Scholarship**: Modern archaeologists emphasize that the distribution of Sichuan-style jades along the coast demonstrates active indigenous agency in regional trade, proving that southern coastal societies were nodal points in vast pre-imperial exchange networks rather than passive recipients of northern culture.
- **Early British Colonial Antiquarianism**: Early British researchers frequently attributed advanced material culture found in the New Territories to direct migration or military incursions from the Central Plains, often underestimating the complexity of indigenous Baiyue trade and societal structures.
