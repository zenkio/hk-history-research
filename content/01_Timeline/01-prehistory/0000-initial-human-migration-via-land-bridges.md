## Background
During the **Late Pleistocene** (approximately **35,000 to 10,000 BCE**), the Earth experienced cycles of significant cooling. These glacial periods caused global sea levels to drop drastically, often by more than 100 meters. The area that now encompasses the South China Sea (南海) was not the vast expanse of water seen today, but rather a wide, low-lying coastal plain intersected by river systems.

## What happened
As sea levels retreated, the current seabed of Hong Kong and the Pearl River Delta (珠江三角洲) were exposed as terrestrial land. It is widely accepted by archaeologists that these exposed land bridges served as corridors for early hominid and human migration. While definitive evidence of permanent settlements from this specific period remains sparse within the borders of modern Hong Kong, stone tools and lithic assemblages found in various parts of the region and neighboring Guangdong (廣東) indicate that hunter-gatherer populations were active in the wider coastal area. These groups likely followed migrating game and seasonal vegetation across the emerging landmass. As the climate warmed and the **Last Glacial Maximum** ended, sea levels rose rapidly, eventually inundating these bridges and isolating the higher elevations that would later become the hills and islands of Hong Kong.

## Significance
This period establishes that the region was accessible to human occupation long before it became a coastal maritime environment. The transformation of this geography explains the distribution of later Neolithic sites found along the current coastline, as the sea eventually stabilized near its present level. Understanding this migratory phase is essential for interpreting the subsequent development of coastal cultures, such as the **Sham Wan (深灣)** and **Sai Wan (西灣)** cultural phases, which relied on the established geography of the delta. However, the precise nature of these early interactions remains a subject of ongoing geological and archaeological investigation, as much of the potential evidence lies submerged beneath the modern harbor and seabed.

## Perspectives

- **Mainland Chinese archaeological consensus**: Academic institutions in Beijing and Guangdong emphasize the connectivity of the Hong Kong region to the broader Lingnan (嶺南) prehistoric cultural complex. They view this early migration as evidence of an unbroken historical link between the Pearl River Delta and the mainland's interior development.
- **Colonial-era British scholarship**: Earlier British archaeologists often viewed the region as a peripheral frontier to the major civilizations of the Yellow River. They typically characterized the prehistoric occupants as highly mobile, isolated maritime tribes rather than part of an integrated terrestrial society.
