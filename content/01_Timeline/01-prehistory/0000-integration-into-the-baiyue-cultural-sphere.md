## Background
Long before the northward expansion of the Central Plains dynasties, the coastal regions of modern-day Guangdong and Hong Kong were inhabited by diverse indigenous groups collectively known to early Chinese chroniclers as the **Baiyue** (百越) peoples. Archaeological evidence suggests that by the late Neolithic and early Bronze Age, these communities had developed sophisticated maritime adaptations, relying heavily on fishing, coastal trade, and boat-building.

## What happened
Around **c. 1000 BCE**, during the late Bronze Age, the region saw a profound convergence in material culture with the broader Baiyue sphere across the Pearl River Delta. Archaeological excavations throughout the territory—most notably at **Chek Lap Kok** (赤鱲角) and **Lamma Island** (南丫島)—reveal the widespread production and use of geometric hard pottery (印紋硬陶) and the casting of bronze implements, particularly distinctive notched bronze axes and weapons. These artifacts demonstrate that local communities were not isolated settlements, but active participants in a shared coastal network that stretched along the southeastern seaboard of China.

## Significance
This integration laid the foundational cultural and economic identity of the region long before its formal political incorporation into the Chinese empire under the Qin dynasty. The shared maritime technology and stylistic motifs found across the Pearl River Delta highlight a distinct regional trajectory centered on coastal and riverine connectivity rather than inland agrarian expansion.

## Perspectives

- **Later Imperial and Nationalist Historiography**: Traditional Chinese historiography often viewed the Baiyue peoples through the lens of gradual assimilation, framing the spread of material culture as an early stage of civilizing influences radiating southward from the Central Plains.
- **Modern Archaeological and Local Scholarship**: Contemporary archaeologists emphasize the indigenous agency and maritime sophistication of the coastal Baiyue, arguing that their culture developed organically through maritime trade and adaptation rather than passive reception of northern innovations.
