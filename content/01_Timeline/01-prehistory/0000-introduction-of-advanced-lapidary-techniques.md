## Background
The Neolithic period in the Pearl River Delta saw a gradual transition from basic tool production to more refined material culture. While earlier sites suggest a reliance on local stone for functional tools, the emergence of **advanced lapidary techniques** around **2500 BCE** indicates a change in technological sophistication. These developments are most prominently observed in the archaeological layers corresponding to the late Neolithic period in Hong Kong.

## What happened
Archaeological excavations, particularly at sites such as **Sha Ha (沙下)** and **Sham Wan (深灣)**, have unearthed stone ornaments, most notably perforated beads and rings, crafted from quartz, agate, and other hard stones. The production of these items required advanced grinding, drilling, and polishing techniques. Unlike simple flaked stone tools, these lapidary objects required significant labor hours and a high level of specialized skill to bore precise, narrow holes through dense material. This suggests that the early inhabitants of the region had mastered the use of abrasive sands and primitive bow-drills to achieve high-quality finishes.

## Significance
The widespread appearance of these beads and ornaments is interpreted by many scholars as evidence of early **craft specialization**. Because the production of such items was time-intensive, it implies that certain individuals within these communities were freed from the immediate necessity of foraging or fishing, supported instead by a social structure that valued luxury or ritual goods. Furthermore, the presence of these items in specific burial contexts—or conversely, their absence in others—is often cited as an indicator of emerging **social hierarchies**. While these early societies were likely small-scale, the ability to control and display prestige goods reflects the nascent organization of power and status among the prehistoric coastal populations of South China.

## Perspectives

- **Archaeological Scholarship**: Most modern archaeologists interpret these artifacts as clear markers of Neolithic technological evolution. There is ongoing debate, however, regarding whether these skills were indigenous developments or introduced through migration and trade networks connecting the region to inland areas.
- **Regional Cultural History**: Local researchers in the New Territories often emphasize these findings as proof of a sophisticated and independent maritime culture. This view challenges older, colonial-era narratives that previously characterized prehistoric Hong Kong as an isolated or culturally stagnant periphery.
