## Background

Before the widespread adoption of pottery, prehistoric inhabitants of the Lingnan region relied primarily on stone tools, bone implements, and natural containers for survival. Archaeological excavations across various sites in modern-day Hong Kong, particularly in the Sai Kung (西貢) and Lantau Island (大嶼山) areas, have yielded ancient lithic artifacts. However, the transition toward ceramic technology represents a fundamental change in how early human societies processed and stored resources in this coastal environment.

## What happened

Around **c. 35000 BCE**, early artisans in the region began manufacturing **coarse cord-marked pottery** (粗繩紋陶). This technique involved wrapping cords or cordage around a paddle and impressing the pattern onto the wet clay before firing. This process not only decorated the vessel but also strengthened the clay body and provided a non-slip gripping surface. The appearance of these early ceramic wares indicates a localized mastery of pyrotechnology, allowing communities to reliably cook food and store surplus provisions.

## Significance

The introduction of cord-marked pottery is widely viewed by archaeologists as a major milestone in the prehistory of southern China. It reflects greater sedentism and improved subsistence strategies among hunter-gatherer and early fishing communities in the Pearl River Delta. While organic remains from this remote period are rarely preserved, the durable pottery shards left behind provide crucial material evidence of technological innovation long before the written historical record began.

## Perspectives

- **Archaeological and Scientific Perspective**: Modern archaeologists rely on stratigraphic excavation, typology, and thermoluminescence dating to place these early ceramic fragments into a broader East Asian prehistoric timeline, though precise chronology remains subject to ongoing debate.
- **Local Heritage Perspective**: Local Hong Kong communities and educators view these early artifacts as tangible proof of tens of thousands of years of human habitation and resilience in the territory, predating major imperial Chinese dynasties by millennia.
