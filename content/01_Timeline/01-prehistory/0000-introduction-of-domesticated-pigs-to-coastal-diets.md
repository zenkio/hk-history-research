## Background
During the middle Neolithic period, around **c. 3500 BCE**, the coastal regions of South China and the area now known as Hong Kong were inhabited by early sedentary and semi-sedentary communities. Archaeological findings across various coastal sites suggest that these early inhabitants relied heavily on marine resources, gathering, and hunting. The introduction of domesticated livestock represented a major technological and ecological development for these communities.

## What happened
Excavations at regional prehistoric sites have yielded animal remains that include domesticated pigs (**家豬**). Unlike wild boars hunted in the local forested hills, these domesticated specimens show morphological changes associated with human breeding and controlled feeding. This practice supplemented the traditional marine-based diet—which consisted of fish, shellfish, and sea mammals—with a reliable, land-based source of protein. Animal husbandry required new forms of community coordination, waste management, and possibly the fencing or penning of livestock near residential areas.

## Significance
The integration of domesticated pigs into local diets diversified prehistoric subsistence economies and reduced reliance solely on foraging and hunting. It illustrates the connectivity of the Pearl River Delta region with broader agricultural and pastoral developments spreading through Neolithic China. Furthermore, it demonstrates how early coastal dwellers adapted mainland practices to their maritime-oriented environment, laying foundational patterns for animal husbandry in the region.

## Perspectives

- **Modern Archaeological Scholarship**: Contemporary archaeologists view the appearance of domesticated pigs as clear evidence of regional exchange networks and gradual shifts from pure foraging to mixed subsistence economies in coastal South China.
- **Local New Territories Communities**: Local oral traditions and indigenous histories do not explicitly reach back to the Neolithic era, but contemporary rural communities maintain a deep-rooted cultural tradition of pig farming and ritual sacrifice that reflects centuries of continuity with early agricultural practices in the Pearl River Delta.
