## Background
Prior to the introduction of iron, prehistoric communities in the Pearl River Delta relied primarily on stone, jade, and later bronze implements for daily life, fishing, and rudimentary farming. Archaeological findings across the region indicate that metallurgy advanced slowly through the Bronze Age before ironworking traditions spread south from the Central Plains of China during the Warring States period (戰國時期).

## What happened
Around **c. 400 BCE**, iron artifacts began to enter the archaeological record of coastal Guangdong and Hong Kong, though bronze and stone tools remained in simultaneous use for centuries. Excavated sites containing early metal fragments and implements suggest an incremental adoption rather than an abrupt technological revolution. Local inhabitants integrated iron into existing maritime and agricultural toolkits, improving the efficiency of woodworking, land clearance, and the manufacture of utilitarian objects.

## Significance
The transition to iron tools marked a critical technological turning point for the region, laying the material foundations for more intensive land use and larger, more permanent settlements. While the pace of adoption varied across different coastal and inland communities, the greater durability and abundance of iron ultimately displaced bronze as the dominant metal for practical applications, foreshadowing the deeper integration of the Lingnan region into the broader imperial Chinese economy.

## Perspectives

- **Modern Archaeological Scholarship**: Contemporary archaeologists view the appearance of iron around **c. 400 BCE** as part of a gradual diffusion of technology and trade networks from northern and central China rather than a sudden demographic replacement.
- **Local and Regional Historiography**: Local historical traditions and traditional Chinese historiography often emphasize the southward expansion of Central Plains culture and metallurgy as a civilizing process that unified the diverse Yue (越) tribes of the southern coast.
