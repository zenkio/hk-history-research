## Background
During the middle Neolithic period, the coastal inhabitants of the Pearl River Delta began to refine their resource extraction strategies. While earlier archaeological layers suggest a heavy reliance on mollusks and shallow-water harvesting, the archaeological record from approximately **3500 to 3000 BCE** indicates a transition in material culture. This era is generally associated with the **Middle Neolithic** phase, often linked to the broader **Dapenkeng (大坌坑)** culture, which spread across coastal South China.

## What happened
The critical development during this period was the widespread adoption of standardized **net sinkers (網墜)**. Crafted primarily from fired clay or shaped stone, these objects were attached to fishing nets to allow them to descend rapidly through the water column. Unlike earlier tools designed for foraging in tidal flats, these sinkers were robust enough to withstand the currents found in deeper, open-water environments. Excavations at sites like **Sham Wan (深灣)** on Lamma Island have yielded high concentrations of these artifacts, suggesting that fishing had become an intentional, organized activity rather than an opportunistic one.

## Significance
The introduction of net sinkers represents a technological leap that allowed early settlers to access pelagic—or open-sea—fish stocks. By moving beyond the littoral zone, these communities secured a more reliable and protein-rich food source, which in turn likely supported population growth and increased social complexity. This shift reflects a deepening mastery of the maritime environment, framing the sea not merely as a boundary, but as the primary foundation for local economic and social life. It marks one of the earliest instances where the inhabitants of the Hong Kong region demonstrated the seafaring orientation that would define the area’s human geography for millennia.

While the precise social organization behind this industry remains unknown, the ability to exploit open waters implies a level of communal cooperation, whether in the production of gear or the operation of watercraft. This development established a long-standing tradition of maritime subsistence that persisted until the formal administrative integration of the region under the Qin dynasty in **214 BCE**.

## Perspectives

- **Archaeological Community**: Mainstream archaeologists focus on the transition from a 'shell-midden' economy to one based on pelagic fishing as a key indicator of technological adaptation. They view the sinker as a proxy for the development of offshore watercraft capable of reaching deeper waters.
- **Cultural Historians**: Some scholars interested in regional heritage emphasize that these fishing practices form the ancestral roots of the 'Tanka' (蜑家) or boat-dwelling identities. They argue that these Neolithic technologies are the technological ancestors of the maritime-focused lifestyle that defined the New Territories until modern urbanization.
