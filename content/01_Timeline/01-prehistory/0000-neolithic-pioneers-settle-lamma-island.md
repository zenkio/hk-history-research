## Background
Long before the written history of imperial dynasties, the coastal bays and islands of the Pearl River Delta were shaped by changing sea levels and dense subtropical vegetation. Archaeological evidence indicates that by the mid-Holocene, coastal dwellers began to inhabit the region's sheltered beaches and headlands. While the precise origins of these early populations remain a subject of ongoing archaeological research, scholars generally agree that these groups formed part of a broader network of coastal Neolithic cultures spanning southern China.

## What happened
Around **4000 BCE**, early Neolithic pioneers established a settlement at **Sham Wan** (深灣) on **Lamma Island** (**Namma Chau**, 南丫島). Archaeological excavations at this site have uncovered distinct layers of human activity. The inhabitants utilized polished stone tools, cord-marked pottery, and stone ornaments, reflecting an economy deeply integrated with the coastal environment. Faunal and botanical remains suggest that these settlers relied heavily on marine exploitation—such as fishing and collecting shellfish—alongside hunting terrestrial animals and gathering wild plants.

## Significance
The settlement at Sham Wan provides crucial empirical data for understanding the pre-imperial history of the Hong Kong region. It demonstrates that the territory was part of the ancient coastal culture spheres of Lingnan long before its formal incorporation into the Chinese empire during the Qin dynasty. The site remains a foundational benchmark for archaeologists attempting to trace the chronology of prehistoric settlement patterns, technological developments, and human adaptation to changing sea levels along the southern Chinese coastline.

## Perspectives

- **Modern Archaeological Scholarship**: Archaeologists view Sham Wan as a key type-site for understanding the regional Neolithic sequence of the Pearl River Delta. They emphasize material culture comparisons, such as pottery styles and lithic technology, to link Hong Kong's prehistory with broader developments in Guangdong.
- **Local New Territories Communities**: Indigenous inhabitants and local historians often view these ancient archaeological finds as proof of deep-rooted, indigenous heritage tied to the land and seas of Hong Kong long before dynastic records began.
