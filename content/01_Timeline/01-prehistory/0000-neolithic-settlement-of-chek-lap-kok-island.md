## Background
Long before the integration of the Pearl River Delta into imperial Chinese administrations, the coastal and island geography of present-day Hong Kong supported prehistoric maritime communities. Archaeological investigations—heavily advanced during late 20th-century infrastructure projects such as the construction of the Hong Kong International Airport—revealed that islands like Chek Lap Kok (赤鱲角) were utilized by Neolithic hunter-gatherers and fisher-gatherers.

## What happened
Around **c. 4000 BCE**, prehistoric groups frequented Chek Lap Kok, likely utilizing it as a seasonal base for marine resource exploitation, shellfishing, and stone tool manufacturing. Excavations uncovered polished stone adzes, quartz artifacts, and corded ware pottery fragments. These material remains indicate that these early inhabitants possessed sophisticated stone-working techniques and engaged in seasonal mobility across the South China coast, adapting to changing Holocene sea levels and coastal ecologies.

## Significance
The archaeological finds on Chek Lap Kok are crucial for understanding the deep history of human adaptation in the South China region. They challenge simplistic narratives that the territory was devoid of significant human activity prior to imperial southward expansion. Instead, the site demonstrates that prehistoric populations actively integrated offshore islands into their subsistence networks millennia before written records began.

## Perspectives

- **Modern Archaeological and Academic Scholarship**: Modern scholars view the Chek Lap Kok excavations as vital evidence of a distinct maritime Neolithic culture in the Lingnan region, characterized by coastal adaptation, seasonal migration, and long-distance exchange networks spanning the Pearl River Delta.
- **Infrastructure Development and Heritage Management**: Rescue archaeology initiatives, accelerated by the massive land reclamation for the Chek Lap Kok airport, highlighted the tension between modern economic development and the preservation of submerged or endangered prehistoric heritage in Hong Kong.
