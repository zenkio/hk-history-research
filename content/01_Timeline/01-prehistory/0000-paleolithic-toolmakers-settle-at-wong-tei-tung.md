## Background
Long before the written history of imperial China or the arrival of British administrators, the coastal region of the Pearl River Estuary was inhabited by mobile hunter-gatherer communities. The physical geography of the area during the **Upper Paleolithic** period (around **38,000 BCE**) was notably different from today, featuring lower sea levels that exposed vast coastal plains extending into what is now the South China Sea.

## What happened
In **2003**, archaeologists discovered an important lithic production site at **Wong Tei Tung** (黃地峒), located on the **Sham Chung** (深涌) peninsula in Sai Kung. Excavations by local and mainland archaeologists yielded thousands of stone artifacts, including rough-hewn choppers, scrapers, and points. These tools were fashioned primarily from local materials such as quartz and volcanic rock using primitive flaking techniques. The presence of unfinished tools, hammerstones, and debitage indicates that Wong Tei Tung functioned as a workshop where humans actively manufactured stone implements rather than merely passing through.

## Significance
The discovery fundamentally pushed back the timeline of human presence in the territory by tens of thousands of years, establishing that Hong Kong's history of human habitation predates the Neolithic period by a significant margin. While no human skeletal remains or organic artifacts survived the acidic soil conditions of the region, the stone tools offer a tangible link to the Pleistocene inhabitants of the Lingnan region, illustrating early adaptation to coastal and estuarine environments.

## Perspectives

- **Modern Archaeological and Academic Consensus**: Contemporary archaeologists view the Wong Tei Tung site as a critical anchor for understanding prehistoric human migration and technological development in the Lingnan region. Scholars emphasize that the lithic assemblage reflects regional technological traditions shared with other Paleolithic sites in Guangdong province.
- **Local New Territories and Heritage Perspectives**: Local communities and regional historians highlight the site to emphasize the deep, ancient roots of human settlement in the rural areas of the New Territories, predating agrarian societies by millennia. This perspective values the preservation of such prehistoric landscapes against modern development pressures.
