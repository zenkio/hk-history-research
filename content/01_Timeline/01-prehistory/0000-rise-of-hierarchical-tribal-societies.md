## Background

Before the Qin dynasty's expansion into the Lingnan region, the territory comprising modern-day Hong Kong was inhabited by indigenous groups collectively referred to in ancient Chinese texts as the **Baiyue** (百越) peoples. Archaeological excavations across various sites in the region, particularly on islands and along coastal areas, reveal a long history of maritime adaptation, stone tool production, and early metallurgy during the Bronze Age. For many decades, early archaeological frameworks tended to view these prehistoric coastal communities as relatively simple, egalitarian kinship groups reliant on fishing, hunting, and shifting cultivation.

## What happened

Around **c. 350 BCE**, material culture begins to reflect significant shifts in social organization. Archaeologists analyzing burial sites from this period have noted a marked differentiation in the quality, quantity, and type of grave goods interred with individuals. While some burials contained simple pottery and modest stone implements, others featured sophisticated bronze weapons, refined geometric-patterned pottery, and valued ornaments. This unequal distribution of burial wealth is widely interpreted by modern archaeologists as a physical manifestation of social stratification, indicating the rise of hierarchical tribal societies or early chiefdoms where certain individuals or lineages held higher status, power, or wealth than others.

## Significance

This structural evolution marks a critical turning point in the pre-imperial history of the Pearl River Delta. The emergence of social hierarchy suggests increased regional trade, craft specialization, and the potential for centralized leadership in organizing communal labor or defense. These developments occurred just decades before the region was formally incorporated into the imperial administration under the Qin dynasty in **214 BCE**. Understanding this pre-Qin social complexity helps contextualize how local indigenous populations interacted with, and were subsequently integrated into, the expanding northern Chinese empires.

## Perspectives

- **Later Archaeological Scholarship**: Contemporary archaeologists emphasize material culture, utilizing grave goods and burial stratification to argue for complex social hierarchies and emerging chiefdoms prior to imperial conquest.
- **Traditional Historiography**: Historical texts from the imperial period often generalized southern coastal populations under the broad, homogenous category of the Baiyue, frequently overlooking internal social differentiation and local political structures.
