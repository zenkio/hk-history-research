## Background

By approximately **3000 BCE**, during the mid-Neolithic period, the coastal areas and islands of present-day Hong Kong witnessed significant cultural developments. Archaeological findings across various sites indicate that early human communities were transitioning from basic hunter-gatherer lifestyles to more specialized adaptations suited for coastal and marine environments. 

## What happened

During this era, inhabitants of the region refined their maritime capabilities. Archaeological excavations have revealed advanced polished stone tools, including stepped adzes, and distinct pottery styles such as fine white pottery and geometric-impressed wares. These artifacts suggest that local populations possessed the woodworking and technological skills necessary to construct seaworthy watercraft. Rather than relying solely on inland resources, these groups established seasonal coastal camps and settled near sheltered bays and estuaries. From these locations, they engaged in fishing, shell-collecting, and the hunting of marine fauna, effectively integrating the sea into their daily subsistence strategies.

## Significance

The emergence of this specialized coastal maritime culture laid the foundational economic and social patterns for prehistoric settlement in the Pearl River Delta region. It demonstrates an early human adaptation to marine ecosystems, showcasing sophisticated survival strategies that predated the arrival of major agrarian states from the north. The widespread distribution of similar pottery styles and manufacturing techniques across the region points to active maritime networks and cultural exchange among coastal communities during the Neolithic period.

## Perspectives

- **Later scholarship**: Modern archaeologists and anthropologists view the c. 3000 BCE development as part of a broader South China coastal Neolithic maritime adaptation, emphasizing indigenous technological evolution and regional seafaring networks rather than external domination.
- **Local Hong Kong and New Territories communities**: Local oral traditions and regional histories often connect the deep-rooted maritime identity of coastal inhabitants to an ancient heritage of seafaring, viewing these early prehistoric coastal dwellers as the distant precursors to traditional boat-dwelling and coastal communities.
