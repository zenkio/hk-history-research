## Background
During the Last Glacial Maximum, global temperatures were significantly lower, and vast amounts of water were locked in ice sheets. During this earlier epoch, the area now known as Hong Kong was not an island chain but rather an elevated inland region of coastal Guangdong, featuring rolling hills and extended river valleys that drained toward a distant coastline.

## What happened
Around **6000 BCE**, as the last ice age came to a close, rapid post-glacial warming drove a dramatic rise in global sea levels. The encroaching waters flooded the lower river valleys and coastal plains. This marine transgression isolated higher terrain features, turning ridge tops into islands and peninsulas, and creating the deeply indented coastline, natural harbors, and ria landscapes that define Hong Kong today.

## Significance
This geological transformation laid the foundational physical geography for all subsequent human settlement in the region. The newly formed bays, sheltered waters, and extended coastline provided rich marine resources and natural harbors that would eventually attract early maritime communities, fishermen, and traders thousands of years later.

## Perspectives

- **Modern Geological and Archaeological Scholarship**: Scientists and archaeologists view the mid-Holocene sea-level rise as the definitive physical event that created Hong Kong's unique insular geography, explaining why most early prehistoric sites are now found elevated above modern shorelines or buried under marine sediment.
- **Local Indigenous and Oral Traditions**: While geological timelines are modern scientific constructs, local New Territories communities and indigenous inhabitants have long preserved ancestral awareness of a changing landscape, where the sea and land share an ancient, dynamic relationship tied to maritime livelihoods.
