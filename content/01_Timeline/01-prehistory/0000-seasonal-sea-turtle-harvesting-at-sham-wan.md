## Background

By the middle of the first millennium BCE, coastal areas and islands of present-day Hong Kong supported maritime-oriented communities. Archaeological excavations, particularly on Lamma Island (南丫島), have revealed rich material cultures characterized by geometric pottery and stone tools. The geography of the region, featuring sheltered bays and sandy beaches, historically provided ideal nesting grounds for marine life, most notably the green sea turtle (**Chelonia mydas**).

## What happened

Around **c. 600 BCE**, inhabitants at sites such as Sham Wan (深灣) developed systematic methods for harvesting nesting green sea turtles. Archaeological findings suggest that these early coastal dwellers understood the predictable migratory and reproductive cycles of the reptiles. Rather than haphazard foraging, the seasonal appearance of turtles on the beaches was met with organized gathering strategies to supplement the local diet with protein and marine resources.

## Significance

This early exploitation demonstrates an advanced understanding of marine animal lifecycles among prehistoric populations in South China. It highlights the deep historical relationship between the inhabitants of the Pearl River Delta (珠江三角洲) and the marine environment, illustrating how early societies successfully integrated seasonal coastal resources into their subsistence patterns long before the advent of written historical records.
