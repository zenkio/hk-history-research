## Background
By the mid-second millennium BCE, the region now known as Hong Kong was inhabited by populations associated with the **Neolithic** 'Geometric Pottery' culture. These early communities primarily settled in sheltered bays, relying on near-shore fishing, shellfish harvesting, and rudimentary agriculture. Life was dictated by the geography of the Pearl River Delta, which provided protection from the open South China Sea.

## What happened
Around **1500 BCE**, archaeological evidence—specifically the distribution of stone adzes and fishing gear—suggests a technological shift. Settlers developed more robust vessel construction techniques, likely utilizing dugout canoes reinforced or expanded with planks. This engineering advancement allowed these maritime groups to move beyond the safety of sheltered bays and venture into deeper waters. The ability to navigate the open sea fundamentally altered their resource base, shifting the focus from intertidal collection to the systematic exploitation of pelagic fish species.

## Significance
This technological leap had profound social implications. Navigating the open sea required higher levels of coordination and skill, likely leading to more stratified social structures. It established the maritime identity that would define the region's inhabitants for millennia. While it is uncertain exactly how these groups organized their labor, the expansion of their territory suggests the development of wider trade networks across the Pearl River Delta. This era represents a departure from purely localized subsistence toward a more complex, mobile maritime economy.

## Perspectives

- **Archaeological Scholarship**: Modern archaeologists view this period as a critical technological transition evidenced by the presence of 'hard-geometric' pottery and stone tools found on offshore islands. They emphasize the environmental adaptation of these groups rather than attributing the change to external migration.
- **Traditional Historiography**: Older colonial-era narratives often categorized these populations as 'Baiyue' (百越) tribes, focusing on their eventual assimilation into broader Han administrative structures. In contrast, local New Territories communities often view these early maritime ancestors as the true indigenous inhabitants who defined the region's sea-bound culture before mainland agricultural influence arrived.
