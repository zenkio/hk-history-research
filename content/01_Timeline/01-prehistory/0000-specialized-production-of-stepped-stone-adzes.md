## Background

During the mid-Holocene period, around **c. 2500 BCE**, human settlement along the South China coast and the islands of present-day Hong Kong entered a more sophisticated technological phase. Archaeological excavations across sites such as **Sham Wan (深灣)** on Lamma Island and **Ma Wan (馬灣)** have yielded substantial evidence of prehistoric lithic industries. Before this period, local toolmaking relied on simpler pebble and flake stone implements. However, changing environmental conditions and expanding maritime networks necessitated more durable and specialized equipment for woodworking and settlement expansion.

## What happened

Craftsmen in the region began the standardized, specialized production of **stepped stone adzes** (有肩石斧 or 梯形石锛). These tools were distinguished by a unique stepped profile on the butt or blade, which allowed for more secure hafting onto wooden handles compared to earlier, simpler axes. Archaeological indicators suggest that production sites engaged in systematic sourcing of fine-grained volcanic and sedimentary rocks, employing techniques of sawing, grinding, and intense polishing. The precision required to shape these adzes points toward the emergence of specialized craftspeople rather than general domestic toolmaking.

## Significance

These stepped adzes were essential woodworking instruments, heavily utilized for felling timber, shaping planks, and constructing heavy-duty marine canoes. The availability of advanced watercraft significantly enhanced the mobility of prehistoric coastal populations, facilitating long-distance trade, resource gathering, and cultural exchange across the Pearl River Delta and beyond. Consequently, the specialized production of these adzes marks a critical technological milestone in the maritime adaptation of early Hong Kong inhabitants, laying the groundwork for complex regional societies prior to the expansion of the Qin dynasty.
