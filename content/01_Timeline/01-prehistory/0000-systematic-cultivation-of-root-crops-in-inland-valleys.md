## Background
During the middle to late **Neolithic period** in the Pearl River Delta, the inhabitants of the region are widely recognized for their reliance on the marine environment. Archaeological sites across Hong Kong, particularly those along the coast, have yielded large quantities of shells and fish bones, characterizing a society primarily driven by fishing and gathering. However, as populations stabilized, the need for a more consistent food supply became apparent.

## What happened
Archaeological investigations have uncovered starch grains and phytoliths in inland valleys, suggesting that settlers began the systematic cultivation of root crops, such as taro or yam, between **2000 BCE and 1500 BCE**. While it is uncertain whether this represented a full transition to agriculture, it signifies an intentional effort to manage local flora. These residues are often found in strata associated with ceramic fragments, implying that these early settlers were not merely nomadic foragers, but were beginning to establish semi-sedentary living patterns in the sheltered valleys away from the immediate coastline.

## Significance
This period marks a pivotal shift in the subsistence strategy of early Hong Kong inhabitants. The integration of cultivated crops alongside maritime resources allowed for a more varied diet and potentially supported larger, more stable communities. While the extent to which these early groups relied on cultivated crops versus wild gathered foods remains a subject of ongoing debate, the evidence of crop management suggests an advanced understanding of the local environment. This diversification of the food supply is considered a foundational step toward the more sedentary lifestyles that preceded the influence of broader regional administrative powers.

## Perspectives

- **Mainland Chinese Archaeological Perspective**: Mainland scholars often categorize this shift as part of a wider regional pattern of Neolithic development consistent with the southward expansion of agricultural practices from the Yangtze and Pearl River basins. They emphasize the technological continuity between Hong Kong's Neolithic cultures and those of the surrounding Guangdong provinces.
- **Colonial-Era and Later Western Scholarship**: Earlier British colonial archaeologists occasionally viewed these findings through the lens of external cultural diffusion, questioning whether such agricultural practices were indigenous inventions or influenced by migratory groups. Modern scholars tend to emphasize local environmental adaptation, moving away from purely diffusionist theories.
