## Background
Prior to **c. 2200 BCE**, human activity in the Pearl River Delta region was largely characterized by mobile or semi-mobile hunter-gatherer and fisher-forager lifestyles. Archaeological findings across various coastal sites indicate that early populations relied heavily on shellfish, marine resources, and seasonal movement. As environmental conditions stabilized following the Holocene climatic shifts, the region's geography—defined by indented coastlines, sheltered bays, and fertile alluvial valleys—offered new potentials for subsistence.

## What happened
Around **c. 2200 BCE**, during the late Neolithic period, archaeological evidence suggests a gradual shift toward more sedentary coastal villages. Communities began establishing longer-term habitations along sandy beach ridges and raised coastal terraces. While marine foraging and coastal fishing remained central to daily survival, material culture indicates the tentative adoption of early agricultural trials, alongside the production of increasingly sophisticated pottery, such as geometric impressed wares. Excavations have revealed postholes, stone tools suited for clearing and tilling, and specialized pottery types that point to localized permanence rather than temporary seasonal camps.

## Significance
This transition marked a foundational turning point in the prehistory of the region, laying the groundwork for more complex social structures and permanent human geography in modern-day **Hong Kong (香港)**. The move toward sedentary village life fostered closer ties to specific territories, local resource management, and regional trade networks connecting the Pearl River Delta with wider southeastern coastal cultures. It represents the crucial bridge between ancient foraging bands and the Bronze Age societies that subsequently emerged in South China.

## Perspectives

- **Later scholarship**: Modern archaeologists view this period as part of a broader, gradual neolithization process across coastal South China, driven by environmental stabilization and localized demographic growth rather than sudden migration. They emphasize material culture continuity, particularly pottery styles, linking local developments with contemporary cultures in the wider Pearl River Delta.
- **Local Hong Kong and New Territories communities**: Local indigenous histories and rural traditions generally focus on much later periods of lineage settlement, but oral traditions and heritage preservation efforts recognize the deep-rooted ancestral connection to the region's coastal and maritime environment over millennia.
