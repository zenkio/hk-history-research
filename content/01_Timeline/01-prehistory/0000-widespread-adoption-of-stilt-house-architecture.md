## Background

Long before the formal incorporation of the territory into imperial Chinese administration, the coastal areas and islands of the Pearl River Delta were inhabited by maritime communities. Archaeological evidence from various coastal sites suggests that early inhabitants relied heavily on fishing, gathering, and rudimentary agriculture. The environment was characterized by dense mangrove swamps, mudflats, and frequent tidal fluctuations, presenting significant challenges for permanent settlement and habitation.

## What happened

Around **c. 300 BCE**, archaeological indicators point to the widespread construction of **Ganlan-style stilt houses** (干欄式建築) built on wooden piles over water, shallow bays, and tidal mudflats. This architectural method involved driving sturdy timber posts deep into the seabed or mud, elevating the living platform well above the reach of high tides and seasonal flooding. These structures maximized airflow to cope with the humid sub-tropical climate, protected stored goods and grain from dampness and pests, and provided immediate, direct access to water transport for fishing boats.

## Significance

The adoption of stilt-house architecture represents a crucial milestone in human adaptation to the ecology of South China's coastline. By mastering pile-dwelling techniques, early inhabitants successfully turned hostile wetlands and shorelines into viable, long-term settlements. This architectural tradition persisted in various forms through the centuries, most notably influencing the traditional stilt houses seen in modern fishing villages such as **Tai O** (大澳) in the New Territories.

## Perspectives

- **Local Hong Kong and New Territories Communities**: Local indigenous villagers often view stilt-house traditions as a direct architectural lineage connecting contemporary coastal fishing culture, such as that in Tai O, with ancient maritime lifeways that predate urban industrialization.
- **Later Archaeological Scholarship**: Modern archaeologists analyze these settlements to understand the migration patterns of Baiyue (百越) peoples and their technological innovations in adapting to maritime and estuarine environments across the broader Lingnan region.
- **British Colonial Historiography**: Early British colonial accounts tended to categorize these indigenous coastal building methods as primitive vernacular architecture, often overlooking their sophisticated engineering adaptations to local tidal dynamics.
