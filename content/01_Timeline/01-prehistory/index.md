## Overview

The prehistory of Hong Kong is a narrative of human adaptation to a changing marine environment and the development of distinct coastal cultures. The earliest evidence of human presence in the region is found at **Wong Tei Tung** (黃地峒), where stone tool assemblages suggest **Paleolithic** toolmakers settled as early as the late Pleistocene or early Holocene, though the exact dating remains debated. Around **4000 BCE**, the dramatic stabilization of rising sea levels formed the intricate coastline, bays, and islands of modern Hong Kong, prompting a shift toward maritime subsistence strategies.

During the Middle Neolithic, **Neolithic pioneers** established settlements on **Lamma Island** (南丫島) and other coastal sites, marked by the introduction of coarse cord-marked pottery. By the Late Neolithic, a specialized coastal maritime culture flourished. The emergence of fine geometric pottery and the transition to sedentary coastal villages, such as those excavated at **Tung Wan Tsai South** (東灣仔南), revealed complex social patterns. Discoveries of human remains at **Tung Wan Tsai South** provide physical evidence of these early inhabitants, showing traits aligned with southern populations and giving clues to their diet and health.

The transition to the Bronze Age around **1500 BCE** introduced metallurgy, which coexisted with highly active quartz ring and ornament workshops at sites like **Sha Lo Wan** (沙螺灣) on Lantau Island (大嶼山). The inhabitants carved mysterious, abstract geometric patterns onto coastal cliffs, creating sacred rock carvings at **Shek Pik** (石壁), **Po Toi** (蒲台), and **Cheung Chau** (長洲). These communities were part of the broader **Baiyue** (百越) cultural sphere, characterized by stilt-house architecture (干欄式建築) and advanced maritime skills. By the first millennium BCE, the development of kiln-fired hard pottery and the introduction of advanced bronze weapons and iron tools signaled growing social complexity. This period saw the rise of hierarchical tribal societies, defensive tribal alliances, and expanded Pearl River Delta (珠江三角洲) trade networks, which lasted until the **Qin Dynasty** (秦朝) conquest of Lingnan (嶺南) in **214 BCE**.

## Key themes

* **Maritime Adaptation and Environment:** The physical geography of Hong Kong—shaped by post-glacial sea-level rises—dictated a lifestyle heavily reliant on the sea. Rather than relying solely on terrestrial farming, early populations combined fishing, shell-collecting, and hunting with coastal agriculture.
* **Technological Innovation and Trade:** The evolution from coarse earthenware to fine geometric pottery, and later to kiln-fired hard pottery and bronze metallurgy, demonstrates that prehistoric Hong Kong was not isolated. It acted as a key node in a regional network of technological exchange stretching across coastal south China and the Pearl River Delta.
* **Cultural Continuity and the Baiyue Sphere:** The archaeological record links Hong Kong's early inhabitants to the **Baiyue** peoples. Their material culture—such as quartz ornaments, stepped stone adzes, and stilt houses—shows a distinct cultural identity separate from the Central Plains (中原) cultures of northern China.

## How it shaped Hong Kong

The prehistoric era laid the foundational human and environmental geography of Hong Kong. The stabilization of the coastline created the natural harbors and sheltered bays that would later define Hong Kong as a global maritime hub. Furthermore, the early development of coastal trade routes and specialized maritime technology established a pattern of regional connectivity that persisted for millennia.

The tension between the region's indigenous maritime identity and the administrative centralization of northern dynasties began with the **Qin** conquest in **214 BCE**. This event marked the beginning of Hong Kong's incorporation into the Chinese imperial framework, yet the legacy of the pre-imperial maritime cultures remained a distinct, enduring thread in the region's historical character.

## Perspectives

- **Traditional Imperial Chinese Historiography**: Historically, dynastic records viewed the pre-Qin southern regions as 'barbarian' (蠻夷) wastes devoid of civilization until the civilizing influence of northern dynasties arrived. Under this framework, the prehistory of Hong Kong is treated as a primitive prelude that only achieved historical significance through its integration into the Chinese empire.
- **British Colonial Historiography**: Colonial-era accounts often minimized the significance of Hong Kong's pre-British past, portraying the area as a barren island populated only by a few scattered fishermen. This perspective framed the territory's history as beginning in earnest with British administration, downplaying the depth and complexity of its prehistoric maritime societies.
- **Modern Archaeological and Local Scholarship**: Modern scholars and local historians emphasize that prehistoric Hong Kong was part of a highly sophisticated, distinct, and interconnected Baiyue maritime culture. This view highlights the region's agency as an active trading hub and technological innovator long before the imposition of imperial administrative boundaries from the north.
