## Background

Long before the **Han dynasty** (漢朝) extended its administrative reach southward, coastal communities along the Pearl River Delta engaged in subsistence fishing and rudimentary salt extraction. Salt was a vital commodity for food preservation and physiological needs, making it a prime candidate for state control as imperial authorities sought to consolidate power and generate revenue in newly annexed southern territories.

## What happened

Around **c. 110 BCE**, during the reign of **Emperor Wu of Han** (漢武帝), the central government instituted state monopolies on the production and distribution of salt and iron. While direct administrative records detailing the exact mechanics of salt production in the immediate Hong Kong territory during this early period are sparse, archaeological evidence and later historical gazetteers indicate that coastal areas—specifically sheltered inlets like **Kowloon Bay** (九龍灣) and parts of **Tuen Mun** (屯門)—began to see organized salt-boiling pans (煮鹽場). These operations utilized the region's tidal flats and abundant fuelwood to evaporate seawater, integrating the local frontier economy into the broader imperial fiscal network.

## Significance

The emergence of the coastal salt industry marked a pivotal transition for the region from a peripheral frontier zone into an economically integrated component of the Chinese empire. It established patterns of maritime resource exploitation, state oversight, and commercial exchange that would persist and expand through subsequent dynasties, laying the socioeconomic foundations for later permanent settlements in the territory.

## Perspectives

- **Imperial Historiography**: Traditional dynastic histories emphasize the civilizing and unifying effect of the Han monopolies, viewing the regulation of salt as a necessary measure to curb regional warlordism and fund frontier administration.
- **Local and Archaeological Perspective**: Modern archaeological findings suggest that indigenous communities may have engaged in independent salt production long before formal imperial taxation, pointing to a more complex negotiation of economic autonomy at the empire's edge.
