## Background
Archaeological evidence dating approximately from **-1500 to -1000** indicates that the coastal regions of the Pearl River Delta, including modern-day Hong Kong, were inhabited by sedentary or semi-sedentary groups. These communities lived in proximity to the sea, relying on marine resources, which made them vulnerable to both environmental fluctuations and external pressures. The emergence of specialized hilltop settlements suggests a transition toward more stratified social organizations capable of managing communal labor.

## What happened
Between the mid-second and early first millennium BCE, settlers began constructing defensive perimeters, often utilizing earthen ramparts and strategic hilltop positioning. While the exact scale of these fortifications remains a subject of archaeological debate, excavation sites such as those found on **Lamma Island (南丫島)** and **Chek Lap Kok (赤鱲角)** reveal remnants of compacted earth mounds and cleared hilltop clearings. These features are interpreted by many scholars as defensive earthworks designed to protect living quarters and storage areas from encroachment. The construction of these sites required the coordination of labor beyond that of a single household, pointing toward the existence of a proto-polity or organized community structure.

## Significance
The creation of these defensive earthworks marks a pivotal shift in the regional archaeological record. It demonstrates that early inhabitants of the Hong Kong region were not merely transient coastal dwellers but established communities with the capacity to modify their landscape for security. These findings complicate earlier narratives that portrayed the region as a peripheral 'wasteland' prior to imperial contact, suggesting instead a sophisticated, localized development pattern. Whether these structures were intended for protection against maritime raids or served as symbols of territoriality remains a point of scholarly discussion, but their existence undeniably confirms that organized defense was a high priority for Bronze-Age societies in the Pearl River Delta.

## Perspectives

- **Colonial-era Archaeology**: Early 20th-century British surveys often characterized these sites as minor, transient camps, downplaying the level of political organization present in the region prior to Chinese imperial expansion.
- **Mainland and Local Scholarship**: Contemporary scholars from both Hong Kong and Mainland China increasingly view these fortifications as proof of an indigenous, sophisticated cultural layer that contributed to the later development of the Baiyue (百越) maritime cultures, emphasizing the region's historical agency.
