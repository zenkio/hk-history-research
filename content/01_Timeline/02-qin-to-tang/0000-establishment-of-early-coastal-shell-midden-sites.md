## Background
During the period spanning the late **Qin (秦朝)** dynasty and the early **Han (漢朝)** dynasty, the coastal region of modern-day Hong Kong was geographically isolated from the political centers of the North. Archaeological surveys have identified clusters of shell middens—deposits of discarded mollusk shells, fish bones, and stone tools—concentrated along the sandy beaches and embayments of the New Territories and outlying islands. These sites suggest that early inhabitants utilized a subsistence strategy highly adapted to the shallow, nutrient-rich estuarine environment of the Pearl River Delta.

## What happened
Between approximately **200 BCE and 100 BCE**, human activity in the region transitioned from transient foraging to more structured, recurring seasonal occupations. These shell midden sites provide evidence of a maritime culture capable of processing large quantities of marine life. Excavations have revealed **Geometrically patterned pottery (幾何印紋陶)**, which is widely considered a hallmark of the prehistoric cultures in Southern China. While the exact demographics of these groups remain a subject of academic debate, the proximity of these sites to high-tide zones suggests an intimate knowledge of tidal rhythms and local food chains. It is uncertain whether these settlements were inhabited year-round or functioned as seasonal processing stations for groups traversing the wider coastal corridor.

## Significance
The emergence of these sites represents a pivotal stage in the regional transition toward sedentary life. By establishing permanent or semi-permanent bases, early settlers were able to develop specialized tools for fishing and harvesting, such as perforated stone sinkers and adzes. This period lays the foundation for later maritime trade and resource management. The shell middens serve as the earliest concrete indicators of human agency in transforming the coastal landscape of Hong Kong into a site of sustained economic productivity long before the region’s formal incorporation into the imperial Chinese administrative structure.

## Perspectives

- **Mainland Chinese Archaeology**: Academic perspectives from the mainland often categorize these sites as a southern extension of the broader Baiyue (百越) cultural sphere. They emphasize the integration of these coastal communities into the technological trends prevalent across the Lingnan (嶺南) region.
- **Local Hong Kong Archaeological Scholarship**: Local researchers frequently highlight the distinctiveness of Hong Kong’s maritime adaptation compared to contemporary inland settlements. They suggest that the reliance on marine resources fostered a unique coastal identity that persisted independently of imperial political fluctuations.
