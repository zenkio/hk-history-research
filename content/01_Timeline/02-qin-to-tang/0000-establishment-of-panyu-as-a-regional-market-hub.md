## Background
Before the **Qin Dynasty (秦朝)** expansion into the south, the coastal region comprising modern-day Hong Kong and the Pearl River Delta was inhabited by the **Baiyue (百越)** peoples. These populations engaged in maritime trade and coastal subsistence, yet they operated outside the centralized political structures of the Central Plains. 

## What happened
In **214 BCE**, under the orders of **Qin Shi Huang (秦始皇)**, imperial forces marched south to consolidate control over the region. The administrative center of **Panyu (番禺)**, located in modern-day Guangzhou, was established as a frontier capital. While archaeological evidence confirms the presence of Qin-era artifacts and salt-production sites in the Hong Kong region—most notably at **Tung Wan (東灣)** on Lantau Island—the extent to which the Qin administration exerted direct, day-to-day governance over the islands remains a subject of historical debate. It is widely accepted that Panyu served as the primary nexus for military logistics and trade, effectively creating a gravitational pull that drew the coastal outposts into a nascent regional economic framework.

## Significance
The founding of Panyu marked the first step in the formal integration of the Hong Kong region into the administrative sphere of the Chinese state. By establishing a reliable market hub for salt and other coastal resources, the Qin created a trade network that relied on the sheltered harbors of the Pearl River estuary. This administrative framework was later refined during the **Han (漢朝)** and **Tang (唐朝)** dynasties, solidifying the role of the region as a strategic maritime corridor. The presence of Panyu transformed Hong Kong from an isolated frontier into a peripheral link in a larger, interconnected economic system that prioritized the flow of goods toward the northern administrative centers.

## Perspectives

- **Mainland Chinese Historiography**: Emphasizes the early and continuous inclusion of the region within the imperial administrative system, highlighting the establishment of Nanhai Commandery as a definitive moment of national unification.
- **Local Hong Kong and New Territories perspectives**: Often focuses on the indigenous maritime identity of the Baiyue, suggesting that the 'integration' under the Qin was a slow, incomplete process that left local customs and social structures largely independent for centuries.
- **Colonial-era scholarship**: Traditionally viewed the region as a backwater until the late imperial era, often downplaying the significance of Qin-era coastal administration in favor of trade routes developed during the Tang and Song periods.
