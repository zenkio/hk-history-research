## Background
Long before the Qin dynasty (秦朝) established the Nanhai Commandery (南海郡) in **214 BCE**, the coastal regions of the Pearl River Delta were inhabited by maritime-oriented populations. Archaeological evidence, particularly from sites such as Sha Ha (沙下) and Sham Wan (深灣), reveals that these early settlers possessed advanced seafaring capabilities. It is widely accepted by modern scholarship that these communities were part of, or closely linked to, the broader expansion of Austronesian and early Baiyue (百越) peoples who moved throughout the South China Sea and the Pacific.

## What happened
Between **1500 BCE and 200 BCE**, these maritime networks matured. These early inhabitants utilized Hong Kong’s natural deep-water harbors, which offered protection from seasonal typhoons. Artifacts such as stone adzes and geometric-patterned pottery suggest that these communities engaged in long-distance trade, exchanging local marine resources for exotic materials like nephrite or specialized ceramics found in other parts of Southeast Asia. While the precise nature of these transactions remains a subject of archaeological debate, the presence of these materials strongly implies a structured system of maritime interaction that operated independently of the political centers in Northern China.

## Significance
This period established the foundational geography of Hong Kong as a critical node for maritime trade. The sheltered bays, such as those found in Tuen Mun (屯門) and Lantau (大嶼山), served as essential points of anchorage. While the later imperial administrations of the Qin and Tang (唐朝) would eventually claim the territory, the early maritime networks defined the region's character as a frontier zone integrated into a southern maritime world rather than solely oriented toward the continental interior. These early routes provided the framework for the subsequent development of coastal ports that would become pivotal during later dynasties.

## Perspectives

- **Mainland Chinese historiography**: Often emphasizes these populations as proto-Baiyue, framing their existence as the earliest stage of the integration of Lingnan (嶺南) into the broader Chinese cultural and political sphere.
- **Modern archaeological and anthropological scholarship**: Focuses on the maritime-centric identity of these peoples, positing that their trade networks were part of a widespread Austronesian sphere that predates and operates independently of centralized Chinese state-building efforts.
