## Background

Before the Qin dynasty's expansion southward, the region encompassing modern Guangdong, Guangxi, and northern Vietnam was inhabited by various indigenous communities collectively referred to as the Baiyue (百越) peoples. In **-214**, the armies of Qin Shi Huang completed their conquest of the Lingnan region, establishing administrative commanderies including Nanhai (南海), Xiang (桂林), and Guilin. To secure this distant frontier, the Qin administration deployed hundreds of thousands of soldiers and settlers from the Central Plains, laying the foundational roots of Han-Yue demographic and cultural interaction in the territory.

## What happened

As the Qin dynasty disintegrated amidst civil war following the death of its first emperor, **Zhao Tuo** (趙佗), a Qin general stationed in the south, recognized the vulnerability of the region. Rather than intervening in the northern conflicts, **Zhao Tuo** chose to consolidate power. In **-204**, he blocked the mountain passes connecting Lingnan to the Central Plains, annexed the neighboring commanderies of Guilin and Xiang, and declared himself King of Nanyue (南越武王). This decisive act founded the Nanyue Kingdom, with its capital established at Panyu (番禺, modern Guangzhou), bringing the wider Pearl River Delta region—including present-day Hong Kong—under localized dynastic rule.

## Significance

The establishment of the Nanyue Kingdom marked a critical turning point in the history of southern China and Hong Kong. By pursuing a deliberate policy of "harmonizing and gathering the Yue" (和輯百越), **Zhao Tuo** encouraged intermarriage and administrative cooperation between the newly arrived Han settlers and the indigenous Yue populations. This governance model accelerated technological advancements, such as advanced iron metallurgy and agriculture, while preserving distinct regional characteristics. The era established a precedent of southern autonomy and hybrid identity that would shape the geopolitical and cultural development of the region for centuries to come.

## Perspectives

- **Imperial Chinese historiography**: Traditional dynastic histories, such as the Shiji, generally viewed Zhao Tuo as a pragmatic and clever frontier official who preserved order in the south during dynastic collapse, eventually accepting vassal status under the Han dynasty to maintain regional stability.
- **Modern regional and local perspectives**: Contemporary scholars and local communities in Guangdong and Hong Kong often emphasize Nanyue as an independent foundational culture, highlighting the synthesis of indigenous Baiyue traditions with Han elements rather than viewing the area merely as an extension of northern empires.
