## Background

Following the collapse of the Qin dynasty, the former Qin general **Zhao Tuo** (趙佗) established the Kingdom of Nanyue (南越國) in **204 BCE**, uniting parts of modern Guangdong, Guangxi, and northern Vietnam. The territory included the coastal areas of modern Hong Kong, which served as maritime trade nodes and maritime routes. For decades, Nanyue maintained a fragile tributary relationship with the Han dynasty founded by **Emperor Gaozu of Han** (漢高祖). However, tensions steadily escalated over trade monopolies, succession disputes, and imperial ambitions.

## What happened

By **111 BCE**, during the reign of **Emperor Wu of Han** (漢武帝), internal political instability weakened Nanyue following a coup led by the Han-sympathizer Prime Minister **Lu Jia** (呂嘉). In response, Emperor Wu dispatched a massive multi-pronged military expedition consisting of naval and land forces totaling over 100,000 troops. The Han forces overwhelmed Nanyue defenses, capturing the capital of Panyu (modern Guangzhou) and extinguishing the kingdom. Archaeological evidence from this period in Hong Kong, such as salt-production sites and coin hoards, suggests the region was rapidly incorporated into the newly established commandery system.

## Significance

The conquest marked a fundamental turning point in the geopolitical and administrative history of southern China and Hong Kong. By dividing Nanyue into nine commanderies—with the Hong Kong region falling under the jurisdiction of **Panyu County** (番禺縣) within **Nanhai Commandery** (南海郡)—the Han Empire integrated the agrarian and coastal frontier into its centralized bureaucratic apparatus. This administrative incorporation facilitated the introduction of Han material culture, ironworking, and agricultural techniques to the Lingnan region, laying the demographic and cultural foundation for subsequent centuries of Chinese dynastic rule.

## Perspectives

- **Imperial Chinese Historiography**: Traditional Chinese histories, beginning with Sima Qian's *Records of the Grand Historian*, frame the conquest as a righteous restoration of imperial order and the legitimate unification of the southern frontier under the Mandate of Heaven.
- **Modern Lingnan and Local Scholarship**: Contemporary regional scholars often emphasize Nanyue as an independent, multi-ethnic civilization with its own distinct maritime identity, viewing the Han conquest as an act of imperial expansion that subjugated local indigenous populations.
