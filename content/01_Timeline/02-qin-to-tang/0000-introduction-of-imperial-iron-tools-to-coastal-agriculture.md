## Background

Before the formal incorporation of the Lingnan region into the **Qin dynasty** (秦朝) and its subsequent consolidation under the **Han dynasty** (漢朝), coastal communities in the area relied primarily on stone, bone, and bronze tools for subsistence. Agriculture in what is now Hong Kong was characterized by small-scale swidden or coastal gathering practices. Following the military expansion of **Emperor Wu of Han** (漢武帝) in **111 BCE**, the imperial administration sought to integrate the newly acquired southern territories into the greater economic and administrative network of the empire.

## What happened

Around **81 BCE**, shortly after the famous Salt and Iron Debates, the imperial state's administrative apparatus and trade networks facilitated the southward migration of metallurgical technology and iron implements. Archaeological evidence from various sites across the Lingnan region indicates an increase in the presence of iron agricultural tools, such as shares, hoes, and sickles. While direct, localized written records for the immediate Hong Kong area during this specific decade are sparse, regional excavations point to a technological shift where bronze and stone implements were progressively supplemented or replaced by durable iron tools in coastal settlements.

## Significance

The introduction of iron tools marked a critical technological turning point for coastal agriculture. Durable iron implements enabled deeper tilling, more efficient land clearing, and better management of local water resources for early forms of paddy rice cultivation. This technological infusion laid the groundwork for denser permanent settlements and increased agrarian output, integrating the coastal fringes more closely into the broader economic patterns of the Han empire.

## Perspectives

- **Mainland Historiography**: Official Chinese scholarship generally emphasizes this era as a progressive integration of Lingnan into a unified Chinese civilization, highlighting how imperial state policies and technological superiority elevated local productive forces.
- **Modern Archaeological Scholarship**: Contemporary archaeological analysis tends to view the process as more gradual and localized, suggesting that indigenous communities selectively adopted iron technology rather than undergoing a sudden, top-down cultural transformation.
- **Colonial and Western Scholarship**: Early British colonial researchers often framed the early history of the territory primarily through the lens of external imperial expansion and migration from the Central Plains, occasionally underestimating the agency of indigenous populations.
