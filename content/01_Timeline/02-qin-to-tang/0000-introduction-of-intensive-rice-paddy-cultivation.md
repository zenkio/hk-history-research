## Background
Before the **Qin Dynasty (秦朝)** period, the coastal region of Hong Kong was primarily occupied by the **Baiyue (百越)** tribes. These early inhabitants practiced a combination of fishing, hunting, and primitive agriculture. While exact historical records from this era are scarce, archaeological findings suggest a transition toward more permanent settlements occurred as techniques from the North and the Yangtze River valley began to influence southern coastal territories.

## What happened
Between **200 BCE and 100 BCE**, evidence emerges suggesting the introduction of intensive rice paddy cultivation. This technology required the management of water levels through sophisticated irrigation and the leveling of fields, typically in the low-lying valleys of the **New Territories (新界)**. Unlike earlier slash-and-burn methods, this sedentary approach allowed for multi-seasonal harvests and a more reliable food supply. The shift coincided with the southward expansion of the **Han Dynasty (漢朝)** administration, which promoted agricultural migration as a means of solidifying control over the southern frontiers.

## Significance
The adoption of wet-rice farming was a pivotal moment in the environmental and social history of the region. It facilitated a more sedentary lifestyle, enabling the first significant population growth in the area. This change necessitated organized communal management of water resources and land, which laid the rudimentary foundations for the social structures seen in later centuries. By modifying the land to suit agricultural needs, these early settlers began the process of altering the Hong Kong landscape from its natural forested state into an anthropogenic environment capable of sustaining denser human settlements.

## Perspectives

- **Colonial-era archaeological scholarship**: Early 20th-century researchers often framed the introduction of farming as a 'civilizing' process brought by northern Han migrants, characterizing the indigenous inhabitants as having negligible agricultural capacity prior to this intervention.
- **Contemporary indigenous and local history perspectives**: Modern scholars and members of local clans often emphasize the continuity of indigenous skills, arguing that the transition was an adaptive evolution rather than a complete replacement of local knowledge by external arrivals.
