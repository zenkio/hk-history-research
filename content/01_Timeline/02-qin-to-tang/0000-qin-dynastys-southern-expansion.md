## Background
Prior to the late third century BCE, the territory comprising modern Hong Kong was inhabited by indigenous Yue (越) peoples, often referred to in ancient texts as the Baiyue (百越). These communities practiced agriculture, fishing, and maritime trade, possessing advanced bronze-working technologies but existing outside the political sphere of the Central Plains states. 

## What happened
Following the unification of China in **221 BCE**, the **Qin Dynasty** (秦朝), under **Emperor Qin Shi Huang** (秦始皇), turned its attention southward to the resource-rich Lingnan region. In **214 BCE**, after overcoming fierce resistance from local populations, Qin forces successfully secured the territory. The imperial court established three commanderies in the newly conquered south: Guilin, Xiang, and **Nanhai Commandery** (南海郡). Modern Hong Kong fell geographically within the jurisdiction of Nanhai Commandery, specifically administered under Panyu County (番禺縣). To facilitate this expansion and supply the armies, the Qin notably constructed the Lingqu Canal (靈渠), connecting the Xiang and Li rivers.

## Significance
This military campaign marked the first definitive incorporation of the Hong Kong region into the administrative structure of a unified Chinese empire. It initiated a long, gradual process of Han Chinese migration, cultural integration, and economic development in China's southern frontier. Archaeological finds in Hong Kong, such as Qin-style pottery and coins found at sites like Lei Cheng Uk and Lantau Island, provide physical evidence of this early southward extension of imperial influence, though the exact nature of day-to-day control during the early imperial period remains a subject of ongoing archaeological research.

## Perspectives

- **Traditional Historiography (Qing/Beijing)**: Views the expansion as a progressive, civilizing unification that brought the Lingnan region into the fold of Chinese civilization and established undisputed sovereign administration from the Central Plains.
- **Modern and Local Scholarship**: Emphasizes that Qin control over the far south was initially loose, military-focused, and coexisted with a dominant indigenous Baiyue culture that retained considerable autonomy for centuries.
- **British Colonial Historiography**: Often marginalized early imperial history, framing Hong Kong primarily as an empty or sparsely populated fishing outpost prior to British acquisition in 1841, thereby downplaying deep-rooted indigenous and imperial ties.
