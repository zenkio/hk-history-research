## Background
Following the annexation of the Nanyue (南越) Kingdom by the **Han Dynasty** in **111 BCE**, the region encompassing modern-day Hong Kong was incorporated into the Panyu (番禺) County of Nanhai (南海) Commandery. This period saw the Han administration seeking to project authority over the southern coastline to ensure the safety of maritime trade, particularly routes leading toward the South China Sea. The area was known for its natural resources, specifically the production of salt and the harvesting of pearls, which were highly valued by the imperial court.

## What happened
Historical records and archaeological surface finds suggest that around **50 CE to 150 CE**, administrative control intensified. While large-scale garrisons were not typically positioned on the islands themselves, a network of coastal watchtowers and signal stations was established or expanded along the Pearl River Delta. These structures served a dual purpose: they provided coastal surveillance against piracy and monitored the movement of vessels involved in the state-controlled salt trade. Archeological evidence from sites such as **Lei Cheng Uk (李鄭屋)** indicates that Han-style burial practices and material culture were present, suggesting a stable, albeit peripheral, imperial presence that relied on these watchtowers to maintain order over the local maritime economy.

## Significance
The expansion of these watchtowers represents the formal integration of the Lingnan coast into the imperial logistical framework. By securing the bays and harbors—including areas near **Tuen Mun (屯門)**—the central government could effectively tax the pearl industry and control the lucrative salt trade. For centuries, these sites provided the necessary security for nascent maritime infrastructure, setting a precedent for the region's later development as a strategic port. While the exact scale of these installations remains a subject of archaeological debate due to the scarcity of surviving structural remains, their existence highlights the strategic importance the Han state placed on protecting its southern maritime frontier.

## Perspectives

- **Mainland Chinese historiography**: Often emphasizes these outposts as evidence of the inevitable and early integration of the Lingnan region into the unified Chinese state under the Han Dynasty, highlighting administrative continuity.
- **Local and colonial-era scholarship**: Early British-era historians occasionally questioned the density of Han military presence, suggesting that these regions remained largely autonomous frontiers where imperial authority was sporadic rather than entrenched.
