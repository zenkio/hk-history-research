## Background
During the **Han Dynasty (漢朝)**, the imperial administration sought to consolidate its influence over the southern coastal regions, including the area of present-day Hong Kong. While the region was sparsely populated by indigenous maritime communities, it served as a critical node for coastal transit. The geography, characterized by jagged islands and complex tidal channels, presented significant challenges to early vessels. Historical records suggest that maritime movement was vital for both local trade and the connectivity of the southern commanderies.

## What happened
Around **100–200 CE**, evidence suggests an evolution in the maritime practices of southern mariners. Improved coastal navigation charts and the refinement of vessel designs allowed sailors to better manage the perilous waters surrounding the islands of the Pearl River Delta. While direct archaeological evidence of Han-era navigational charts is scarce, contemporary scholarship points to a transition from purely shoreline-hugging cabotage to more strategic navigation across the wider regional waters. Mariners utilized the natural harbors of Hong Kong to shelter from typhoons and replenish supplies, effectively integrating the region into a broader maritime network that extended toward the South China Sea.

## Significance
This period marked the transformation of Hong Kong’s waters from a peripheral maritime frontier into a functional link in the regional coastal trade route. The increased frequency of transit laid the conceptual foundation for later administrative developments, such as the later establishment of military and trade outposts. By mastering the tidal and seasonal wind patterns of the region, local maritime actors were able to maintain connectivity between the southern administrative centers of **Panyu (番禺)** and more distant coastal settlements. This era reflects the gradual incorporation of the South China coast into the imperial sphere, a process mediated by the sea rather than purely through land-based expansion.

## Perspectives

- **Later Chinese Imperial Historiography**: Traditional accounts, such as those found in the *Book of Han (漢書)*, emphasize this period as part of a civilizing mission, where the expansion of maritime routes is framed as the benevolent spread of administrative order to the coastal 'southern barbarians'.
- **Modern Archaeological and Local Scholarship**: Contemporary researchers often focus on the agency of indigenous coastal communities, arguing that these groups possessed sophisticated, long-standing maritime knowledge that existed long before formal imperial interest, treating the 'expansion' as a symbiotic interaction rather than a purely top-down imperial project.
