## Background

Following the collapse of the Han dynasty, China fractured into the Three Kingdoms period. The southern region, including modern-day Guangdong and Hong Kong, fell under the control of the **Eastern Wu** (東吳) state, founded by **Sun Quan** (孫權) in **220**.

## What happened

Cut off from overland trade routes to the north, Eastern Wu heavily invested in maritime commerce and naval expansion. Historical records indicate that the state sent envoys and merchants southward by sea, establishing more formalized maritime networks linking the Chinese mainland with Southeast Asia. Coastal commanderies, particularly **Cangwu** (蒼梧) and **Nanhai** (南海), served as administrative hubs for these southern operations. Sheltered waters and strategic locations within the modern Hong Kong territory, such as **Tuen Mun** (屯門), likely functioned as vital waypoints, anchoring sites, and sources of fresh water for passing fleets and merchant vessels.

## Significance

This era marks a transitional phase where the Hong Kong region shifted from a remote imperial outpost into an integrated node within a broader maritime trading network. Later scholarship suggests that this early maritime development laid the logistical groundwork for the economic and navigational importance of the Pearl River Delta in subsequent dynasties. However, the exact nature, scale, and permanence of local settlements during this period remain difficult to reconstruct due to sparse archaeological evidence.

## Perspectives

- **Later scholarship and maritime historians**: Researchers emphasize that Eastern Wu's state-sponsored maritime policies transformed the South China Sea into an active trade corridor, integrating coastal areas like Hong Kong into international exchange networks earlier than previously assumed.
- **Traditional dynastic historiography**: Qing and imperial-era accounts generally frame these southern expansions through the lens of pacification, taxation, and the civilizing mission of the central state over southern non-Han populations and frontier maritime spaces.
