## Background
Prior to the 4th century, the territory encompassing modern-day Hong Kong and Shenzhen was administered as part of the broader **Nanhai Commandery** (**南海郡**), established following the Qin conquest of the Baiyue region in **214 BCE**. Because this southern frontier was distant from the imperial center in northern China, early administration was relatively loose, relying heavily on local indigenous leadership and military garrisons stationed at strategic points such as Tuen Mun (**屯門**).

## What happened
In **331** during the Eastern Jin Dynasty (**東晉**), the imperial government reorganized the administrative divisions of the south. Part of the **Nanhai Commandery** (**南海郡**) was carved out to create **Bao'an County** (**寶安縣**). This administrative restructuring meant that settlements, salt production sites, and population centers within the Hong Kong region were placed under the direct jurisdiction of a county magistrate based in the new county seat.

## Significance
The creation of **Bao'an County** (**寶安縣**) marks a watershed moment in the region's administrative history. It brought the Hong Kong territory under direct, localized imperial administration for the first time, laying the groundwork for subsequent dynastic taxation, census-taking, and cultural integration. While the local population remained ethnically and culturally distinct from the northern Han mainstream, this administrative anchor began centuries of deeper integration with the Chinese imperial state.

## Perspectives

- **Imperial Historiography**: Traditional Chinese dynastic histories view the establishment of Bao'an County as a natural extension of civilizing governance and administrative rationalization over southern frontier territories.
- **Modern Regional Scholarship**: Recent historians emphasize that despite the new county administration, actual control on the ground remained weak, with local indigenous clans and maritime communities maintaining a high degree of autonomy.
