## Background

Before the fourth century, the region encompassing modern Hong Kong and Shenzhen existed primarily as a peripheral frontier of the Chinese empire. Following the Qin conquest of the Baiyue lands in **214 BCE**, the area was nominally incorporated into commanderies such as Nanhai (南海). However, direct imperial control remained weak during the Han Dynasty and the early periods of fragmentation. Settlements were sparse, consisting largely of indigenous coastal communities, salt producers, and transient military outposts.

## What happened

In **331** (the 6th Xiankang year of the Eastern Jin Dynasty), the imperial administration split Baoan County (寶安縣) from Nanhai Commandery and established **Dongguan County** (東莞縣). This administrative restructuring created a localized seat of governance closer to the southern coast. The territory of contemporary Hong Kong, including areas like Tuen Mun (屯門), fell within the jurisdiction of this newly formed county. This change brought the region into a more formalized tax and census system, marking a transition from a distant military frontier to an administered territory.

## Significance

The establishment of Dongguan County in **331** is widely regarded by historians as a crucial watershed moment. It marks the first time Hong Kong was placed under a distinct, localized county-level administration rather than being governed solely through distant commanderies. This administrative shift laid the groundwork for subsequent waves of migration, increased economic activity related to salt and pearl production, and the gradual integration of the Pearl River Delta into broader Chinese dynastic structures.

## Perspectives

- **Imperial Historiography**: Traditional dynastic records view the creation of Dongguan County as a natural extension of civilizing administration and border pacification, successfully bringing the southern coastal frontiers under the tax and ritual systems of the Jin Dynasty.
- **Local and Regional Scholarship**: Modern regional historians emphasize that despite this administrative leap on paper, actual day-to-day imperial control over the islands and coastal bays of Hong Kong remained minimal, with local clans and indigenous populations largely managing their own affairs.
