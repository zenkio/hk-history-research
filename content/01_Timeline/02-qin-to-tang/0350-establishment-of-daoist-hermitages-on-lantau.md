## Background
During the **Eastern Jin dynasty** (317–420), the region now known as Hong Kong served as a peripheral maritime frontier. While the administrative center was based further inland, the islands around the Pearl River estuary provided sanctuary for those seeking isolation from the political instability of the northern plains. By the mid-to-late **4th century**, as Daoism gained prominence among the literati and the elite, coastal areas and mountain retreats were increasingly viewed as spiritually potent spaces for cultivation.

## What happened
Between **350 and 400**, archaeological and historical fragments suggest that individuals identified as reclusive Daoists began establishing hermitages on **Lantau Island** (大嶼山). These practitioners, who often combined their religious pursuits with the study of alchemy, medicinal botany, and mineralogy, found the rugged topography of the island conducive to their practices. While the exact locations of these early structures remain difficult to identify with absolute archaeological certainty, oral traditions associated with later temple sites often point to these early arrivals as the precursors to permanent settlement. These hermits likely engaged in basic agriculture and maritime resource collection, slowly establishing a degree of permanent habitation on an island previously characterized by transient fishing communities.

## Significance
The arrival of these figures is widely interpreted as a catalyst for the introduction of written culture and formal knowledge systems to the coastal inhabitants of the region. As these practitioners brought with them scrolls, herbal texts, and metallurgical knowledge, they initiated a gradual integration of the maritime periphery into the broader intellectual sphere of the **Jin** and later the **Tang dynasty** (618–907). Their presence transformed Lantau from an purely strategic or extractive frontier into a space associated with spiritual and medicinal traditions, a legacy that evolved into the extensive temple culture seen in later centuries.

## Perspectives

- **Later Local Tradition**: Local communities often view these early hermits as the founding ancestors of sacred geography on Lantau. They emphasize the continuity between these ancient hermitages and the established temples that serve as centers for modern village life.
- **Academic Historians**: Professional historians caution that evidence for specific 4th-century hermitages is anecdotal. They often highlight the difficulty of distinguishing between historical fact and the much later 'myth-making' used to grant local sites greater historical prestige.
