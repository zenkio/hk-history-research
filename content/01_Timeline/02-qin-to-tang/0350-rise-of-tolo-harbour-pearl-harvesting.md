## Background

During the Jin dynasty (**265–420**), the southern territories newly integrated into the Chinese imperial administration increasingly became sources of rare and exotic tribute goods. While the region comprising modern-day Hong Kong was largely viewed by the imperial center as a remote and malaria-prone frontier, its coastal waters possessed natural marine environments suitable for pearl oysters. The shallow, sheltered waters of Tolo Harbour (吐露港) gradually emerged as a notable habitat for these valuable bivalves.

## What happened

Around **350**, commercial and state-directed harvesting of pearls in the Tai Po (大埔) area began to intensify. Local inhabitants, possessing specialized maritime knowledge, engaged in arduous and often dangerous free-diving to collect pearl oysters from the seabed. These pearls—prized for their luster and rarity—became key luxury commodities funneled upward through regional administrative networks to satisfy the extravagant tastes of the imperial court and southern elites. The trade transformed parts of the local coastal economy from basic subsistence fishing and agriculture into an enterprise tied directly to imperial consumption.

## Significance

The rise of the Tolo Harbour pearl industry marked an important early integration of the Hong Kong region into the broader economic and political orbit of Chinese dynasties. Rather than remaining an isolated backwater, the area gained strategic and economic value as a provider of imperial tribute. This industry laid the foundation for centuries of official pearl harvesting in the region, which would experience subsequent peaks and severe regulatory crackdowns during the Tang (**618–907**) and Han-descended dynasties due to the immense physical toll the diving demands placed on local communities.

## Perspectives

- **Imperial Chinese Historians and Court Officials**: Viewed the Tolo Harbour pearl fisheries as a legitimate and auspicious source of rare tribute, reflecting the reach of imperial virtue and providing essential luxury items for state rituals and elite ornamentation.
- **Local New Territories Communities and Modern Scholarship**: Emphasize the severe human cost borne by indigenous divers, noting that excessive imperial demand frequently forced locals into dangerous conditions, sometimes leading to local uprisings or deliberate over-harvesting to deter official exploitation.
