## Background
During the Eastern Jin dynasty, **Faxian** (法顯) embarked on a monumental overland journey to India in search of Buddhist scriptures. By the early fifth century, after years of travel across Central Asia and the Indian subcontinent, he prepared to return to China. Rather than retracing his steps across dangerous overland routes, he chose to take a maritime passage across the Indian Ocean and through the South China Sea, reflecting the increasing viability and utilization of maritime trade and pilgrimage routes during this era.

## What happened
In **412**, **Faxian** boarded a merchant vessel in India bound for Guangzhou (廣州). The perilous voyage encountered severe storms, forcing the crew to jettison cargo to save the ship. According to his later written account, *A Record of Buddhist Kingdoms* (佛國記), the ship drifted off course and eventually made landfall after enduring months at sea. While the precise route remains a subject of historical debate, scholars generally agree that the journey passed through the vital maritime networks of Southeast Asia and the South China Sea, waters adjacent to the modern territory of Hong Kong, which served as natural shelters, navigation landmarks, and watering stops for early seafarers.

## Significance
**Faxian**’s journey is a landmark in the history of Asian maritime navigation. It provides some of the earliest detailed written documentation of long-distance sea travel connecting India and Southern China. For the region surrounding Hong Kong, such as Tuen Mun (屯門) and the broader Pearl River Delta, accounts like **Faxian**'s highlight the area's ancient role within the broader South China Sea maritime network. Long before it became a modern entrepôt, the coastal waters of Hong Kong were part of a crucial shipping lane utilized by monks, merchants, and diplomats navigating between China and the wider world.

## Perspectives

- **Modern Historiography**: Historians view Faxian's voyage as vital evidence of the integration of maritime Silk Road networks, demonstrating that by the fifth century, ocean-going routes were sufficiently established to support long-distance religious and commercial travel.
- **Traditional Buddhist Scholarship**: Buddhist tradition emphasizes Faxian's journey as a spiritual triumph, viewing the survival of his perilous sea voyage as a testament to the protective power of the Buddha's teachings and the sacred mission of bringing authentic scriptures back to China.
