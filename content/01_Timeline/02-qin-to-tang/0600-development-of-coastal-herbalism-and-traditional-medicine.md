## Background
During the **Tang Dynasty (618–907)**, the region now known as Hong Kong was administratively distant from the imperial centers of the Central Plains. Under the jurisdiction of **Tuen Mun (屯門)**, coastal communities functioned as essential nodes in maritime trade, yet their remote location meant that access to standardized, northern-produced medicinal materials was sporadic. Residents relied heavily on the surrounding subtropical environment, characterized by high humidity and a diverse array of endemic plant life.

## What happened
Around **600–650**, there is evidence suggesting an intensification of local botanical collection. Lacking consistent access to imperial pharmacies, local practitioners—often acting as family elders or maritime healers—developed specialized knowledge of local herbs, roots, and shrubs capable of treating tropical ailments such as heatstroke, skin irritations, and gastrointestinal issues. While written records from this era remain sparse, the oral traditions of the indigenous **Punti (本地)** communities suggest that this period marked a formalization of "Cool Tea" (涼茶) culture and the use of bitter herbs to balance the body's internal humidity, a practice necessitated by the harsh southern maritime climate.

## Significance
This period highlights the emergence of a regional medical identity distinct from, yet conceptually aligned with, the imperial medical canon. By adapting traditional healing theories to the local flora, coastal communities ensured their survival in a challenging ecological niche. This synthesis of maritime knowledge and traditional philosophy laid the foundational practices for what would later become the distinct Southern medical tradition. The reliance on indigenous flora was not merely a survival tactic but a sophisticated adaptation that allowed Tuen Mun and its surrounding areas to maintain health despite significant geographical and political detachment from the capital.

## Perspectives

- **Local New Territories Lineage Perspectives**: Many ancestral clans view this era as the beginning of an autonomous medical heritage, arguing that the knowledge of coastal herbs was passed down through matrilineal lines and clan elders, independent of imperial approval.
- **Imperial Historical Records**: Official Tang sources often minimized the medical innovation of the southern frontier, categorizing the region primarily as a maritime transit point (Tuen Mun) rather than a center for intellectual or medical development.
