## Background
Long before the full integration of the Pearl River Delta into the imperial Chinese administrative apparatus, the coastal waters and sheltered bays of modern-day Hong Kong supported diverse indigenous populations. Following the Qin conquest in **214 BCE**, successive dynasties sought to pacify and assimilate the non-Han groups collectively referred to in historical texts as the Yue (越) peoples. Over centuries of migration from the Central Plains, interactions between mainland settlers and indigenous maritime dwellers grew more complex, setting the stage for distinct occupational and social stratification along the coast.

## What happened
By the early **Tang Dynasty (唐朝)** around **618 CE**, historical and archaeological indicators suggest that the boat-dwelling populations along the southern littoral began to cohere into a more clearly defined social group known as the Dan (蜑). Unlike the land-based peasantry, these maritime dwellers lived entirely on vessels, mastering the intricacies of coastal navigation, deep-sea fishing, and local pilotage. While early imperial gazetteers often marginalized them as outsiders or non-citizens, the Dan people developed specialized maritime knowledge that became indispensable for regional trade and coastal defense in areas such as Tuen Mun (屯門).

## Significance
The consolidation of the Dan community laid the foundational social structure for Hong Kong's pre-modern maritime economy. Their emergence established a dualistic pattern in local history: a land-based agrarian society developing alongside a distinct, water-bound society that operated largely outside the traditional land-based lineage and clan systems. This boat-dwelling culture persisted as a vital pillar of the region's economy for centuries, shaping the unique coastal identity of the Pearl River Delta.

## Perspectives

- **Imperial Chinese / Qing Historiography**: Traditional dynastic records frequently categorized the Dan as marginal or 'barand' populations (Yue), often emphasizing their separation from mainstream Confucian tax and ritual structures while utilizing their maritime expertise for coastal administration.
- **Local Tanka Community Oral Tradition**: Descendants of the boat-dwelling communities often emphasize a long, proud heritage of self-sufficiency, ancient indigenous roots in the southern waters, and unique spiritual traditions distinct from mainland agrarian customs.
- **Modern Scholarship**: Contemporary historians and anthropologists view the emergence of the Dan as a gradual, fluid process of ethnic and occupational boundary-formation shaped by state marginalization and economic specialization rather than a sudden tribal origin.
