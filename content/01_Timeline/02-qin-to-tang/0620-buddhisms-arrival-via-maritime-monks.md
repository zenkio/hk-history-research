## Background
During the **Tang Dynasty** (618–907), the Pearl River Delta began to emerge as a critical node in maritime trade. As the empire stabilized, Buddhist thought spread from the capital to the southern frontiers. The geography of the region—defined by deep-water bays and natural harbors—made Tuen Mun (屯門) a strategic point for vessels traveling between the Chinese mainland and Southeast Asia. This period saw the gradual integration of the region into the broader economic and religious networks of the empire.

## What happened
Between **620 and 650**, it is widely believed that Buddhist monks traveling on merchant ships utilized the coastal inlets of the territory for shelter, water, and rest. These monks often established small, temporary shrines or stone markers near landing points. While there is little archaeological evidence of grand temple structures from this specific timeframe, later historical records and folklore suggest that these coastal shrines served as focal points for both seafaring travelers and small-scale local settlements. The presence of these monks introduced new ritualistic practices to the local population, who were largely engaged in fishing and salt production.

## Significance
The introduction of Buddhism to the coastal frontier signifies the transition of the region from an isolated outpost to a participant in the maritime Silk Road. Scholars debate the extent of this influence during the early **7th century**. Some argue that the arrival of these monks was merely incidental to trade, while others contend it marked the beginning of a religious shift that would later define the cultural landscape of the New Territories. The establishment of these shrines helped create a social infrastructure that supported maritime safety and connectivity. Whether these sites were permanent or nomadic remains a subject of ongoing historical investigation, as modern urban development has frequently obscured evidence of early medieval structures.

## Perspectives

- **Local New Territories lineages**: Many local clans view these early shrines as the precursors to the ancestral worship and temple networks that later anchored their community identity. They often emphasize a long, continuous tradition of spiritual practice that predates later administrative integration.
- **Academic historians**: Academic historians often maintain a more skeptical stance, noting the scarcity of primary sources from the 7th century. They emphasize that while maritime contact definitely occurred, the 'Buddhist influence' may have been highly localized and lacked the institutional structure seen in later centuries.
