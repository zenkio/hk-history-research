## Background
While the region around Tuen Mun (屯門) had served as a strategic maritime outpost since the Qin (秦) and Han (漢) dynasties, the agrarian landscape underwent significant changes during the **Tang (唐) Dynasty (618–907)**. Before this period, human activity in the region was primarily centered on salt production and fishing. As the Tang administration consolidated its presence along the South China coast, the need for sustainable food supplies for stationed garrisons and passing merchant vessels became a priority.

## What happened
Between **650 and 750**, historical evidence—corroborated by archaeological findings of Tang-era ceramics—suggests that settlers began to implement more intensive land management practices. The most notable development was the introduction of hillside terracing (梯田). By carving flat, leveled steps into the slopes, settlers were able to maximize arable land in a terrain characterized by hilly geography. This allowed for better irrigation control, particularly for the cultivation of rice. It is uncertain to what extent these techniques were developed by local indigenous groups versus introduced by migrant agriculturalists from the Central Plains, but the shift indicates a transition toward a sedentary, surplus-producing agrarian economy.

## Significance
These advancements in land management were critical for the development of **Tuen Mun (屯門)** as a maritime hub. By increasing local food production, the region could sustain a larger permanent population and provide necessary provisions for the burgeoning maritime trade routes connecting Guangzhou (廣州) to the rest of the world. This infrastructure laid the foundational social and economic patterns that would define the New Territories (新界) for centuries to come, integrating the region more tightly into the imperial economic system.

## Perspectives

- **Later scholarly/archaeological perspective**: Archaeologists emphasize that the transition to intensive agriculture was driven by both population growth and state-sponsored land reclamation policies. They argue that these terrace sites are essential physical evidence of Hong Kong’s integration into the medieval Chinese economy.
- **Local New Territories clan traditions**: Traditions held by indigenous clans often trace their land rights and cultivation customs to early settlement periods, viewing the landscape not merely as an imperial outpost, but as an ancestral legacy shaped by generations of family labor.
