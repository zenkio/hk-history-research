## Background
During the **Tang Dynasty** (618–907), the maritime trade routes connecting Guangzhou (廣州) to the wider Indian Ocean expanded significantly. While the region was once considered a remote frontier, the growth of the Maritime Silk Road brought greater administrative attention to the coastal clusters of the Lingnan (嶺南) region. The area now known as the New Territories, particularly around the **Tuen Mun** (屯門) and **Tai Po** (大埔) areas, provided a distinct ecological environment suitable for the cultivation of agarwood, a resinous wood highly prized for its fragrance.

## What happened
Between approximately **700 and 750**, the production of incense emerged as a localized economic specialization. Historical accounts suggest that local communities began harvesting *Aquilaria sinensis* (土沉香) to supply markets in **Chang'an** (長安), the imperial capital. The incense was transported via coastal shipping routes to the port of Guangzhou, which served as the primary gateway for international trade and domestic distribution. The scale of this trade remains a subject of archaeological debate; while some scholars argue for a highly organized, state-sanctioned industry, others suggest it functioned as a decentralized, community-driven enterprise that supplemented subsistence agriculture.

## Significance
The incense trade marked a pivotal transition for the region from a peripheral zone to an integrated component of the imperial economy. The designation of 'incense harbors' or *Heung Gong* (香港) in historical toponymy likely finds its roots in these early trade activities. This era established a economic template where coastal resources were extracted and redirected to meet the aesthetic and religious demands of the central government. Furthermore, the presence of military patrols in **Tuen Mun** during the **Tang Dynasty** suggests that the authorities deemed the region’s maritime security essential for protecting these lucrative trade flows.

## Perspectives

- **Imperial-centric (Qing/Beijing tradition)**: This perspective emphasizes the role of central administrative planning and the integration of the coastal periphery into the taxation and trade systems of the Tang state, viewing local activity as a natural consequence of imperial development.
- **Local New Territories perspective**: Many local communities view the incense industry as a foundational element of indigenous identity, highlighting the ingenuity of early settlers in utilizing local ecology to maintain economic independence from central authorities.
- **Modern Historiography**: Contemporary scholars often call for caution, noting that while the incense trade is historically significant, the lack of extensive, site-specific archaeological evidence from the 8th century makes it difficult to distinguish between sustained industrial output and small-scale, opportunistic commerce.
