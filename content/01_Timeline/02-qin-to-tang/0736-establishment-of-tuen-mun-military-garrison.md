## Background
During the Tang dynasty (**618–907**), the imperial court increasingly recognized the economic and strategic importance of maritime commerce. As trade flourished along the Maritime Silk Road, linking southern Chinese ports with Southeast Asia, India, and the Middle East, the Pearl River Delta emerged as a vital hub. However, the region's complex coastline, dotted with islands and sheltered bays, also provided ideal refuges for pirates and armed groups who threatened merchant shipping.

## What happened
In **736**, under the reign of Emperor Xuanzong of Tang (**唐玄宗**), the imperial government stationed a military garrison consisting of **2,000 soldiers** in Tuen Mun (屯門). This outpost was intended to establish direct imperial control over the strategic waterway, protect foreign and domestic merchant vessels, and defend against coastal incursions. The garrison also served as an administrative and logistical node supporting the broader naval and military infrastructure of the Lingnan (嶺南) region, which was overseen from nearby Guangzhou (廣州), then known as Canton.

## Significance
The establishment of the Tuen Mun garrison marks one of the earliest well-documented instances of direct, institutional military presence by a central Chinese dynasty in the territory that is now Hong Kong. It underscores the integration of the region into the imperial administrative and defensive network during the Tang period, moving the area from a loose frontier zone to a strategically managed maritime gateway.

## Perspectives

- **Imperial and Official Historiography**: Traditional Chinese historical records view the garrison as a necessary and benevolent measure by the imperial center to maintain maritime security, project state power, and foster prosperous international trade along the southern coast.
- **Modern Regional and Archaeological Perspective**: Modern historians and archaeologists note that while the garrison represents early state incorporation, the local reality likely involved a high degree of autonomy, with indigenous coastal communities engaging in maritime trade and local governance largely on their own terms.
