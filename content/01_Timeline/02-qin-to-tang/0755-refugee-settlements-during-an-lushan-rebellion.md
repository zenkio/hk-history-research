## Background
During the mid-**Tang dynasty (唐朝)**, the empire experienced unprecedented territorial expansion and economic prosperity. However, the internal stability of the Middle Kingdom was shattered in **755** by the **An Lushan Rebellion (安史之亂)**. Initiated by the general **An Lushan (安祿山)**, this conflict engulfed the northern heartlands, destabilizing the political order and prompting massive internal displacement. As northern administrative control weakened, the southern frontier regions—previously considered peripheral—began to receive waves of migrants seeking safety from the devastation.

## What happened
The influx of refugees into the coastal areas of modern-day Guangdong and the Pearl River Delta, including the territory of Hong Kong, occurred primarily between **755** and **763**. While Hong Kong was not a centralized urban hub at the time, its geography—characterized by sheltered bays and islands—made it a natural destination for maritime-oriented migrants. Archaeological evidence from this period suggests an increase in activity at strategic locations such as **Tuen Mun (屯門)**, which served as a vital maritime gateway. It is widely accepted by historians that these settlements were not necessarily planned state resettlements, but rather organic, smaller-scale arrivals of families and officials fleeing the chaos of the north, seeking to establish agrarian or salt-producing livelihoods in the relative isolation of the southern coast.

## Significance
The arrival of these populations is often cited as a turning point in the integration of Hong Kong into the broader cultural sphere of the **Tang dynasty**. The migration brought northern linguistic influences, agricultural practices, and lineage-based social structures that would eventually define the developmental trajectory of the region. Furthermore, the increased human presence led to a more permanent establishment of administrative oversight in the coastal districts, shifting Hong Kong from a sparsely populated maritime waypoint toward a more settled frontier society. While specific records of individual settlements are scarce, this period likely solidified the role of the coastal islands as a buffer zone and a conduit for trade and culture, laying the foundation for the lineage-based clans that would become prominent in the New Territories in subsequent centuries.

## Perspectives

- **Mainland Chinese historiography**: Traditional accounts emphasize the rebellion as a critical event that solidified the southward shift of Chinese civilization. This view posits that these refugees were instrumental in 'civilizing' the southern frontiers and cementing the legitimacy of Tang cultural dominance in the region.
- **Local New Territories historiography**: Many local clans and family lineages in the New Territories point to these early migrations as the ancestral roots of their settlement patterns. They view these events not merely as refugee episodes, but as the foundational arrivals of the families that would define the area's ancestral heritage.
- **Archaeological scholarship**: Modern scholars exercise caution, noting that while migration undoubtedly occurred, the scale and impact are often difficult to quantify due to a lack of contemporary written records specific to the Hong Kong area. They focus on material evidence like ceramics and kiln sites to trace these demographic shifts.
