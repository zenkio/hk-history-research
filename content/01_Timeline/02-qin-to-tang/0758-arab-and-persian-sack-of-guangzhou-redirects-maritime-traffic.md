## Background
During the Tang dynasty (**唐朝**), Guangzhou (廣州) served as the preeminent southern port for the Maritime Silk Road, hosting a large, thriving community of foreign merchants, primarily from Arabia and Persia. The imperial court established the office of the Superintendent of Trading Ships (市舶使) in Guangzhou around 714 to oversee this lucrative maritime commerce, taxing foreign traders and managing their diasporic communities.

## What happened
According to historical records, in **758** (the 21st year of the Tianbao era under Emperor Suzong of Tang), disgruntled Arab and Persian foreign merchants and pirates sacked and burned Guangzhou, subsequently fleeing the city by sea. While the Tang authorities eventually restored order, this violent disruption severely compromised the safety and reliability of Guangzhou's inner-river harbor for incoming merchant fleets. Consequently, maritime traffic seeking to avoid the unrest began utilizing alternative, remote deep-water anchorages and coastal waystations along the Pearl River Delta's periphery, including the sheltered bays of the modern Hong Kong territory such as Tuen Mun (屯門).

## Significance
This disruption marked an important early catalyst in bringing the coastal waters of Hong Kong into the broader sphere of documented maritime trade networks. Although the shift was initially a temporary security measure, it highlighted the strategic geographical value of Hong Kong's deep-water bays as natural refuges and trading nodes. Tuen Mun, later fortified by the Tang government, emerged as a vital naval and navigational outpost to police these very sea lanes and manage the resurgent maritime traffic.

## Perspectives

- **Imperial Chinese Historiography**: Traditional dynastic histories frame the event primarily as a foreign rebellion and lawless sacking that threatened state stability, justifying subsequent tighter imperial controls and military patrols over coastal waters and foreign merchant quarters.
- **Modern Maritime Scholarship**: Contemporary historians view the 758 raid as a crucial structural pivot in early Maritime Silk Road logistics, demonstrating how localized political violence routinely forced merchant fleets to diversify their routes and rely on peripheral island anchorages like those in Hong Kong.
