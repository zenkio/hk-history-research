## Background
During the Tang dynasty (**618–907**), the imperial center of China actively encouraged the development and integration of its southern frontier regions. While the Hong Kong area had previously served primarily as a military outpost, salt-production center, and maritime transit point, shifting demographics and regional instability in the Central Plains drove waves of migration southward.

## What happened
Around **c. 800**, oral genealogies and clan records indicate that the earliest precursors and ancestors of what would become the prominent indigenous lineage clans—such as the **Tang clan (鄧氏)** of Kam Tin (錦田)—began arriving in the region. These northern migrants brought advanced agricultural techniques, particularly paddy rice cultivation, which allowed them to transform the marshy coastal lowlands and river valleys of the New Territories into productive agrarian settlements. Over subsequent centuries, these groups consolidated their presence, establishing walled villages and ancestral halls that formed the bedrock of local society.

## Significance
This early migration wave laid the foundational social and economic structures of the New Territories. The descendants of these northern settlers developed sophisticated lineage networks that preserved distinct cultural traditions, distinct from both urban Kowloon and mainland Guangdong, shaping the rural identity of the region well into the modern era.

## Perspectives

- **New Territories Indigenous Communities**: Local lineage traditions and written genealogies (zupu) emphasize continuous ancestral habitation dating back to the Tang dynasty, viewing their forebears as pioneers who brought civilization and agriculture to an untamed southern frontier.
- **Modern Historiography**: Contemporary historians suggest that while early migration roots trace back towards the late Tang and Song periods, many formalized lineage structures and exaggerated Tang-era origin myths were actually codified much later during the Ming and Qing dynasties to bolster local land rights and social status.
