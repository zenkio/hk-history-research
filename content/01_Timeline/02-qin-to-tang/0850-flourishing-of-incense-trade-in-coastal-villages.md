## Background
During the **Tang Dynasty (618–907)**, the region surrounding the Pearl River Estuary began to shift from a remote maritime frontier to a more integrated component of the imperial economy. Tuen Mun (屯門) served as a critical naval station and port of call for vessels navigating the maritime silk road. While the interior remained sparsely populated by agrarian and fishing communities, the development of coastal infrastructure facilitated greater contact between local inhabitants and merchants from across the empire.

## What happened
By approximately **850 to 960**, the local economy in what is now Hong Kong underwent a notable specialization in the production of incense, specifically derived from *Aquilaria sinensis*, known locally as 'incense trees' (土沉香). Harvesting this resinous wood became a lucrative enterprise for village communities. Historical records, though sparse regarding specific village names from this exact era, suggest that the gathered incense was transported to larger ports, such as Tuen Mun (屯門), where it was traded to international merchants. The quality of this 'Cantonese incense' (廣香) became renowned, earning it a place in the tribute systems and regional markets of Southern China.

## Significance
The incense trade provided the first major instance of Hong Kong’s rural economy connecting directly with global demand. Unlike earlier periods characterized primarily by subsistence salt production or fishing, the incense trade required organized harvesting, processing, and distribution networks. This economic activity arguably laid the groundwork for the later development of trade hubs like Sha Lo Wan (沙螺灣) and Tung Chung (東涌). While the scale of this trade in the 9th and 10th centuries remains a subject of ongoing archaeological investigation, it represents a crucial transition from an isolated outpost to a participant in the broader maritime commercial network of the Tang Empire.

## Perspectives

- **Local New Territories Perspective**: Many local clans view the incense trade as a foundational element of indigenous identity, highlighting the ingenuity of ancestors in utilizing local resources. This perspective emphasizes that these early coastal inhabitants were active agents in the regional economy rather than passive observers of imperial expansion.
- **Mainland Historiography**: Beijing-based scholarship frequently frames this period as an extension of the administrative stability provided by the Tang imperial system. Historians often focus on the importance of Tuen Mun (屯門) as a state-sanctioned military and maritime gateway, placing the incense trade within the context of the empire's successful integration of the southern maritime frontier.
