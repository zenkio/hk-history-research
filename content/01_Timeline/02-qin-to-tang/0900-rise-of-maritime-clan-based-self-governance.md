## Background
By the late **9th century**, the central authority of the Tang Dynasty (唐朝) was in severe decline, marked by internal rebellions and administrative paralysis. While the region of Tuen Mun (屯門) had served as a strategic military outpost and naval base during the **8th century**, the physical distance from the imperial capital meant that central control became increasingly nominal. As the grasp of the central government loosened, the local environment necessitated a transition from state-led administration to localized, informal structures.

## What happened
Between **900 and 960**, as the transition from the Tang to the Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms period occurred, coastal lineages emerged as the primary arbiters of order. These clans, often centered around kinship networks, took control of local maritime traffic, port management, and salt production. Historical records from the era are sparse, leading to uncertainty regarding the extent of their autonomy. It is broadly accepted that these groups managed the entry points of the Pearl River, providing protection and logistical support to merchants in exchange for tributes or fees. Whether this represented a formal breakdown of imperial law or simply a pragmatic adaptation to a power vacuum remains a subject of ongoing academic debate.

## Significance
This period is viewed as the formative era for the clan-based society that would later characterize the New Territories. By consolidating power, these maritime lineages established the social and economic foundations for later ancestral halls and land-ownership patterns. The shift established a precedent where local communities, rather than the imperial bureaucracy, became the primary stakeholders in the maritime economy. This era reflects the emergence of a regional identity defined by seafaring, trade, and lineage loyalty, which would persist long after the central government reasserted its influence in the subsequent Song Dynasty (宋朝).

## Perspectives

- **Colonial-era Historiography**: Early colonial historians often characterized this period as one of 'lawless' piracy and isolation, emphasizing the lack of effective government to justify the civilizing role of later administrations. They tended to view the rise of clans as a primitive response to an absence of imperial oversight.
- **Local New Territories Lineage Tradition**: Many local clans possess genealogies that trace their ancestors back to this era, framing the development as a foundational settlement period. From this perspective, the rise of these lineages represents the dignified establishment of orderly ancestral governance rather than a reaction to political instability.
