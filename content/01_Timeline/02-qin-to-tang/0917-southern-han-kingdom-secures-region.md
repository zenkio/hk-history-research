## Background

In **907**, the collapse of the **Tang dynasty (唐朝)** plunged China into the turbulent **Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms (五代十國)** period. During this era of regional fragmentation, the far southern periphery of the former empire saw the rise of local regimes seeking to secure trade routes and territorial control. The coastal areas corresponding to the modern **Lingnan (嶺南)** region and present-day Hong Kong experienced shifting authorities as local warlords vied for dominance.

## What happened

In **917**, the warlord **Liu Yin (劉隱)** and his successor **Liu Yan (劉龑)** established the **Southern Han kingdom (南漢)**, centered in modern-day Guangzhou. With this consolidation of power, the territories encompassing the Pearl River Delta and the coastal areas of Hong Kong were officially brought under Southern Han administration. Historical records suggest that the regime sought to maximize the economic output of its newly secured southern frontiers. This included the intensification of salt production, pearl harvesting, and the exploitation of other valuable coastal natural resources in areas such as **Tuen Mun (屯門)**.

## Significance

Under Southern Han rule, the integration of the Hong Kong region into a localized southern kingdom marked a shift from a distant imperial outpost to a more actively managed economic zone. The emphasis on salt and pearl production laid foundational economic patterns for coastal settlements that would persist into the subsequent **Song dynasty (宋朝)**. While written records from this era are sparse, archaeological findings in the region point to growing maritime trade and localized industry during the tenth century.

## Perspectives

- **Traditional Historiography**: Traditional Chinese histories often view the Southern Han through the lens of regional fragmentation, occasionally depicting its rulers as extravagant while acknowledging their role in maintaining southern economic development during a chaotic national period.
- **Modern Archaeological Perspective**: Contemporary scholars emphasize physical artifacts and trade ceramics found in the region, suggesting that the Southern Han period was crucial for integrating Hong Kong's coastal communities into broader maritime trade networks of the South China Sea.
