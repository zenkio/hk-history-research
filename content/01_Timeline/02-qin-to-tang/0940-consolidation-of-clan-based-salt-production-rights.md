## Background
Historically, the coastal areas of present-day Hong Kong, particularly around **Tuen Mun (屯門)** and **Tai O (大澳)**, served as vital maritime nodes. Since the **Qin (秦朝)** and **Han (漢朝)** dynasties, these regions were periodically integrated into the imperial administrative apparatus, primarily to manage the lucrative salt trade. Under the **Tang (唐朝)**, the state maintained a strict salt monopoly, establishing official salt administrative bureaus. However, as central authority waned toward the mid-10th century, the state's ability to police these remote marshes diminished, leaving a power vacuum in the coastal peripheries.

## What happened
Around **940 to 960 CE**, the decline of the Tang imperial system facilitated a shift in local economic control. Powerful clans, often linked to established families who had provided local administrative services or maritime security, moved to secure hereditary rights over salt production sites. By occupying salt-producing tidal flats and marshes, these lineages bypassed state-controlled distribution channels. This process is generally viewed as an transition from state-sanctioned salt production to a localized, clan-managed economic model. While the exact mechanics of these transfers remain unclear due to the scarcity of contemporary records, archaeological evidence and later genealogical records suggest that these clans established durable monopolies that regulated both the labor and the output of salt pans.

## Significance
The consolidation of these rights was foundational to the development of the **New Territories (新界)** rural social structure. By controlling the primary economic engine of the era, these clans were able to accumulate surplus wealth, which was subsequently invested in ancestral halls, land holdings, and scholarly education. This shift created a stratified society where clan membership dictated access to resources and political influence, a structure that would endure for nearly a millennium. This period marks the embryonic stage of the "land-holding clan" model that characterizes much of the documented history of Hong Kong's indigenous communities.

## Perspectives

- **Local Indigenous Clans**: Many local clans in the New Territories view this era as the founding period of their ancestral lineage authority. They emphasize the continuity of land rights and the inherent autonomy of their forebears against a shifting, distant imperial center.
- **Mainland/Academic Historians**: Academic scholarship often contextualizes these events as part of a wider trend of Southern integration during the Tang-Song transition. They highlight the tension between the expanding imperial tax state and the rise of local power structures that prioritized lineage wealth over central revenue.
