## Background
Long before the **Southern Han (南漢)** state formalized its presence in the region, the coastal waters of present-day Hong Kong—particularly around **Tai Po (大埔)**—were known for producing high-quality pearls. During the early tenth century, the region functioned as a distant frontier of the Chinese empire, characterized by maritime trade, fishing communities, and indigenous populations. As pearls grew increasingly valuable as luxury goods for court consumption and diplomatic tribute, regional authorities recognized the need for tighter administrative and military control over the hazardous pearl-diving industry.

## What happened
Around **950 CE**, amidst the fragmentation of the Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms period, the Southern Han regime formally established the **Mei-chuan Garrison (媚川都)**. This specialized military unit was created not merely for territorial defense, but specifically to oversee, militarize, and monopolize the extraction of pearls. Historical accounts suggest that the administration subjected the local divers—often referred to in historical texts as 'mermaids' or **Tan households (蛋戶)**—to brutal conditions, compelling them to dive into deep waters under armed guard. The state sought to eliminate private trading and ensure that all harvested pearls flowed directly into the imperial treasury of the Southern Han capital at **Guangzhou (廣州)**.

## Significance
The institutionalization of the pearl garrison represents a crucial transition in the history of the Hong Kong territory, shifting it from a loosely administered frontier zone into a militarized resource extraction site. While the garrison was relatively short-lived—as the Southern Han was conquered by the **Song dynasty (宋朝)** in **971 CE**—it laid an institutional precedent for subsequent dynasties to exert direct bureaucratic and military control over the region's maritime resources. It also highlights the early economic integration of the Pearl River Delta's coastal peripheries into broader imperial political economies.

## Perspectives

- **Imperial Chinese Historiography**: Traditional dynastic histories generally viewed the establishment of the garrison as a legitimate exercise of state sovereignty and economic management, focusing on the acquisition of tribute and the suppression of illicit maritime commerce.
- **Modern Scholarly and Local Community Perspective**: Contemporary historians and local heritage advocates emphasize the extreme human cost borne by indigenous divers and coastal inhabitants, viewing the garrison as an early instance of harsh state-sponsored exploitation of marginalized maritime populations.
