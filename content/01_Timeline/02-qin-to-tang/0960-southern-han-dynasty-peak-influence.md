## Background

During the tenth century, the territory encompassing present-day Hong Kong sat within the domain of the **Southern Han Dynasty** (**南漢**, 917–971), one of the Ten Kingdoms during the fragmented Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms period. While the Central Plains of China experienced continuous warfare, the Lingnan region maintained a degree of regional stability centered around Guangzhou (**廣州**). Archaeological findings and historical records suggest that the coastal areas and islands, including Tuen Mun (**屯門**), functioned as strategic maritime nodes and resource extraction zones rather than densely populated agricultural centers.

## What happened

By approximately **960**, the Southern Han state had perfected highly centralized monopolies on vital coastal commodities, most notably salt extraction and pearl diving. The warm waters of the region, particularly around the Tai Po (**大埔**) area historically known as the 'Pearl Pool' (**珠池**), were exploited by state-mandated divers to harvest freshwater and saltwater pearls for the imperial court and luxury export markets. Simultaneously, coastal salt pans produced essential revenue through government-controlled distribution networks. Military garrisons and administrative outposts were established to secure these lucrative economic lifelines against pirates and rival states, integrating the wider estuary more tightly into the Southern Han political sphere before its conquest by the expanding **Song Dynasty** (**宋朝**) in 971.

## Significance

The intensive economic exploitation during this period laid vital groundwork for subsequent Song Dynasty commercial expansion in maritime trade. By establishing institutional frameworks for salt production, pearl harvesting, and coastal defense, the Southern Han demonstrated the economic viability of the Lingnan periphery. This era marked a crucial transition where the Hong Kong region shifted from a remote frontier to an economically integrated maritime zone, setting a precedent for later commercial and administrative developments under imperial Chinese rule.

## Perspectives

- **Imperial Chinese historiography (Qing/Beijing tradition)**: Traditional dynastic histories often framed regional regimes like the Southern Han as localized, illegitimate, or extravagant kingdoms, emphasizing their eventual pacification and incorporation into the orthodox Song Dynasty order.
- **Modern regional and archaeological scholarship**: Recent historical and archaeological analyses focus on the sophisticated maritime networks and state-managed industries of the Southern Han, viewing the period as a foundational era of economic integration rather than mere peripheral isolation.
