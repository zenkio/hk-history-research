## Overview

The period from the late third century BCE to the mid-tenth century CE marked the gradual incorporation of the region now known as Hong Kong into the administrative and economic orbit of successive Chinese dynasties. This millennium-long process began with the **Qin Dynasty's Southern Expansion** (秦代南征) in **214 BCE**, which brought the Lingnan (嶺南) region under imperial purview for the first time. Following the rapid fall of the Qin, the region was integrated into the independent **Nanyue Kingdom** (南越國), founded by **Zhao Tuo** (趙佗) in **203 BCE**, which blended Han Chinese and indigenous Baiyue (百越) cultures. The **Han Conquest of Nanyue** in **111 BCE** under **Emperor Wu of Han** (漢武帝) re-established direct imperial control, initiating centuries of gradual administrative integration, punctuated by maritime trade growth and resource extraction under subsequent dynasties, including the Eastern Wu, Jin, Sui, Tang, and Southern Han.

## Key Themes

**Administrative Integration and Security**
Imperial control in the early centuries of this era was often nominal, with the region situated on the rugged edge of the empire. During the Eastern Jin Dynasty, the **Establishment of Dongguan County** (東莞郡) in **331 CE** marked a significant step in formalizing administrative control over the Pearl River Delta, including modern Hong Kong. Security remained a persistent challenge; around **411 CE**, during the Jin Dynasty, the rebel leader **Lu Xun** (盧循) utilized Lantau Island (大嶼山) as a strategic rebel base to launch operations against imperial forces. By the Sui Dynasty (581–618 CE) and Tang Dynasty (618–907 CE), administrative consolidation solidified imperial reach. This culminated in the establishment of the **Tuen Mun Military Garrison** (屯門鎮) in **736 CE** during the Tang Dynasty, tasked with defending the Pearl River estuary from pirates and securing key maritime shipping lanes.

**Economic Development: Salt, Lime, and Pearls**
The coastal geography of Hong Kong fostered specialized marine industries that became highly valued by imperial authorities. The **Emergence of the Coastal Salt Industry** (鹽業) provided a lucrative state monopoly, with salt pans operating along the coasts of Kowloon and Lantau. Simultaneously, the **Development of Coastal Lime Kilns** (石灰窑) in areas like Sai Kung (西貢) and Lantau supported regional boat-building and construction. Further north, the **Rise of Tolo Harbour Pearl Harvesting** (採珠業) in the waters of Tai Po (大埔) produced highly sought-after pearls. During the tenth century, the regional **Southern Han Kingdom** (南漢) secured the region and formalized this industry by institutionalizing the specialized **Pearl Garrison** (媚川都), deploying thousands of state-controlled divers to harvest the valuable resource as the Southern Han Dynasty reached its peak of regional influence.

**The Maritime Silk Road and Global Trade**
During the Tang Dynasty, Guangzhou emerged as one of the world's premier trading ports, positioning Hong Kong's waterways as vital corridors for the **Rise of Tang Maritime Silk Road** (海上絲綢之路). Merchant vessels laden with **Global Trade of Tang Ceramics** (唐代陶瓷), spices, and silk navigated the waters around Lantau and Tuen Mun (屯門). The strategic importance of Tuen Mun was widely recognized in contemporary literature. **Han Yu's Writings on Tuen Mun** (韓愈文集) and works by other prominent poets documented the bustling, wind-swept harbor, cementing **Tuen Mun Featured in Tang Poetry** (唐詩中的屯門) as a symbol of the empire's vibrant, far-flung maritime frontier.

## How It Shaped Hong Kong

This era laid the physical and cultural foundations of Hong Kong's identity as a maritime gateway. The **Construction of Lei Cheng Uk Tomb** (李鄭屋漢墓) during the Eastern Han period—discovered in Kowloon in 1955—provides physical evidence of Han cultural penetration and the presence of wealthy settlers or officials in the region. The security provided by imperial garrisons like Tuen Mun facilitated the **Early Migration of Northern Clans** (北方氏族南遷), who sought refuge from conflicts in northern China, bringing agricultural techniques and lineage structures that would dominate the New Territories for centuries. By the time the **Southern Han Kingdom Secures Region** (南漢割據) ended with the rise of the Song Dynasty in **960 CE**, the regional economy had transitioned from an isolated frontier inhabited by indigenous fishing communities into a highly militarized, economically diverse, and globally connected maritime threshold.

## Perspectives

- **Qing and Beijing State Historiography**: This perspective emphasizes the early, continuous integration of the region into the Chinese empire, beginning with the Qin conquests and formalized through Dongguan County (東莞郡). Archaeological evidence like the Lei Cheng Uk Tomb (李鄭屋漢墓) is presented as proof of an unbroken, two-millennium-old cultural and political bond with the Chinese mainland.
- **British Colonial Historiography**: Early colonial narratives often downplayed this era, depicting pre-1841 Hong Kong as a barren island inhabited only by fishermen and pirates. By framing the region's imperial past as one of neglect and peripheral lawlessness, this viewpoint served to legitimize British rule as the true starting point of the territory's modern development.
- **Local New Territories Indigenous Communities**: For local lineages, historical identity is rooted in the early migration of northern clans and the establishment of ancestral villages rather than distant imperial administration. They view this era through the lens of family genealogies and physical heritage, emphasizing local survival and continuity over shifting dynastic politics.
- **Modern Archaeological and Critical Scholarship**: Modern scholars view this era as a complex, fluid maritime frontier where imperial administrative control was often nominal rather than absolute. They focus on the hybridity of the region, highlighting how indigenous Baiyue (百越) cultures coexisted with and slowly adapted to expanding Han economic, military, and administrative networks.
