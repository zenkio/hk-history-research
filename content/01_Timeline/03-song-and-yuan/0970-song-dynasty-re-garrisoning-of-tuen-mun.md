## Background
During the early **Song Dynasty** (北宋), the imperial court sought to consolidate control over its southern maritime frontiers. The Pearl River Delta was emerging as a crucial artery for international trade, connecting Chinese ports with Southeast Asia and the Indian Ocean. Tuen Mun (屯門), strategically positioned at the gateway of these coastal waterways, had long served as a coastal anchorage and naval outpost, though its exact administrative footprint in the pre-Song era remains sparsely documented in surviving primary sources.

## What happened
Around **970**, the **Song court** re-garrisoned Tuen Mun, deploying military forces to the area. While comprehensive details regarding the size and permanent nature of the garrison are limited in extant records, the primary objective was clear: to suppress piracy, monitor foreign merchant vessels, and secure the vital sea lanes leading into Guangzhou (廣州), the empire's premier foreign trade port. This deployment transformed Tuen Mun from a loosely supervised coastal settlement into an active military outpost of the imperial state.

## Significance
The re-garrisoning of Tuen Mun marked an important step in the administrative and military incorporation of modern-day Hong Kong into the imperial framework. By projecting state power southward, the **Song Dynasty** laid the groundwork for the region's later development as a bustling maritime transit hub. It reflected a broader dynastic strategy that recognized maritime commerce not merely as a peripheral economic activity, but as a critical domain requiring direct military oversight and revenue extraction.

## Perspectives

- **Imperial Chinese Historiography**: Traditional Chinese accounts, largely shaped by later dynastic and Qing (清) period scholars, view the military presence at Tuen Mun as a righteous assertion of imperial sovereignty and the civilizing management of maritime borders against piracy and foreign incursions.
- **Modern and Local Scholarship**: Contemporary historians and local Hong Kong researchers tend to analyze the event through the lens of economic geography, emphasizing that military deployment was primarily driven by the imperative to protect lucrative maritime trade taxes rather than purely territorial expansionism.
