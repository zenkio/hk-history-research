## Background

During the early **Song dynasty (960–1279)**, the coastal regions of Guangdong experienced significant demographic shifts and increased integration into the imperial administrative sphere. As the imperial government sought to develop the southern coast, maritime activities including fishing, salt production, and pearl harvesting expanded rapidly. Within this environment, boat-dwelling groups long associated with the southern littoral began to form more structured and permanent economic relationships with land-based authorities and markets.

## What happened

Between **960 and 1000**, the **Tanka (蜑家)** and **Hoklo (福佬)** communities established enduring maritime and coastal settlements throughout the sheltered bays and islands of present-day Hong Kong. The **Tanka**, traditionally living entirely aboard junks and sampans, operated as specialized fishermen and boat-operators navigating local waters. Simultaneously, the **Hoklo**, migrants and maritime traders originating from coastal Fujian and eastern Guangdong, established shore-based communities and engaged in fishing, coastal trade, and navigation. These groups developed sophisticated fishing techniques tailored to local waters, utilizing the region's rich marine resources to sustain their growing populations and supply inland markets.

## Significance

This formative period marked the emergence of Hong Kong's early marine economy, transforming the archipelago from a peripheral maritime zone into an active hub of coastal enterprise. The permanent presence of the **Tanka** and **Hoklo** established a distinct maritime culture that existed alongside, yet separately from, the land-based agricultural clans that later arrived during the late Song and Ming dynasties. Their foundational role in maritime commerce, fishing, and coastal navigation shaped the demographic and economic landscape of the region for centuries to come.

## Perspectives

- **Local Hong Kong and New Territories Communities**: Oral histories and lineage traditions of the boat-dwelling populations emphasize their ancient, continuous presence in local waters, often viewing themselves as the earliest indigenous inhabitants of the coastal areas prior to the arrival of major land-based clans.
- **Imperial Chinese Historiography**: Traditional imperial records often categorized boat-dwelling populations separately from land-dwelling subjects, historically marginalizing them as outcasts or 'boat people' (danmin 蜑民) while recording their economic contributions to taxation, fishing, and pearl collection.
