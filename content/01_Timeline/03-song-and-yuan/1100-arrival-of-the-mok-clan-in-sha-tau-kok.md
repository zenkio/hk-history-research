## Background
During the **Song Dynasty (960–1279)**, the political landscape of northern China was marked by recurring instability, including the Jin–Song Wars. This volatility prompted waves of southern migration, as gentry families sought safer regions to preserve their ancestral traditions and social standing. The area now known as **Sha Tau Kok (沙頭角)**, situated on the border of modern-day Hong Kong and mainland China, became an attractive destination due to its coastal resources and potential for agricultural development.

## What happened
Historical records and clan genealogies suggest that the **Mok (莫)** clan arrived in the **Sha Tau Kok** area between **1100 and 1150**. While exact dates of arrival are difficult to pinpoint with archaeological certainty, the establishment of the clan in this locale is widely associated with this period of the Northern and Southern Song transition. The clan reportedly integrated into the local economy by engaging in land cultivation and fishing. Their arrival contributed to the development of the broader New Territories, which were becoming an increasingly populated frontier for migrants from the north.

## Significance
The settlement of the **Mok** family is viewed as a microcosmic example of the 'gentry migration' phenomenon. By establishing permanent dwellings and genealogical records, the clan helped solidify the lineage-based social structure that defined rural Hong Kong for centuries. This era of migration was critical in transforming the region from a loosely populated maritime outpost into a more structured agricultural society integrated with broader Guangdong lineage networks. Their presence underscores the continuous demographic exchange between the Pearl River Delta and the northern interior, a process that arguably shaped the cultural heritage of the New Territories.

## Perspectives

- **Local New Territories Lineage Perspective**: Local clan members often view their ancestors' arrival as an act of deliberate settlement, preserving ancestral rites and historical continuity despite changing dynastic regimes. Genealogies are considered sacred documents that provide historical legitimacy and territorial rights passed down through generations.
- **Academic/Historian Perspective**: Modern scholars note that while clan records (zupu) are valuable, they may contain retrospective additions intended to bolster the clan's prestige. Consequently, historians interpret these dates as approximate markers of a longer, more gradual process of regional integration rather than a singular, definitive event.
