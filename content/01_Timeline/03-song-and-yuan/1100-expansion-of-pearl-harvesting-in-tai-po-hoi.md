## Background
During the Northern Song dynasty (**960–1127**), the imperial court's demand for luxury goods, particularly pearls, led to the systematic exploitation of coastal resources in the south. The waters of Tai Po Hoi (大埔海), also known as Tolo Harbour, were identified as a rich source of high-quality South Sea pearls. While sporadic gathering had occurred earlier, **c. 1100** marked a significant administrative and operational expansion of the industry in the region to supply the court.

## What happened
The imperial government established official oversight of the pearl harvesting grounds in Tai Po Hoi. Local villagers and specialized divers, often referred to in historical texts as 'merfolk' or pearl divers, were conscripted or driven by economic necessity to harvest oysters. These operations were notoriously hazardous. Divers operated under extreme physical pressure, often descending to dangerous depths without modern equipment, facing threats from drowning, exposure, and dangerous marine life. Historical accounts suggest that the intensity of the harvesting led to severe overfishing and exhaustion of the local pearl oyster stocks.

## Significance
The expansion of pearl harvesting integrated the Hong Kong region more closely into the economic and administrative network of the Song empire. It transformed Tai Po Hoi into a prominent economic node, though at a heavy human cost to the local coastal communities. The depletion of the pearl beds eventually led to the decline of the industry in later dynasties, leaving a legacy preserved in local folklore and historical gazetteers regarding the hardships of the pearl divers.

## Perspectives

- **Imperial Chinese Historians and Officials**: Viewed the expansion through the lens of tribute and economic resource extraction, emphasizing the loyalty of the region in providing rare luxuries for the emperor. Official records tended to focus on yields, administrative quotas, and the regulation of the trade.
- **Local Hong Kong and New Territories Communities**: Remember the era through oral traditions and folklore emphasizing the extreme suffering, exploitation, and mortal danger faced by the local divers forced to harvest pearls under harsh imperial quotas.
