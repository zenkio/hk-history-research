## Background
During the Song dynasty (宋朝), southern China experienced substantial demographic growth driven by migration from the north. As the population of the Pearl River Delta expanded, local communities sought agricultural innovations to secure reliable food supplies. The terrain of present-day Hong Kong, characterized by steep hills and limited flat alluvial plains, presented continuous challenges for traditional farming.

## What happened
Around **1100**, farmers in the New Territories (新界) increasingly adopted Champa rice (占城稻), a drought-resistant and early-ripening rice variety originally from Champa in modern-day central Vietnam. Unlike traditional regional varieties, Champa rice matured rapidly and required less water, allowing farming households to harvest two crops per year from the same plots of land—a practice known as double-cropping.

## Significance
The shift to double-cropping fundamentally altered the rural economy of the region. Higher agricultural yields supported larger populations, facilitated the stabilization of permanent settlements, and contributed to the rise of powerful local clans. This technological advancement laid the foundation for the agrarian society that sustained much of the New Territories for centuries to come.

## Perspectives

- **Local New Territories Communities**: Indigenous lineages often view these foundational agricultural practices as the bedrock of their long-standing heritage and ancestral ties to the land, preserving memories of intensive farming techniques through generations.
- **Modern Historiography**: Recent scholarship emphasizes that the adoption of Champa rice was part of a broader economic integration of the Lingnan region into the wider Song dynasty empire rather than an isolated local event.
