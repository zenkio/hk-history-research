## Background
During the **Northern Song dynasty (960–1127)**, the coastal areas of modern-day Hong Kong and the New Territories experienced gradual population growth driven by migration from the Central Plains. The nutrient-rich mudflats and brackish waters of **Deep Bay (後海灣)**, where the Pearl River delta meets the sea, provided ideal natural conditions for marine life and mollusk growth. Local coastal villagers and fishermen sought ways to supplement their livelihoods beyond traditional fishing and salt production.

## What happened
Sometime around **1110–1120**, local residents in the Deep Bay area began experimenting with cultivating oysters systematically rather than merely harvesting wild populations. Historical oral traditions and local genealogies suggest that villagers laid stones, wooden stakes, and tile shards into the intertidal mudflats to capture naturally floating oyster larvae. This method allowed the spat to grow securely in the nutrient-rich tidal zone. Over time, this technique evolved into a sophisticated form of aquaculture, with families passing down specialized knowledge regarding water salinity, seabed conditions, and optimal harvesting cycles across generations.

## Significance
The introduction of oyster farming transformed Deep Bay into one of the region's most economically vital coastal zones. The industry provided a reliable, lucrative staple commodity—dried and fresh oysters—that could be traded throughout Guangdong and beyond. It established a pattern of intensive maritime resource utilization that shaped the social structure, economy, and settlement patterns of the northwestern New Territories for nearly a millennium, laying the groundwork for cooperative clan-based coastal management systems that persisted well into the modern era.

## Perspectives

- **Local New Territories Communities**: Folk history and clan archives emphasize that the early oyster farming techniques were the ingenious invention of indigenous villagers who adapted to the harsh coastal environment through collective labor and meticulous observation of the tides.
- **Later Scholarship and Economic Historians**: Modern academic research suggests that the rise of Deep Bay aquaculture was part of a broader southern Chinese economic boom during the Song dynasty, driven by expanding regional markets for luxury and preserved foodstuffs rather than isolated local innovation alone.
