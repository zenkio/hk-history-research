## Background
During the **Song Dynasty (960–1279)**, the coastal regions of the Pearl River Delta began to see increased economic integration with the Chinese heartland. While Tuen Mun (屯門) had long served as a vital maritime anchorage and defensive outpost for the imperial government, the mid-12th century marked a transition in the social landscape. As the salt production industry matured and maritime trade routes expanded, the region began to support more complex social and religious infrastructures. By approximately **1130**, historical records and oral traditions suggest the emergence of formal religious institutions in the area.

## What happened
The development of early Buddhist sites in Tuen Mun was likely tied to the arrival of settlers and maritime travelers seeking religious refuge or spiritual guidance. While precise architectural records from this period are scarce, scholars note that the construction of temples served as a nexus for communal life. These structures often functioned as meeting places for local clans and a waypoint for traders navigating the waters between Guangzhou (廣州) and the South China Sea. The establishment of these temples suggests a degree of economic stability, as the maintenance of such buildings required the support of a sedentary population capable of producing agricultural or trade surpluses.

## Significance
The construction of these temples is widely interpreted as a manifestation of the Sinicization of the coastal frontier. It highlights the integration of Hong Kong’s coastal territories into the broader cultural sphere of the Song state. The temples provided a physical anchor for clans migrating southward, facilitating the formation of lineage-based communities that would define the New Territories' social structure for centuries. Furthermore, the presence of these sites underscores the importance of the Tuen Mun corridor as a maritime gateway, blending the logistical requirements of a naval outpost with the cultural needs of a developing local society.
