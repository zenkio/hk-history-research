## Background
During the Song dynasty (960–1279), the northern frontier of present-day Hong Kong experienced significant demographic shifts as mainland clans migrated southward to escape political instability, taxation pressures, and social turmoil in central China. The Hau clan (侯氏), originating from central and northern provinces before settling in Guangdong, was among the prominent lineages that participated in this southward movement. While precise genealogical records from the period are often shaped by later lineage compilation practices, oral traditions and ancestral halls trace the clan's arrival in the region to the mid-to-late 12th century.

Selecting a site near fertile alluvial plains suited for wet-rice agriculture, the settlers cleared the land, built ancestral shrines, and organized communal labor systems. Over succeeding generations, the Hau clan expanded their agricultural holdings, constructed fortified walled villages, and established economic networks with neighboring communities, gradually transforming Ho Sheung Heung into a thriving rural hub.

The enduring presence of the Hau lineage highlights the complex interplay between local clan histories and the wider administrative changes of the Song and Yuan dynasties.
