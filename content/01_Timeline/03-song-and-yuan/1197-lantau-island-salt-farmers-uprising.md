## Background
During the Song dynasty (960–1279), the coastal regions of modern-day Hong Kong, including Lantau Island (大嶼山), were important centers for salt production and pearl harvesting. To secure revenue, the imperial government established strict monopolies over the production and distribution of salt, treating unauthorized trade as criminal smuggling. By the late 12th century, these heavy-handed economic controls and enforcement measures generated severe economic hardship and resentment among local salt farmers and producers.

## What happened
In **1197** (the fourth year of the **Qingyuan** (慶元) reign), simmering discontent boiled over into open rebellion. Salt farmers on Lantau Island rose up against the Song authorities in defiance of the government's anti-smuggling monopolies. In response to the insurgency, the imperial court deployed military forces to suppress the uprising. The rebellion was met with violent military force, resulting in the subjugation of the salt producers and the re-establishment of state control over the region's lucrative maritime industries.

## Significance
The Lantau Island salt farmers' uprising is one of the earliest recorded localized rebellions against imperial economic policies within the territory that would become Hong Kong. It highlights the crucial economic role of coastal industries during the Song period, as well as the inherent tensions between peripheral maritime communities and central dynastic authority. While the uprising was decisively crushed, it remains a notable event in local historiography, illustrating the social instability caused by state monopolies in the Pearl River Delta region.

## Perspectives

- **Imperial Song and Traditional Historiography**: Official dynastic records generally framed the uprising as a criminal rebellion and a disturbance of public order by lawless salt smugglers, justifying stern military intervention to maintain state monopolies and fiscal security.
- **Local New Territories and Regional Communities**: Later local perspectives and regional folklore often viewed the salt producers not as common criminals, but as hardworking laborers driven to desperation by oppressive taxation and heavy-handed government interference in their traditional livelihoods.
