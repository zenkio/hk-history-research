## Background
During the **Southern Song Dynasty (南宋)** (1127–1279), Northern China faced persistent political instability, triggering significant southward migrations of Han Chinese clans. Many of these families settled in the coastal regions of the Pearl River Delta, including the area now known as the New Territories (新界). These clans, often fleeing the pressures of northern conflict, brought with them not only ancestral traditions but also advanced agrarian expertise developed in the Yangtze River valley.

## What happened
Between **1200 and 1250**, established clans such as the **Tang (鄧)**, **Hau (侯)**, **Pang (彭)**, **Liu (廖)**, and **Man (文)** settled in various parts of the region. These groups introduced systematic wet-rice cultivation, which required complex water management infrastructure. They constructed sophisticated hydraulic systems, including embankments, sluice gates, and irrigation channels, to reclaim low-lying coastal marshes for paddy fields. This process often involved the transformation of tidal flats into productive land, a technique that required the collective labor and organization of lineage-based societies.

## Significance
The introduction of these techniques marked a pivotal shift in the local economy. By stabilizing the food supply, these agricultural innovations supported a significant increase in the local population. The land, once utilized primarily for salt production (鹽業) and pearling, became increasingly characterized by sedentary, rice-based agriculture. This period laid the structural foundation for the lineage society that would dominate the New Territories for the next seven centuries, creating enduring social and land-ownership patterns. Furthermore, the reliance on collective irrigation management strengthened the communal ties within the clans, who maintained these systems as part of their ancestral holdings.

## Perspectives

- **Local New Territories Clans**: The clans emphasize these developments as a testament to their civilizing influence and ancestral diligence in transforming the landscape. They view the establishment of ancestral halls and the associated land management systems as the bedrock of their long-standing tenure in the region.
- **Later Academic Scholarship**: Historians often contrast this narrative with the earlier reliance on maritime trade and salt production, questioning the degree to which these clans displaced or integrated with indigenous peoples already present in the region. There is ongoing debate regarding the extent to which these techniques were innovations versus regional adaptations of existing practices.
