## Background
By the **13th century**, the **Song Dynasty (宋朝)** had established a sophisticated maritime trade network. While the regional center was Guangzhou, the islands at the mouth of the Pearl River, particularly **Lantau Island (大嶼山)**, were becoming strategically important. Historically, these areas were peripheral, populated by sea-dwelling communities and itinerant workers involved in coastal resource extraction. The central government sought to tighten its fiscal control over the region to ensure that the production of salt and lime was taxed appropriately rather than operating as informal trade.

## What happened
Around **1200**, there is evidence of increased administrative attention directed toward Lantau. Historical records from the **Song Shi (宋史)** and local gazetteers suggest the establishment of regulatory oversight mechanisms, potentially linked to the salt monopoly. Archaeologists have identified kiln sites and hearths dating to this era, which indicate that the production of salt—essential for preserving fish and seasoning food—and lime, used for caulking wooden vessels, had become large-scale, semi-industrial activities. The administration dispatched officials to monitor these production centers, aiming to curb smuggling and ensure that the imperial treasury received its dues from the lucrative island economy.

## Significance
The integration of Lantau into the Song administrative apparatus marks the transition of the region from a loosely governed frontier to a taxable territory. This period reflects the broader Song policy of "active maritime management," where the state projected its power not just through military garrisons, but through the regulation of essential commodities. The presence of official salt-tax administration served as a precursor to the more formalized coastal defense systems that would appear in the later **Yuan (元朝)** and **Ming (明朝)** dynasties, permanently altering the demographic and economic landscape of the Hong Kong region.

## Perspectives

- **Local New Territories Clans**: Many local clans view this era as the beginning of their ancestral land management, interpreting early administrative presence as the start of a long-standing tradition of local land stewardship. They often emphasize their role in developing these islands through maritime trade and local industry prior to formal state arrival.
- **Academic/Archaeological Perspective**: Modern historians and archaeologists emphasize that the Song government's presence was primarily fiscal and reactive. They argue that the state was interested in extraction rather than infrastructure, and that the 'integration' was likely incomplete and subject to constant negotiation with local maritime populations.
