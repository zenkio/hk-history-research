## Background
During the **Southern Song dynasty (1127–1279)**, the waters surrounding Hong Kong, particularly the **Tolo Harbour (大埔海/吐露港)** region, became renowned for the production of high-quality pearls. Known as the 'Pool of Pearls' (珠池), this area was a vital source of revenue for the imperial court and local coastal communities. The pearls harvested here were highly prized by the aristocracy and were often sought as tribute to the capital.

## What happened
By approximately **1220**, the intensity of pearl diving led to concerns regarding the sustainability of the oyster beds. To prevent the depletion of the resource, local officials and the imperial administration introduced a system of seasonal harvesting bans. Under this regulatory framework, diving was restricted during certain months of the year, allowing the **pearl oyster (珍珠蠔)** populations to spawn and mature without human interference. This intervention represents one of the earliest documented attempts at marine resource management in the region.

## Significance
The implementation of these conservation practices highlights the economic importance of the **Tai Po (大埔)** pearl industry during the 13th century. It suggests a sophisticated understanding of local marine ecosystems by Song dynasty officials, who balanced the immediate demand for luxury goods with the long-term survival of the pearl industry. While the industry eventually declined, partly due to over-exploitation and shifting imperial priorities, these measures provide a window into the administrative oversight of the period. Historians note that this period of regulation underscores the integration of the Hong Kong region into the broader economic and administrative structures of the Song empire, moving beyond its traditional role as a mere maritime outpost.

## Perspectives

- **Qing and Imperial Beijing Perspective**: Traditional records from this era often emphasize the industry as a vital source of imperial tribute, framing the conservation measures as prudent statecraft to ensure the continued supply of prestige goods to the throne.
- **Local New Territories Community Perspective**: Local oral histories often frame these activities within the context of the hardships faced by the 'pearl divers' (珠民), suggesting that government regulations were primarily designed to protect imperial assets rather than the welfare or safety of the divers themselves.
