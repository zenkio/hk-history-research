## Background
During the **13th century**, specifically between **1250 and 1300**, the Hong Kong region experienced a significant shift in land settlement. As the Southern Song Dynasty (960–1279) declined and the Yuan Dynasty (1271–1368) rose, migration from mainland China—particularly from the Jiangxi and Guangdong regions—intensified. Prominent lineages, known later as the **Five Great Clans** (五大氏族), began to solidify their presence in the New Territories. These clans brought with them sophisticated systems of land management, ancestral worship, and, crucially, the practice of **Feng Shui** (風水), or geomancy, which dictated how to harmonize living spaces with the natural environment.

## What happened
The development of village layouts during this period was rarely random. Villages were meticulously sited to maximize the flow of *qi* (energy), typically following the 'back to the mountain, face to the water' principle. This required the selection of a location with a protected 'dragon' ridge behind the village and a clear, open vista toward water or agricultural land at the front. 

Architectural organization became hierarchical. The placement of the **ancestral hall** (宗祠) became the anchor of the village, serving as the physical and symbolic center for clan identity and governance. Houses were often constructed in rows facing the same direction, reflecting a highly disciplined social order. By the late 13th century, these spatial arrangements were deeply integrated into the management of communal irrigation, pond systems, and defensive structures, which were essential for surviving in the coastal environment.

## Significance
The application of these geomantic principles played a vital role in the resilience of the Five Great Clans. By standardizing village design, these communities were able to maintain internal social cohesion and territorial legitimacy. This landscape-based organization allowed clans to define their boundaries clearly, which became a foundation for the land-holding patterns that persisted in the New Territories for centuries. The permanence of these stone-and-mortar layouts helped these clans survive the political transitions of the era, establishing a permanent heritage that shaped the demographic landscape of Hong Kong.

While the exact extent to which 13th-century layout practices were later modified by colonial-era surveys or Qing-dynasty tax records remains a subject of ongoing historical investigation, the core structural archetypes established during this formative period remain central to the study of Southern Chinese agrarian settlement.

## Perspectives

- **New Territories Indigenous Clans**: Local communities view these village layouts as essential cultural inheritance, emphasizing that geomantic planning was a spiritual and practical necessity to ensure ancestral prosperity and community harmony.
- **Academic and Colonial Historians**: Later scholarship often debates how much of the extant village architecture reflects original 13th-century designs versus later Qing-era reconstruction, while colonial administrators often viewed these layout patterns primarily as markers of land ownership and administrative territoriality.
