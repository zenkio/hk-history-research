## Background
By the mid-**13th century**, the region of **Yuen Long (元朗)** and the broader **New Territories (新界)** had become increasingly attractive to migratory clans from mainland China. These groups, often fleeing instability or seeking fertile land, encountered a landscape characterized by low-lying coastal marshes and tidal flats. While early settlers had utilized these areas for salt production and pearl harvesting, the shift toward permanent agriculture necessitated large-scale environmental engineering.

## What happened
Around **1250**, local clans began the systematic reclamation of wetlands. This process involved the construction of elaborate dike systems (known locally as *wai* or 圍) to prevent saltwater inundation and manage irrigation for rice paddies. By channeling freshwater streams and clearing mangroves, these groups converted flood-prone marshlands into stable, fertile fields. This was a labor-intensive process requiring the collective effort of entire lineages, which enforced communal land ownership and social hierarchy. The transformation of the environment was not merely a subsistence strategy; it provided the surplus grain necessary to support larger populations and sustain the ancestral halls that would become the cultural heart of these clans.

## Significance
The success of this reclamation effort provided the economic base for the long-term settlement of the five major clans: the **Tang (鄧)**, **Hau (侯)**, **Pang (彭)**, **Liu (廖)**, and **Man (文)**. The resulting agricultural landscape allowed for the development of sophisticated lineage governance. These clans institutionalized land rights through ancestral trusts, creating a land tenure system that remained largely intact through the **Qing dynasty (清朝)** and significantly influenced later interactions with the British colonial administration. The permanence of these landholdings facilitated the construction of schools and shrines, cementing the clans' status as local gentry.

## Perspectives

- **Local New Territories Lineages**: Local clans emphasize these reclamation efforts as foundational acts of 'civilization' and ancestral perseverance. They view the lineage-controlled land as a traditional, autonomous system that predates modern state structures and warrants protection under the principle of historical land rights.
- **Colonial-era Historiography**: Earlier colonial scholarship often framed these developments as 'frontier expansion,' characterizing the clans as pioneers who brought order to a wild, unmanaged landscape. This narrative frequently served to legitimize the transition of land rights during the 1898 lease of the New Territories.
