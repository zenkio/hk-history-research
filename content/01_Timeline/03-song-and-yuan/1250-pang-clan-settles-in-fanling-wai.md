## Background
During the Song dynasty (960–1279), the region that is now the New Territories of Hong Kong experienced significant migration from the Central Plains of China. Lineage groups moved southward to escape northern unrest and political instability. Among these arriving families was the Pang clan (彭氏), who sought arable land and security in the fertile river valleys of the southern coastal frontier.

## What happened
Around **c. 1250**, members of the Pang family established their ancestral home in **Fanling Wai (粉嶺圍)**. Historical tradition and localized genealogies suggest that the clan cleared the land for wet-rice cultivation, organized communal labor, and gradually constructed a walled village structure for defense against bandits and rival lineages. Over subsequent centuries, the settlement evolved into a close-knit agricultural community, maintaining ancestral halls, ritual traditions, and localized irrigation networks that sustained their lineage through both the Yuan and Ming dynasties.

## Significance
The settlement of the Pang clan at Fanling Wai represents a key chapter in the establishment of the 'Five Great Clans' (五大氏族) system within the New Territories. These early lineage foundations shaped the rural social structure, land tenure systems, and localized governance patterns of the region for centuries, enduring through dynastic transitions, British colonial administration, and modern urban development.

## Perspectives

- **Local New Territories Lineage Tradition**: Oral histories and written lineage books (族譜) emphasize continuous settlement, ancestral continuity, and deep-rooted indigenous rights to the land dating back to the Song dynasty.
- **Later Historiography and Scholarship**: Modern historians note that while lineage origins are traditionally traced to the Song period, many of the physical structures, walled defenses, and formal documentation were heavily shaped or reconstructed during the Qing dynasty in response to regional insecurity.
