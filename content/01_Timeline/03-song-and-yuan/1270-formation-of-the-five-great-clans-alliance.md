## Background
By the mid-13th century, the coastal regions of the Pearl River Delta were increasingly integrated into the imperial administrative framework of the **Song Dynasty (960–1279)**. As maritime trade and salt production expanded, waves of migration from mainland provinces such as Guangdong and Jiangxi brought prominent scholarly families to the area now known as the New Territories. These lineages sought to secure fertile agricultural land and manage irrigation resources, which necessitated a high degree of communal organization.

## What happened
Around **1270**, the lineages—specifically the **Tang (鄧)**, **Hau (侯)**, **Pang (彭)**, **Liu (廖)**, and **Man (文)**—began to formalize a collaborative structure often referred to as the Five Great Clans (五大氏族). While the term "alliance" is a later historical construct used to describe their cooperative dominance, these groups utilized common ancestral worship, land deeds, and intermarriage to consolidate influence over the local landscape. By controlling key agrarian hubs near **Tuen Mun (屯門)** and the northern plains, these families functioned as the primary intermediaries between the imperial tax collectors and the local tenant farmers. This consolidation occurred against a backdrop of increasing political instability as the **Yuan Dynasty (1271–1368)** approached and the Song court retreated southward through the region.

## Significance
The formation of this structure established a lineage-based governance model that remained robust through the Ming and Qing dynasties. By creating a unified front, these clans were able to defend their landholdings against bandits and negotiate favorable terms with regional officials. This socio-political arrangement ensured that the New Territories operated as a semi-autonomous rural society where the power of the central state was mediated through the patriarchs of the five clans. This legacy persists today in the form of ancestral halls and the customary land rights preserved within the village representative system, serving as a pillar of local cultural identity.

## Perspectives

- **Colonial-era British ethnographers**: Early British administrators often interpreted these clan structures as static, ancient feudal holdovers, emphasizing their role in maintaining social order to justify indirect rule via the New Territories' village heads.
- **Local New Territories communities**: For the descendants of these clans, the alliance is viewed through the lens of lineage continuity and the successful preservation of ancestral land rights against external pressures and government encroachment over the last seven centuries.
