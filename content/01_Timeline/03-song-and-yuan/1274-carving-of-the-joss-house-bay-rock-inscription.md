## Background
During the Song dynasty (960–1274), the coastal areas of modern-day Hong Kong saw increasing maritime trade, salt production, and settlement. Seafarers and coastal communities worshipped local deities for protection against unpredictable maritime hazards, leading to the early establishment of coastal shrines dedicated to **Tin Hau** (天后), the Queen of Heaven and patron saint of fishermen and sailors.

## What happened
In **1274**, during the Southern Song dynasty, an official or literate local patron recorded the renovation of a Tin Hau temple located at Joss House Bay, also known as **Tai Miu Wan** (大廟灣) on the **Clear Water Bay Peninsula** (清水灣半島). The text was carved directly into the coastal rock face. The inscription, consisting of over a hundred characters written in regular script, documents the repair work and provides a rare, concrete anchor point for the chronology of human activity in the region during the thirteenth century.

## Significance
As Hong Kong's oldest surviving dated historical relic, the Joss House Bay Rock Inscription holds immense value for historians and archaeologists studying the medieval period of the Pearl River Delta. It bridges the gap between legendary accounts of early settlement and verifiable epigraphic evidence. The site continues to be associated with traditional coastal religious practices, linking modern festival celebrations directly to medieval maritime traditions.

## Perspectives

- **Local Hong Kong and New Territories Communities**: For generations of coastal villagers and fisherfolk, the inscription and the adjacent Tai Miu temple represent enduring spiritual lineage and communal continuity, validating their deep-rooted history in the region long before British colonization.
- **Later Scholarship and Antiquities Authorities**: Archaeologists and heritage preservationists view the inscription as a priceless primary epigraphic source that anchors the region's chronology, prompting formal legal protection as a declared monument to safeguard it from weathering and urban encroachment.
