## Background
During the **Song Dynasty (960–1279)**, the coastal regions of the Pearl River Delta began to see increased economic activity. While the hinterlands were increasingly settled by lineages migrating from the Central Plains, the coastal fringes and sheltered bays remained the domain of populations whose livelihoods were tied to the sea. The **Tanka (疍家)** people, who historically relied on fishing, maritime trade, and pearl diving, emerged as a specialized social group during this period. The geography of the region—with its numerous islands and protected harbors—facilitated a lifestyle centered on boat-dwelling.

## What happened
By approximately **1275**, as the Southern Song faced collapse, the demographic differentiation between the land-based clans (who focused on rice cultivation and lineage building) and the sea-faring Tanka (who lived on sampans and larger junks) had solidified into a dual-layered social structure. The Tanka served as the essential maritime infrastructure of the era, providing transport and marine resources to the coastal settlements. Their society was highly mobile, organized around family-based boat units rather than the ancestral halls and landed property characteristic of the inland New Territories clans. While records from this time are fragmented, it is widely accepted that the Tanka maintained a distinct cultural, linguistic, and economic sphere that rarely overlapped with the landed gentry of areas such as **Tuen Mun (屯門)** or **Tai Po (大埔)**.

## Significance
The rise of the Tanka established a socio-economic dichotomy that would characterize the region for centuries. This structure defined Hong Kong as a hybrid space where land-based agrarian society and maritime commercial society coexisted, yet remained largely segregated. The Tanka's mastery of the local waters proved critical for trade and survival during periods of upheaval, such as the final years of the **Yuan Dynasty (1271–1368)**, when the imperial court and loyalist forces retreated through the islands. This maritime history challenges traditional historical narratives that prioritize land-based lineage development, emphasizing instead the vital role of mobile, sea-based communities in the coastal economy.

## Perspectives

- **Local New Territories Lineage Communities**: Traditional clan narratives often emphasize the history of land reclamation and the establishment of ancestral halls as the primary markers of civilization. From this perspective, the Tanka were viewed as a peripheral 'other' with different customs, occupying a social status that was historically distinct from the land-owning lineages.
- **Modern Maritime Historians**: Contemporary scholarship frequently rejects the colonial-era characterization of the Tanka as 'marginal.' Instead, these researchers argue that the Tanka were the essential economic engine of the Pearl River Delta, possessing a specialized knowledge of maritime navigation and local ecology that was vital to the prosperity of the region.
