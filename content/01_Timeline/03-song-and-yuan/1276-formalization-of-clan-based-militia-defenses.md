## Background

By the mid-13th century, the coastal regions of the Pearl River Delta, including modern-day Hong Kong, were experiencing significant socio-political shifts. The expansion of salt production and pearl harvesting had led to the growth of wealthy, land-owning clans. As the **Southern Song (南宋)** dynasty weakened under the pressure of the **Mongol (元)** advance, central authority in the Lingnan region began to fracture. The arrival of the fleeing Song court and the Battle of Yamen brought the conflict to the doorstep of the local populace.

## What happened

Between **1276 and 1279**, the threat of Mongol incursions and the breakdown of imperial governance forced established families to seek self-reliance. Historically, many of the 'Five Great Clans' of the New Territories, such as the **Tang (鄧)**, **Hau (侯)**, and **Pang (彭)**, began to formalize their security arrangements. This involved the recruitment and training of local villagers into militia units. These militias were tasked with patrolling ancestral lands, securing storage facilities, and protecting coastal trade routes that had become vulnerable to pirates and opportunistic raids. While the extent of their military engagement against the Mongol armies remains a subject of historical debate, it is widely accepted that the structure of these defensive units allowed these clans to maintain local order when formal administrative structures were effectively absent.

## Significance

This period marks the genesis of the autonomous village security systems that characterized the New Territories for centuries. The formalization of these militias established a precedent for clan leadership, where heads of lineages acted as both landowners and military organizers. This dual role strengthened the influence of the lineage, allowing these clans to negotiate their position as the dominant socio-political actors in the region well into the Qing dynasty. Consequently, these systems ensured that, even under changing dynasties, the fundamental structure of local governance in the New Territories remained decentralized and rooted in clan authority.

## Perspectives

- **Local New Territories Lineage Tradition**: Many local clans view these militias as essential acts of patriotic defiance against invading forces, emphasizing their role in protecting their ancestral heritage and the remnants of the Song royalty. This perspective highlights the continuity of clan identity and their long-standing claim to land ownership in the region.
- **Academic and State Historiography**: Historians often interpret these defensive formations through the lens of pragmatic survivalism rather than strictly ideological loyalty. Some scholars note that while these militias served as local defense, they also functioned to protect the clan's economic interests, such as salt works and agricultural output, during a period of extreme administrative chaos.
