## Background
In the late thirteenth century, the advancing armies of the Mongol Empire exerted immense military pressure on the declining Southern Song dynasty (**960–1279**). Following the fall of the capital **Lin'an** (臨安, modern Hangzhou), loyalist officials and surviving members of the imperial family fled southward by sea. By **May 1277**, the young child-emperors **Emperor Duanzong** (宋端宗) and later **Emperor Bing** (宋帝昺), accompanied by officials such as **Lu Xiufu** (陸秀夫) and **Zhang Shijie** (張世傑), sought refuge along the jagged and isolated coastline of the Pearl River Delta.

## What happened
Historical records indicate that the exiled court set up temporary headquarters and an imperial residence at a prominent elevation known as Sacred Hill (**Sung Wong Toi**, 宋王臺) in the area of modern-day Kowloon City. While the exact physical scale and architecture of this temporary court remain uncertain due to a lack of contemporary archaeological remains, the location served as a vital administrative and strategic rallying point for the loyalist resistance. The court's presence in the region was brief, as relentless Mongol pursuits forced them to move further south to **Mui Wo** (梅窩) and eventually to their final, tragic defeat at the **Battle of Yamen** (崖門之役) in **1279**.

## Significance
The establishment of the imperial residence at Sacred Hill cemented the territory's symbolic role in Chinese dynastic history. For centuries, the memory of the loyalist court was preserved by local villagers and gentry. The most tangible commemoration of this era is the massive granite boulder inscribed with the characters "Sung Wong Toi" (Song Wong Toi, meaning "Terrace of the Song Kings"), which was carved into the hill. Although a significant portion of the original rock was blasted away during quarrying works in the colonial period, the surviving fragment was relocated to **Sung Wong Toi Garden** (宋王臺花園), serving as a permanent monument to the final chapters of the Southern Song dynasty.

## Perspectives

- **Qing and Beijing historiography**: Traditional and official Chinese historiography frames the episode through the lens of loyalty, martyrdom, and orthodox dynastic resistance, highlighting officials like Lu Xiufu as paragons of Confucian virtue.
- **Local New Territories clans**: Many indigenous lineages trace their settlement or ancestral prominence in the region to this turbulent transitional period, viewing the court's presence as a foundational moment in local clan history.
- **British colonial administration**: Colonial-era authorities approached the site primarily as an antiquarian curiosity, eventually preserving the surviving inscribed stone fragment during the expansion of the Kai Tak aviation facilities.
