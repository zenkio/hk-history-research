## Background

During the late 13th century, the advancing armies of the Mongol Empire pressed relentlessly southward against the Southern Song dynasty (南宋). By **1277**, the remnants of the Song imperial court, centered around young boy-emperors and loyalist officials, were forced to flee by sea. Seeking refuge along the irregular, island-studded coastline of the Lingnan region, the court eventually entered the waters of present-day Hong Kong. 

## What happened

The retreating royals, including **Emperor Duanzong** (宋端宗) and officials like **Zhang Shijie** (張世傑) and **Wen Tianxiang** (文天祥), found themselves in dire need of basic provisions, local knowledge of navigation, and safe hiding places amidst the labyrinth of bays and islands. According to both local oral traditions and historical records, indigenous boat people (often identified with the Tanka or local coastal villagers) stepped in to aid the court. They shared their meager food supplies, offered intelligence on Mongol troop movements, and provided shelter in secluded coastal inlets. 

## Significance

This episode marks one of the earliest documented instances where the inhabitants of the Hong Kong region played a direct role in major dynastic events of Chinese history. The assistance prolonged the resistance of the Southern Song loyalists, even though their cause ultimately ended in defeat at the Battle of Yamen in **1279**. For centuries following, local communities and later lineage clans in the New Territories drew upon the memory of this loyalist presence to shape their regional identity and demonstrate their orthodox Confucian heritage.

- **Local New Territories Lineage Communities**: Descendants of early clans and local inhabitants have traditionally emphasized their forebears' loyalty to the fallen Chinese dynasty, viewing the era as a foundational moment that legitimized their ancestral roots and cultural alignment with central Han civilization.
