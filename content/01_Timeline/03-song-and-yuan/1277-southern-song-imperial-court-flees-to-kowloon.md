## Background
By the late 1270s, the **Southern Song dynasty** (南宋) faced imminent collapse under the relentless military campaigns of the expanding **Yuan dynasty** (元朝) led by Kublai Khan. Following the fall of the imperial capital of Lin'an (modern-day Hangzhou) in 1276, surviving officials and loyalists scrambled to preserve the dynasty. Under the leadership of key officials such as **Chen宜中** (Chen Yizhong) and **Zhang Shijie** (張世傑), the royal family took to the sea and southern coastal routes to establish a mobile court and continue resistance.

## What happened
In **April 1277** (corresponding to the 4th lunar month of the 13th year of Xianchun/1st year of Jingyan), the imperial entourage, centered around the young child-emperor **Emperor Duanzong** (宋端宗, personal name Zhao Shi 趙昰) and his brother Prince Zhao Bing (趙昺), arrived in the coastal regions of present-day Hong Kong. They sought temporary refuge in Kowloon Bay (九龍灣) and established temporary administrative quarters, traditionally associated by some local oral histories with locations like Sung Wong Toi (宋王臺). The court attempted to rally loyalist forces and secure local supplies from salt pans and fishing communities, though their stay was constantly threatened by approaching Mongol forces.

## Significance
While the court's presence in the territory was brief and marked by military desperation rather than permanent governance, it deeply embedded the region into the dynastic memory of the fall of the Song. The events surrounding the court's flight through the Pearl River Delta eventually inspired numerous local legends, historical shrines, and monuments, symbolizing ultimate loyalty (忠義) to a dying regime in the face of foreign conquest.

## Perspectives

- **Local Hong Kong and New Territories Communities**: Local lineages and oral traditions often emphasize the benevolence and tragic heroism of the fleeing court, preserving memories through clan genealogies, placenames, and shrines dedicated to loyalists who protected the young emperors.
- **Qing and Imperial Historiography**: Later imperial historians, writing under the succeeding dynasties, typically framed the era through the lens of dynastic transition and orthodox loyalty (忠), validating the resistance of loyalist officials while acknowledging the mandate of heaven shifting to the Yuan.
- **Modern Scholarship**: Modern historians analyze the episode as part of a desperate maritime strategy by a failing regime, highlighting the economic and strategic importance of the Pearl River Delta's coastal geography during the Mongol conquest.
