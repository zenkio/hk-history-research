## Background

By the late **13th century**, the Mongol Yuan dynasty under **Kublai Khan** was rapidly conquering the territory of the Southern Song dynasty. In **1276**, the Song capital of Lin'an (modern-day Hangzhou) fell to the invaders. Loyalists and members of the imperial family, led by officials such as **Chen宜中 (Chen Yizhong)** and **Zhang Shijie (張世傑)**, sought refuge in the southern coastal regions, eventually moving their court into the territory of modern-day Hong Kong.

## What happened

The young court established a temporary base in various coastal settlements, including **Tuen Mun (屯門)** and later **Mui Wo (梅窩)** on Lantau Island (**大嶼山**). The harsh conditions of flight, disease, and the constant threat of Mongol attacks severely impacted the health of the imperial family. According to historical accounts, **Emperor Duanzong (宋端宗)**, whose personal name was **Zhao Shi (趙昰)**, suffered from severe illness aggravated by a boating accident in which he fell into the sea. On **May 8, 1278** (**Gangwu day of the 4th lunar month in the 14th year of Xianchun / 1st year of Jingyan**), the seven-year-old emperor died in Mui Wo. Following his death, his younger brother **Zhao Bing (趙昺)** was enthroned as **Emperor Bing (宋帝昺)** at the age of seven.

## Significance

The death of Emperor Duanzong in Mui Wo marked a critical, desperate phase for the collapsing Southern Song resistance. The court's brief presence on Lantau Island and subsequent relocation to **Kai Tak (啟德) / Kowloon City** and eventually **Yamen (崖門)** cemented the region's historical association with the final loyalist stand of the Song dynasty. The events surrounding Duanzong's flight and death became embedded in local folklore across Hong Kong and the New Territories, shaping regional identity and historical memory.

## Perspectives

- **Qing and Mainland Historiography**: Traditional Chinese histories compiled under the Qing dynasty emphasize the unwavering loyalty of officials like Zhang Shijie and Lu Xiufu, framing the resistance and the emperor's tragic end on Lantau Island as a profound moral testament to loyalty and righteousness (忠義) against foreign conquest.
- **Local New Territories Communities**: Local oral traditions and ancestral lineages in the New Territories often connect the southward flight of the Song court to the founding and movement of various prominent clans, preserving memories of the imperial presence through village legends and temple dedications.
- **Modern Scholarship**: Contemporary historians examine the southern flight through the lens of maritime geography and refugee movements, noting how the fragmented geography of the Pearl River Delta enabled the court to survive temporarily despite overwhelming military disadvantages.
