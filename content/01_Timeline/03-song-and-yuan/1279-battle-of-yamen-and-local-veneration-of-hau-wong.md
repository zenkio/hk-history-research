## Background
In the late 13th century, the advancing Mongol forces of the Yuan Dynasty relentlessly pushed the retreating Southern Song (南宋) court southward. Following the fall of the capital, the imperial remnants, including the young boy-emperors, sought refuge along the southern coast, eventually passing through areas in modern-day Hong Kong such as Kowloon City (九龍城) and Lantau Island (大嶼山). Loyalist ministers and generals rallied to protect the crumbling dynasty, among them **Yeung Leung-ye** (**楊亮節**), a maternal uncle of the final Song emperors who is traditionally credited with defending the court and patronizing local communities.

## What happened
On **March 19, 1279** (**祥興二年二月六日**), the final confrontation between the retreating Song fleet and the Mongol navy unfolded at the **Battle of Yamen** (**崖門海戰**). Trapped in a maritime bottleneck without adequate supplies, the Song forces suffered a catastrophic defeat. Rather than submit to capture, the prime minister **Lu Xiufu** (**陸秀夫**) leapt into the sea carrying the eight-year-old Emperor Bing (宋帝昺), followed by countless loyalist officials and soldiers. This naval engagement effectively extinguished the Southern Song Dynasty and completed the Mongol conquest of China.

## Significance
In the wake of this tragedy, fleeing loyalists and local populations in the Pearl River Delta established traditions to honor those who perished for the Song cause. General Yeung Leung-ye, who reportedly fell ill and died while aiding the fleeing royals or defending the region, became the focus of widespread folk religion. Communities across Hong Kong erected shrines in his honor, popularly known as **Hau Wong temples** (**侯王廟**). These sites became integral to local cultural identity, preserving memories of the dynastic transition through annual rituals, community festivals, and architectural heritage that persist to the present day.

## Perspectives

- **Local Hong Kong and New Territories Communities**: Local villagers and indigenous lineages view Hau Wong (侯王) as a protective deity and historical hero who sacrificed his life for the righteous Song cause. Annual birthday celebrations and rituals dedicated to Hau Wong reinforce community solidarity and historical continuity with the Song loyalists.
- **Qing and Imperial Historiography**: Later imperial historians under the Qing Dynasty (清朝) emphasized loyalty, filial piety, and moral rectitude, officially praising figures like Lu Xiufu and Yeung Leung-ye as paragons of Confucian virtue who chose death over surrender to foreign conquerors.
- **Modern Scholarship**: Modern historians often analyze the cult of Hau Wong as a fascinating intersection of elite dynastic loyalty and grassroots folk religion. Scholars debate the precise historical identity of Hau Wong, noting that oral traditions surrounding Yeung Leung-ye sometimes blend different historical personages from the late Song period.
