## Background
During the **Song (960–1279)** and **Yuan (1271–1368)** dynasties, the coastal regions of the Pearl River Delta saw increasing settlement by migrants fleeing conflict in the north. The establishment of the **Tuen Mun (屯門)** naval base and the rise of the **salt and pearl industries** created a need for organized social infrastructure. As kinship clans solidified their presence in the New Territories, the necessity for communal spaces that transcended individual households grew.

## What happened
Between **1300 and 1350**, many existing religious structures in the region began to function as local administrative hubs. Originally serving as sites for ancestral or deity worship, these temples evolved to manage public tasks. This process involved the creation of 'temple estates,' where land donated by wealthy lineages generated income to maintain the building and provide for the community. These hubs became places for record-keeping, where family genealogies and land contracts were stored, and where elders convened to settle disputes through mediation, bypassing the need for distant imperial magistrates. 

## Significance
The transformation of temples into social hubs was central to the development of the 'lineage society' characteristic of the New Territories. These networks provided a framework for resource distribution, particularly in irrigation management and defense against pirates. By linking religious devotion with social order, these temples ensured that even in periods of weak central administration, local communities remained stable and self-regulating. This structure laid the groundwork for the enduring influence of the 'Five Great Clans' (五大氏族) in Hong Kong history.

## Perspectives

- **New Territories Clan Traditions**: Local clans view these temples as the foundational pillars of their ancestral heritage and legitimate authority. They argue that these institutions were autonomously established to protect indigenous interests against external interference.
- **Colonial-era Historiography**: Early British historians often categorized these local structures as 'village institutions' that functioned in a vacuum, focusing on the absence of formal Qing-style bureaucratic control in the region prior to the 19th century.
- **Mainland/Mainstream Academic**: Contemporary scholars often emphasize how these local networks were actually part of a larger 'civilizing' process, reflecting how the agrarian structures of the southern periphery were gradually integrated into the broader Chinese cultural and political system.
