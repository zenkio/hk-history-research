## Background
During the Song (宋朝) and early Yuan (元朝) periods, the southern coastal regions of Guangdong, including present-day Hong Kong, experienced significant demographic growth. Established clans migrated southward, reclaiming coastal land for agriculture, salt production, and pearl harvesting. Prior to the **1300s**, land tenure was often informal, managed through customary practices, lineage networks, and local strongmen rather than centralized imperial taxation frameworks.

## What happened
As the Mongol Yuan administration sought to consolidate control and secure reliable revenue from the south, it implemented detailed land surveys and registration methods, often referred to as fish-scale land registers (魚鱗圖冊). These registers mapped plots of land in a scaled, contiguous pattern resembling fish scales, recording boundaries, soil quality, acreage, and the names of the registered landowners. Local lineages and landholders in the New Territories were required to formally document their holdings, linking private property rights directly to state-mandated grain and tax obligations.

## Significance
This bureaucratic standardization marked a critical turning point in the integration of the Hong Kong region into the broader imperial Chinese administrative apparatus. By formalizing land ownership, the Yuan registers empowered dominant clans to legally defend their ancestral estates while simultaneously binding them to imperial fiscal extraction. Modern historians view this administrative push as a foundational moment that shaped the enduring lineage-village structures characteristic of the New Territories for centuries to follow.

## Perspectives

- **Local New Territories Lineages**: Indigenous clans often view early land registration as a dual-edged sword that legally recognized their ancestral domains and property rights while imposing heavy bureaucratic and tax burdens from distant northern rulers.
- **Imperial Administration and Scholarship**: Imperial and modern historians generally emphasize the fish-scale registers as a rational state-building mechanism designed to curb tax evasion, map taxable resources, and integrate peripheral coastal zones into the agrarian economy.
