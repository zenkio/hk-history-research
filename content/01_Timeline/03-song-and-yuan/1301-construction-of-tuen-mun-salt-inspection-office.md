## Background
During the Song (**960–1279**) and Yuan (**1271–1368**) dynasties, the southern coastal regions of China, including the territory that is now **Hong Kong**, became increasingly integrated into the imperial economy. Salt production and maritime trade were vital sources of revenue for the imperial court. By the late thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries, the Yuan administration sought to exert tighter administrative and fiscal control over the delta region, which had previously operated with a degree of local autonomy and robust maritime commerce.

## What happened
In **1301**, officials constructed or significantly expanded the **Tuen Mun Salt Inspection Office (屯門鹽場官署)**. This post was established to police coastal shipping routes, monitor local salt pans, and enforce strict state monopolies on salt production and distribution. Historical records suggest that the office served as both a customs and tax-collection station, housing government agents tasked with curbing private smuggling and ensuring that the imperial treasury received its proper share of maritime trade revenues.

## Significance
The establishment of the Tuen Mun Salt Inspection Office marks a pivotal moment in the formal administrative incorporation of the **Hong Kong** region into the imperial apparatus. It highlights the strategic importance of **Tuen Mun (屯門)** as a maritime hub and gateway to the Pearl River Delta. While the physical structures have long since vanished, the office exemplifies how dynastic states utilized fiscal and military infrastructure to project power along the southern maritime frontier.

## Perspectives

- **Qing and Beijing Historiography**: Traditional and mainland historiography generally views the establishment of such offices as progressive steps toward effective governance, economic integration, and the enforcement of rule of law over borderlands.
- **Local and Regional Communities**: Local lineages and coastal inhabitants often viewed Yuan salt monopolies and inspection offices as intrusive fiscal burdens that disrupted traditional livelihoods and trade networks, leading to a long history of friction between locals and imperial agents.
- **Modern Scholarship**: Recent historical research emphasizes that these outposts reflect the Yuan dynasty's sophisticated maritime strategy, showing how the state sought to monetize and police complex coastal networks that it could not entirely dominate.
