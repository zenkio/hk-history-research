## Background
During the **Song dynasty (960–1279)** and into the **Yuan dynasty (1271–1368)**, the population of the region that is now Hong Kong experienced significant growth. This demographic shift was largely driven by migration from the Central Plains of China southward into the New Territories. As powerful lineages and clans established walled villages and agricultural settlements, local economic dynamics shifted from purely subsistence farming and fishing toward inter-community exchange.

## What happened
Around **1310**, the first informal periodic markets—often precursors to traditional rural markets or fairs—began to take shape in **Yuen Long (元朗)**. These gatherings were not permanent urban centers, but rather cyclical markets held on specific days. They provided a crucial neutral ground where inland farming clans could trade surplus agricultural produce, such as rice and vegetables, for the catch of coastal fishing communities and marine products. Over time, these periodic exchanges laid the spatial and social foundations for more permanent market towns in the New Territories.

## Significance
The emergence of these early markets marks a pivotal moment in the socio-economic history of the region, demonstrating early regional economic integration independent of major imperial administrative hubs. It highlights the agency of local lineages and communities in organizing their own trade networks. While written records from this early period are sparse, archaeological findings and oral histories from local clans suggest that these early trading hubs cemented patterns of rural commerce that persisted for centuries.

## Perspectives

- **Local New Territories Lineages**: Local indigenous communities view these early markets as foundational to their ancestors' self-reliance and the establishment of powerful regional kinship networks. Oral traditions emphasize the cooperative nature of these gatherings in balancing the needs of farmers and fishers.
