## Background
During the **Song dynasty (960–1279)** and into the **Yuan dynasty (1271–1368)**, the coastal regions of the Pearl River Delta saw increasing settlement by migrant clans fleeing northern instability. The shallow waters of Deep Bay (后海灣), protected by mudflats and nutrient-rich waters, provided an ideal environment for the cultivation of oysters (*Crassostrea hongkongensis*). While coastal communities had likely harvested wild oysters for centuries, the Yuan period marked a shift toward more systematic, sedentary management of these resources.

## What happened
Around **1320**, records and oral histories associated with the **Tang clan (鄧氏)** suggest a significant expansion in the formal management of oyster beds along the northwestern coast of the New Territories. This process involved the demarcation of tidal zones and the deliberate seeding of oyster larvae on stone or tile substrates, a technique known as 'bottom culture.' While evidence for the exact scale of this activity in the early 14th century is based partly on clan genealogy and tradition, it is widely accepted that the **Tang clan** consolidated control over these coastal regions during this era. This management style required cooperation among local settlements to maintain the beds and protect them from outside encroachment.

## Significance
The systematic development of oyster beds established a long-term economic model for the clans residing along the Deep Bay coastline. It transformed the area from a source of transient gathering into a site of sustained industrial production. The oyster industry became an integral part of the local economy, shaping the social hierarchy and land-tenure systems of the New Territories. The influence of this industry persisted for centuries, providing a steady source of food and trade revenue that helped sustain the clan-based villages through the Ming and Qing dynasties.

## Perspectives

- **Local New Territories Clans**: For the indigenous clans, particularly the Tang, the oyster beds represent ancestral land rights and a historical continuity of management that predates modern administrative borders. They view the 14th-century expansion as the formalization of their stewardship over the coastal environment.
- **Later Historical Scholarship**: Historians often highlight the oyster industry as a prime example of human adaptation to the ecological constraints of the Pearl River Delta. While acknowledging the significance of clan records, scholars note that 'ownership' in the Yuan period was likely fluid and contingent on the ability of specific groups to defend their resource claims against the centralizing policies of the imperial state.
