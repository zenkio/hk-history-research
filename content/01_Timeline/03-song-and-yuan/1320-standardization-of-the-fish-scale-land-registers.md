## Background
During the **Song dynasty (960–1279)** and the subsequent **Yuan dynasty (1271–1368)**, the territory now known as Hong Kong—including areas such as **Tuen Mun (屯門)** and **Tai Po (大埔)**—increasingly integrated into the administrative structures of the Chinese imperial state. While the region was peripheral to the political centers of northern China, it was economically vital due to its salt production and pearl diving industries. As the population grew through the migration of clans, the need for a more systematic method of tracking land ownership and agricultural output became apparent to central authorities.

## What happened
Around **1320**, the **Yuan dynasty** government sought to consolidate control over landholdings to improve tax efficiency. The mechanism for this was the standardization of the Fish-Scale Land Registers (魚鱗圖冊). These were so named because the maps, when pieced together, resembled the scales of a fish. Each plot of land was measured, classified by its productivity and soil quality, and recorded alongside the owner’s name. This survey brought previously informal land holdings under a formalized state registry, documenting the expansion of settled agricultural land by local clans and migrant families.

## Significance
The introduction of these registers marked a shift in the relationship between the state and the local population. By creating a standardized ledger of property, the Yuan government facilitated more accurate agricultural tax collection. For the local clans residing in the New Territories (新界), this registration process provided a degree of state recognition for their land claims, though it also subjected them to higher fiscal demands. This bureaucratic transition reflected broader trends in medieval China, where the state moved toward a more centralized and granular oversight of its southern peripheries, laying the administrative groundwork that would persist into the **Ming (1368–1644)** and **Qing (1644–1912)** dynasties.

## Perspectives

- **Colonial-era Historiography**: Earlier British historians often portrayed these administrative shifts as early attempts to bring 'law and order' to a region characterized as a lawless backwater, framing the registers as a precursor to modern cadastral surveys.
- **Local Clan Perspectives**: For the powerful clans, such as the Tang (鄧) lineage, the state registration process was a double-edged sword; while it formalized their landed property rights, it also established the tax obligations that defined their relationship with subsequent imperial dynasties.
- **Modern Scholarly Consensus**: Contemporary historians emphasize that these registers represent a complex negotiation between the Mongol-led Yuan state and local southern elites, rather than a top-down imposition of absolute control.
