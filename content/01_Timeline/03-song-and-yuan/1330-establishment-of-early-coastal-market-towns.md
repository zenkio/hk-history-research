## Background
During the Song and Yuan dynasties (**960–1368**), the population of the Pearl River Delta grew significantly, driven by southward migration from mainland China. In the region that would become Hong Kong, communities relied heavily on agriculture, salt production, and pearl harvesting. As local economic activity intensified, the need for centralized spaces to exchange surplus goods became apparent. 

## What happened
Between **1330 and 1340**, regularized periodic markets began to form at strategic terrestrial and river junctions. These early market towns served as vital nodes where agrarian villagers, fisherfolk, and maritime traders could meet to conduct commerce. Locations near river mouths and sheltered bays offered natural advantages for transporting goods via small boats and carts, encouraging the growth of these nascent trading hubs.

## Significance
The establishment of early coastal market towns laid the socioeconomic foundation for the regional network of villages and market centers that would characterize the New Territories for centuries. These periodic gatherings fostered closer economic interdependence among diverse local communities and integrated the coastal population more firmly into broader maritime trade routes spanning the Pearl River Delta.

## Perspectives

- **Local Hong Kong and New Territories Communities**: Local oral traditions and clan histories often emphasize the grassroots initiative of founding families in establishing market sites to secure economic self-sufficiency and protect community interests.
- **Later Academic Scholarship**: Modern historians analyze these early markets through the lens of regional geography and economic integration, viewing them as a natural response to population growth and the commercialization of the rural Yuan economy.
