## Background
By the mid-14th century, the coastal regions of the Pearl River Delta, including areas now known as the New Territories, experienced significant demographic shifts. Waves of migration from the north, particularly during the late **Song (宋)** and early **Yuan (元)** periods, had brought established scholarly clans into the region. These groups often sought to distance themselves from the encroaching political instability of the Yuan central government by reinforcing their local economic autonomy.

## What happened
Between **1350 and 1368**, there was a notable surge in the compilation of genealogical records or 'zupu' (族譜). These documents were not merely family trees; they served as vital legal instruments. By recording lineage back to ancestors who had performed meritorious service or held official titles, local clans could effectively stake claims to land ownership. This process of consolidation helped integrate fragmented agricultural plots into large-scale, family-managed landed estates. While the central Yuan authority weakened, these clans solidified their local governance, acting as the de facto administrators of water rights, land distribution, and local security.

## Significance
This period marked the transition from transient migrant settlement to the establishment of sedentary, entrenched lineage societies. The lineage system became the primary vehicle for social mobility and resource management. By linking land rights to hereditary bloodlines, clans like the **Tang (鄧)**, **Hau (侯)**, **Pang (彭)**, **Liu (廖)**, and **Man (文)** ensured the continuity of their assets across generations. This social structure would persist through the Ming and Qing dynasties and remains a defining feature of New Territories history today, even as modern urban development has transformed the landscape.

## Perspectives

- **Local New Territories Lineage Communities**: Many clans view these genealogies as authentic historical records that prove ancestral occupation and rights, emphasizing the continuity of their traditions and land stewardship since the late Song and Yuan eras.
- **Academic Historiography**: Modern scholars often debate the extent to which these 14th-century genealogies were 'retrospectively constructed' to grant legitimacy to land claims that were actually consolidated much later, suggesting that the records may represent a social aspiration as much as a historical reality.
