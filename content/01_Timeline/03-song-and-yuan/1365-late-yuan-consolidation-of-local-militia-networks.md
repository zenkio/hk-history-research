## Background
During the waning decades of the Yuan dynasty (元朝), central administrative control over the southern coastal fringes of Guangdong weakened significantly. The Pearl River Delta and its surrounding islands, including areas now comprising Hong Kong (香港), faced mounting instability. Imperial garrisons struggled to maintain order, leaving coastal communities vulnerable to both the encroachment of rival rebel factions and the depredations of heavily armed maritime raiders and pirates operating along the South China coast.

## What happened
Between **1365** and **1368**, powerful local clans and prominent lineages in the region—such as the Tang clan of Kam Tin (錦田鄧氏)—took local security into their own hands. Capitalizing on their entrenched economic resources and kinship ties, these communities constructed fortified settlements (圍村), walled villages, and watchtowers. They organized voluntary local militias (鄉兵) to patrol trade routes, safeguard salt pans, and protect agricultural yields. Rather than relying on failing imperial structures, these militia networks operated autonomously, forming regional alliances to share intelligence and coordinate armed resistance against localized threats.

## Significance
This period of localized consolidation marked a crucial turning point in the socio-political evolution of the New Territories (新界). The reliance on clan-based militias laid the structural foundation for the lineage society that would dominate the region throughout the Ming (明朝) and Qing (清朝) dynasties. By organizing for self-defense, these prominent families secured long-term territorial hegemony, economic dominance, and a tradition of localized autonomy that persisted well into the modern era.

## Perspectives

- **Local Hong Kong and New Territories Communities**: Descendants of the early clans view these militia networks as heroic acts of self-preservation and community foundation, demonstrating the resilience and independence of indigenous lineages against external chaos.
- **Later Historiography and Qing/Beijing Perspective**: State-centered historiography often frames these autonomous armed networks through the lens of local gentry mobilization, emphasizing the eventual necessity of bringing such independent regional power structures back under imperial taxation and legal control during the early Ming dynasty.
