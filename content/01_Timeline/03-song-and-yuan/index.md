## Overview

The Song (宋) and Yuan (元) dynasties (**960–1368**) marked a critical era in the formation of Hong Kong’s social, economic, and cultural landscape. During these four centuries, the region transitioned from a sparsely populated maritime frontier into an economically significant area and a sanctuary for imperial refugees. The period was characterized by two parallel developments: the organized settlement of dominant agricultural clans in the fertile valleys of the New Territories, and the imperial exploitation of coastal resources, notably salt and pearls.

Historically, the local economy was heavily shaped by state-monopolized industries. The **Guanfu Salt Field** (官富場), spanning parts of modern Kowloon, Tsuen Wan, and Sai Kung, was established during the Song to manage lucrative salt production. Similarly, the waters of Tai Po Hoi (大埔海), also known as Tolo Harbour, became famous for pearl harvesting, attracting both imperial greed and local labor exploitation. The era culminated in the dramatic flight of the Southern Song court to the region in **1277** ahead of advancing Mongol forces, an event that left an indelible mark on local memory, religious worship, and geography.

## Key themes

### Clan Settlement and the Walled Villages

This era saw the arrival of the 'Five Great Clans' who would dominate the New Territories' socio-political landscape for centuries. The **Tang Clan** (鄧氏) was the first to settle, establishing themselves in Kam Tin (錦田) during the Northern Song dynasty around **973**. They were followed by the **Hau Clan** (侯氏) in Ho Sheung Heung (河上鄉), the **Liu Clan** (廖氏) in Sheung Shui (上水), the **Pang Clan** (彭氏) in Fanling Wai (粉嶺圍), and the **Man Clan** (文氏) in San Tin (新田) during the late Song and Yuan periods. To protect their ancestral lands from coastal bandits and rival groups, these lineages developed fortified walled villages (*wai* 圍), establishing a pattern of defensive, communal living.

### Imperial Resource Exploitation and Local Resistance

The economic value of the region lay in its natural resources, which the imperial governments sought to control tightly. The **Guanfu Salt Field** was highly productive, but state monopolies often sparked intense local friction. In **1197**, Lantau Island (爛頭島) salt farmers rose in a violent uprising against the Song government’s strict trade prohibitions, an event that was met with brutal military suppression. Under the Yuan dynasty, the administration reorganized the salt fields and constructed the **Tuen Mun Salt Inspection Office** (屯門鹽課司) in **1329** to enforce tax collection. Meanwhile, pearl harvesting in Tai Po Hoi underwent cycles of intense exploitation and periodic suspension due to the high mortality rate among local divers, reflecting the tension between imperial demand and local survival.

### The Tragedy of the Southern Song Court

As the Mongol conquest pushed southward, the infant Song Emperor Duanzong (端宗) and his brother, the future Emperor Bing (帝昺), fled to Kowloon in **1277**. They established a temporary imperial court at **Sacred Hill** (聖山) in modern-day To Kwa Wan (土瓜灣). The court's flight was plagued by disaster: a devastating typhoon struck the imperial fleet in late **1277**, causing massive casualties. Emperor Duanzong fell ill and died at Mui Wo (梅窩) on Lantau Island in **1278**. The surviving court departed Hong Kong waters for Yamen (崖門) in modern Jiangmen, where the dynasty met its final defeat in **1279**. This dramatic episode is commemorated by the **Joss House Bay Rock Inscription** (大廟灣刻石), carved in **1274**, which provides valuable contemporary insights into local governance and religious life.

## How it shaped Hong Kong

The legacy of the Song and Yuan dynasties remains visible in Hong Kong today. The lineage networks established by the Five Great Clans secured ancestral land rights that survived through the Qing dynasty and were formally recognized by the British colonial government under the Block Crown Leases of the early 20th century. These clans still maintain strong communal identities, ancestral halls, and traditional festivals.

Furthermore, the tragic flight of the Song court transformed local geography and spirituality. Sites like **Sung Wong Toi** (宋王臺) became symbols of imperial loyalty and historical memory. The deification of loyalist officials, most notably the general **Hau Wong** (侯王), led to the construction of numerous temples throughout Hong Kong, blending imperial history with local popular religion.

## Perspectives

- **Local New Territories Clans**: For the descendants of the Five Great Clans, this era represents the foundational origin of their ancestral land rights, social prestige, and cultural identity. They emphasize their continuous occupation of the land since the Song dynasty, using historical genealogies to assert their unique indigenous status under modern Hong Kong laws.
- **Qing and Modern Chinese Nationalist Historiography**: Traditional Chinese historiography, from the Qing dynasty to modern mainland narratives, emphasizes the tragic flight of the Southern Song court. This perspective frames Hong Kong not as a peripheral fishing margin, but as a heroic sanctuary of loyalist patriotism and orthodox Chinese imperial culture during a time of foreign invasion.
- **British Colonial Administration**: Early British colonial administrators often viewed the deep-seated clan networks established during this era as a political challenge to centralized colonial governance. However, later 20th-century colonial scholars recognized these lineages as highly sophisticated, self-governing rural communities that had successfully maintained local order for centuries.
- **Modern Revisionist Scholarship**: Recent academic scholarship deconstructs the unified narrative of the 'Five Great Clans,' suggesting that their dominance was not as absolute or continuous as later genealogies claim. Historians argue these lineage structures were actively consolidated and rewritten during the Ming and Qing dynasties to secure tax exemptions and land privileges.
