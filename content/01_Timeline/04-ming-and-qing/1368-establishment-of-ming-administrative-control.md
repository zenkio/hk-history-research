## Background
Before the rise of the **Ming Dynasty** (大明) in **1368**, the region comprising present-day Hong Kong was largely viewed by imperial authorities as a remote, peripheral area prone to maritime instability. During the preceding **Song Dynasty** (大宋) and **Yuan Dynasty** (大元), the salt production, pearl harvesting, and fishing industries had grown significantly, attracting various clans and communities to settle along the coasts and islands. However, formal and continuous bureaucratic control from the imperial center remained loose, with local affairs largely managed by powerful indigenous lineages and maritime networks.

## What happened
With the expulsion of the Mongols and the establishment of the Ming Dynasty under the **Hongwu Emperor** (洪武帝) in **1368**, the new government sought to consolidate territorial control and secure the southern maritime borders. As part of this administrative consolidation, the Hong Kong area was formally integrated into **Dongguan County** (東莞縣) within **Guangdong Province** (廣東省). This integration brought local settlements under the direct fiscal, judicial, and military oversight of the county magistrate, requiring the registration of households, the collection of taxes, and the implementation of the imperial household registration system.

## Significance
The incorporation of the region into Dongguan County laid the foundation for structured imperial governance in the territory. It enabled prominent local clans—such as the **Tang clan** (鄧氏) of Kam Tin—to leverage imperial examination systems and bureaucratic structures to enhance their local social standing and land control. While piracy and coastal raids would continue to challenge Ming authority throughout the dynasty, this administrative act permanently linked the local population to the institutional framework of the Chinese empire.

## Perspectives

- **Imperial Historiography**: Traditional Chinese dynastic records view the establishment of Ming control as a civilizing and stabilizing process that brought outlying coastal populations into the moral and administrative fold of the empire.
- **Local Lineage Perspective**: Indigenous New Territories clans often remember early Ming administration through the lens of lineage self-organization, viewing state integration as an opportunity to secure official recognition, protect land rights, and build local ancestral power structures.
