## Background
During the early **Ming dynasty (1368–1644)**, the imperial government faced significant security challenges along the southern coast. The **Hongwu Emperor** sought to consolidate control over maritime activities and suppress piracy, which had become rampant in the Pearl River Delta. Tuen Mun (屯門), long recognized as a strategic gateway for navigation and trade, became a primary focus for imperial oversight. The establishment of a military installation was intended to project central authority and safeguard the waters near the Pearl River estuary.

## What happened
Around **1370**, the Ming authorities moved to formalize their presence in the region by establishing a military garrison in Tuen Mun. This site served as an outpost for the local maritime administration. While the exact scale of the garrison remains a subject of archaeological debate, historical records indicate that it functioned as a surveillance point for patrolling vessels and monitoring foreign merchants. The garrison was part of a broader network of coastal defenses designed to enforce the *haijin* (海禁), or maritime prohibitions, which restricted private overseas trade to maintain state control over international commerce.

## Significance
The establishment of the Tuen Mun garrison marks a critical juncture in the integration of the Hong Kong region into the Ming imperial defense apparatus. By asserting a permanent military presence, the state sought to define the coast as part of its sovereign territory rather than a lawless frontier. However, the effectiveness of this garrison in curbing illicit activities is often questioned by scholars. While it provided a symbolic assertion of power, the logistical difficulties of maintaining remote coastal outposts meant that the actual level of control fluctuated significantly over the following centuries. The garrison highlights the ongoing historical tension between central state control and the fluid, often autonomous, nature of maritime life in the Pearl River Delta.

## Perspectives

- **Mainland Chinese historical scholarship**: This perspective emphasizes the garrison as evidence of the Ming state's consistent sovereignty and administrative control over the Hong Kong region. It views the installation as a vital component of a unified national defense strategy against foreign maritime encroachment.
- **Local New Territories historiography**: Local accounts often focus on the impact of these military policies on indigenous coastal communities, who frequently found their traditional livelihoods disrupted by imperial maritime bans. This view suggests the garrison was an external imposition that sometimes created friction with the local population.
