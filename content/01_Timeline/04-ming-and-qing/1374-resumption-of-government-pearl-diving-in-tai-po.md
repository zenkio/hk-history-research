## Background
Pearl diving in the waters of present-day Hong Kong, particularly in Tolo Harbour, known historically as Tai Po Hoi (大埔海), has a long history dating back at least to the Han dynasty. During the early Ming dynasty, the imperial court sought to monopolize lucrative luxury commodities. In **1374** (the seventh year of the Hongong reign), the founding emperor, **Emperor Hongwu** (明太祖), ordered the official resumption of government pearl harvesting in the region.

## What happened
Under the imperial decree, local divers—often referred to in historical texts as 'water people' or pearl divers—were conscripted into dangerous state-managed labor to harvest pearls from the seabed of Tolo Harbour. Historical records suggest that the extraction process was perilous and demanding, exposing divers to treacherous currents, cold waters, and exploitation by local officials. The forced labor and heavy quotas imposed by the government caused widespread disruption to the local agrarian and fishing economy, leading to severe hardship and the displacement of communities in the area.

## Significance
This event illustrates the early Ming dynasty's tight fiscal control over southern coastal regions and the heavy human cost of imperial monopolies on peripheral populations. The hardships endured by the Pearl River Delta communities during such state interventions highlight the friction between central dynastic ambitions and the well-being of local inhabitants in the newly incorporated territories of Guangdong.

## Perspectives

- **Imperial Court (Ming Dynasty)**: From the perspective of the central government, the resumption of pearl diving was a legitimate assertion of state rights over natural resources to secure luxury goods and revenue for the imperial household.
- **Local New Territories Communities**: Local oral traditions and regional histories emphasize the unbearable exploitation, physical danger, and economic ruin brought upon indigenous families by harsh imperial labor quotas and official corruption.
