## Background
Long before British administration, the coastal region of modern-day Hong Kong supported specialized agricultural and forestry economies. During the Ming dynasty, local villagers in the Pearl River Delta began cultivating *Aquilaria sinensis* (Chinese eaglewood trees), locally known as **chenxiang** (沉香) or **guanxiang** (官香). The soil and climate of the region proved exceptionally well-suited for producing high-grade incense resin.

## What happened
In the **1420s**, the incense trade transitioned from a localized cottage industry into a broader commercial network. Villagers across **Tsim Sha Tsui** (尖沙咀), **Sha Tin** (沙田), and **Lantau Island** (大嶼山) engaged in harvesting and processing the fragrant wood. The harvested resin was transported via a central transshipment hub at **Shek Pai Wan** (香港仔 / 石排灣) on Hong Kong Island—historically referred to as the 'Fragrant Harbour'—to be shipped onward to Guangzhou (廣州) and eventually to imperial capitals and overseas markets.

## Significance
This economic expansion left a permanent linguistic and cultural mark on the territory. Most notably, the operational name 'Fragrant Harbour' or **Hong Kong** (香港) is widely believed by historians and local communities to have originated from the bustling export trade of this aromatic resin. The industry integrated the coastal populations of the New Territories into wider Ming economic circuits, though it also made them vulnerable to maritime raiding and shifting dynastic policies in later centuries.

## Perspectives

- **Local New Territories Communities**: Oral histories and lineage traditions in the New Territories emphasize the role of indigenous clans in establishing the incense cultivation techniques, viewing the trade as foundational to early settlement prosperity and identity before external merchant networks dominated.
- **Later Scholarship**: Modern historians analyze the incense trade through the lens of Ming fiscal policies and maritime geography, noting that while the trade connected the region to broader Asian markets, its precise scale and economic impact remain difficult to quantify due to fragmentary local records.
