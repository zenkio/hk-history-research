## Background
During the Ming dynasty (**1368**–**1644**), the coastal areas of present-day Hong Kong experienced gradual settlement by fisherfolk, farmers, and salt-pan workers. Lantau Island (大嶼山) and its surrounding waters were integral to maritime trade and local marine harvesting. Within these communities, worship of Tin Hau (天后), the Goddess of the Sea and protector of seafarers also known as Mazu (媽祖), took root as a vital cultural and spiritual practice.

## What happened
Local oral tradition and historical temple records suggest that the Tai O Tin Hau Temple (大澳天后古廟) was originally constructed in **1486** (the 20th year of the Chenghua reign). While the exact physical structure of this initial foundation remains difficult to verify due to subsequent rebuilds and renovations over the centuries, the establishment of the shrine provided a focal point for the scattered boat dwellers and salt-producers operating around Tai O (大澳). The temple served not only as a place of religious devotion for safe navigation and bountiful catches, but also as a central institution for community gathering and dispute resolution.

## Significance
The founding of the Tai O Tin Hau temple is widely cited as a benchmark for the spread of maritime deity worship in the region's outer islands. It illustrates how early economic activities, particularly fishing and salt production, shaped the social geography of the Pearl River Delta. Subsequent renovations in later dynasties reflect the enduring reliance of local communities on maritime networks under both Ming and Qing (清朝) administrations.
