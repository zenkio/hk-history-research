## Background

By the early 16th century, the **Portuguese Empire** was rapidly expanding its maritime trade routes across maritime Asia following **Vasco da Gama**'s pioneering voyage to India. Having successfully captured Malacca in 1511, Portuguese authorities in **Goa** sought to establish formal trade relations with Ming dynasty **China** (**中國**). 

## What happened

In **May 1513**, the explorer **Jorge Álvares** (**若體·阿瓦雷斯**) sailed a trading vessel into the Pearl River Delta and anchored at an island or coastal area known to the Portuguese as *Tamão*, which most modern historians and local authorities identify as **Tuen Mun** (**屯門**). Upon arrival, Álvares erected a padrão (a stone pillar bearing the Portuguese coat of arms) to claim the territory for his king and established a trading post. This initial voyage facilitated peaceful commercial exchanges with local merchants, paving the way for subsequent, larger Portuguese expeditions.

## Significance

This landing represents the first recorded direct maritime contact between Europe and the Hong Kong area. While the initial encounters under Álvares were relatively peaceful, subsequent Portuguese arrivals proved more aggressive, leading to severe diplomatic friction with the Ming court. These escalating tensions ultimately culminated in armed naval confrontations at the **Battle of Tuen Mun** (**屯門海戰**) in 1521 and the **Battle of Xicaowan** (**西草灣之戰**) in 1522, after which the Ming dynasty expelled the Portuguese from the immediate Canton estuary, eventually leading to their permanent settlement further south in **Macau** (**澳門**) decades later.

## Perspectives

- **Portuguese Historiography**: Traditional and modern Portuguese accounts view Jorge Álvares as a pioneering navigator who opened legitimate commercial pathways to the Far East, emphasizing the peaceful and exploratory nature of his initial 1513 landing.
- **Ming Official Records**: Imperial Chinese records generally categorized the early European arrivals through the lens of maritime defense and tributary protocol, viewing unauthorized foreign landfalls and fortification as illegal incursions that threatened regional stability.
