## Background

During the middle of the **Ming Dynasty (1368–1644)**, the coastal regions of Guangdong were subject to shifting administrative focus. The imperial government sought to integrate the Pearl River Delta more firmly into its taxation and production frameworks. Salt production, a state-monopoly industry for much of Chinese history, was a primary driver of this integration. Coastal land near the mouth of the Pearl River and the waters surrounding the **New Territories** offered ideal conditions for tidal salt extraction, necessitating a stable population of workers and administrative oversight.

## What happened

Around **1520**, records indicate the formalization and expansion of the **Xiliang Salt Fields (西瀝鹽場)**. While salt production had likely occurred in smaller, informal capacities for generations, this period saw a concerted effort to scale operations. The establishment involved the construction of evaporation ponds and the settlement of workers, often supervised by local officials appointed by the **Dongguan County (東莞縣)** magistracy, which held jurisdiction over these areas at the time. This process integrated the salt-producing communities into the wider bureaucratic and economic reach of the **Ming** state, facilitating tax collection and the formal registration of households.

## Significance

The salt fields provided the vital economic backbone for early settler villages. In a region where agriculture was initially difficult due to hilly terrain and variable soil quality, the ability to produce salt—a commodity essential for food preservation and trade—provided villagers with a reliable source of income and a means to trade for grain. The development of these fields helped transform temporary coastal encampments into more permanent village structures, some of which became the ancestral foundations for lineages that would later dominate the social landscape of the **New Territories**. The infrastructure built for salt production also necessitated early forms of local self-governance and defense, as these productive sites required protection from piracy and illicit smuggling operations that were prevalent along the South China coast during the **16th century**.

## Perspectives

- **Qing/Beijing official historiography**: Traditional imperial records often emphasize the success of the state in establishing orderly administration and revenue collection through the salt monopoly. This perspective views the expansion as a triumph of central planning and the civilizing influence of the Ming bureaucracy over the coastal frontier.
- **Local New Territories lineages**: Descendants of local clans often interpret the history of these sites through the lens of lineage development and land rights. For these communities, the salt fields represent the early economic efforts of their ancestors to establish a permanent footprint in the region, focusing more on community survival than imperial administrative goals.
