## Background

In the early 16th century, Portuguese maritime expansion reached the shores of southern China. Following the arrival of **Jorge Álvares** in **1513**, Portuguese merchants sought to establish official trade relations and built fortifications at Tuen Mun (屯門). At the time, the Ming dynasty enforced strict maritime prohibition laws (*haijin*), which restricted foreign trade and unauthorized maritime contact. Tensions escalated as the Portuguese established a settlement, mounted artillery, and reportedly engaged in aggressive commercial behavior and the alleged acquisition of local inhabitants, causing alarm among local officials and coastal communities.

## What happened

In **1521**, the Ming imperial court dispatched a naval fleet under the command of regional officials, including **Wang Hong** (汪鋐), to expel the foreigners and clear the area. The initial Ming approach involved diplomatic demands for the Portuguese to leave, which were rejected. Subsequently, a series of naval engagements took place in the waters around Tuen Mun. Utilizing fire ships and superior local knowledge of the winds, tides, and geography, the Ming naval forces successfully overwhelmed the heavily armed Portuguese carracks and caravels. The surviving Portuguese ships, commanded by **Martim Afonso de Melo Coutinho**, were forced to retreat southward toward Malacca, leaving behind artillery and stranded personnel.

## Significance

The **Battle of Tunmen** (屯門海戰) halted early Portuguese attempts to force open direct trade ports on Chinese terms through military strength. For the Ming dynasty, the victory reinforced the efficacy of coastal defense policies and sea prohibitions, though illicit maritime trade persisted. Prevented from establishing a permanent foothold at Tuen Mun, the Portuguese subsequently shifted their diplomatic and commercial strategies, eventually securing a negotiated and tolerated base at Macau later in the century rather than taking territory by force.

## Perspectives

- **Ming Imperial Administration**: Ming officials viewed the expedition as a necessary defense of imperial sovereignty against unruly and aggressive foreign interlopers who violated maritime prohibitions and disrupted regional stability.
- **Portuguese Chroniclers**: Contemporary Portuguese accounts often framed their presence as exploratory and commercial ventures hindered by hostile local officials, interpreting the military conflict as an unfortunate resistance to legitimate international trade.
