## Background

During the early 16th century, Portuguese maritime expansion reached the Pearl River Delta. Following initial contacts led by **Jorge Álvares**, Portuguese traders and adventurers sought to establish commercial and diplomatic relations with the Ming dynasty. While early interactions involved some trade, tensions quickly escalated due to Portuguese demands, fortified settlements, and reports of aggressive behavior in local waters, particularly around Tuen Mun (屯門) and Lantau Island (大嶼山).

## What happened

In **1522**, a Ming naval fleet confronted a Portuguese squadron commanded by **Martim Afonso de Melo Coutinho** at Xicaowan (西草灣), generally identified in modern scholarship as somewhere in the wider Hong Kong or southern Guangdong coastal waters. The Ming forces, mobilized under regional defense orders to clear foreign interlopers, launched a decisive attack against the heavily armed European vessels. Utilizing superior numbers and artillery tactics, the Chinese fleet inflicted heavy casualties on the Portuguese, forcing the surviving ships to retreat.

## Significance

The Battle of Xicaowan marked a crucial turning point in early Sino-Western relations. Rather than opening formal trade, the clash hardened the Ming court's resolve to enforce the *haijin* (海禁), or maritime prohibition laws, which banned unauthorized foreign trade and navigation. Consequently, Portuguese merchants were forced to shift their strategy from armed intrusion and coastal raiding to covert, eventually tolerated, negotiations, which decades later led to the establishment of a leased settlement in Macau (澳門).

## Perspectives

- **Ming / Qing official historiography**: Imperial records frame the conflict as a righteous suppression of foreign pirates and unruly barbarians who violated imperial sovereignty and maritime laws, validating the dynastic policy of coastal defense.
- **Later scholarship and Portuguese accounts**: Modern historians and contemporary European logs often view the engagement as a commercial miscalculation, where aggressive Portuguese mercantile ambitions clashed prematurely with an entrenched and defensive Ming tributary system.
