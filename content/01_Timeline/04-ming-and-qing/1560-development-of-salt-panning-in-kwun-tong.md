## Background
During the Ming dynasty (1368–1644), the coastal regions of the Pearl River Delta, including modern-day Hong Kong, played an active role in maritime commerce and localized industries. Salt production was one of the most strictly regulated and economically vital enterprises across imperial China. The coastal topography of the region, featuring tidal flats and sheltered bays, provided natural advantages for solar evaporation methods of salt production.

## What happened
Around **1560**, local communities in the area known as Kwun Tong (官塘) expanded their traditional salt panning activities. While salt harvesting had likely occurred in various parts of the wider Xin'an county (新安縣) for centuries, this period marked a notable intensification and formalization of production sites along the sheltered coastline. These operations involved constructing earthen dykes, managing tidal influxes, and crystallizing brine into consumable salt blocks for broader distribution.

## Significance
The expansion of salt pans in Kwun Tong contributed significantly to the local economy and tied the inhabitants more closely to the imperial fiscal apparatus. Salt was heavily monopolized by the Ming state, meaning that local production was subject to official oversight, taxation, and quotas. The industry supported local livelihoods, fostered regional trade networks stretching across the Pearl River estuary, and left a lasting imprint on the historical geography of the area, as reflected in place names associated with official salt administration.

## Perspectives

- **Imperial Qing and Ming Historiography**: Official dynastic records and gazetteers generally viewed coastal industries like salt panning through the lens of state revenue, administrative control, and border security, frequently noting the challenges of smuggling and illicit private salt trade.
- **Local New Territories Communities**: Oral traditions and local lineage histories emphasize the self-reliance, hard labor, and economic resilience of indigenous coastal settlers who adapted their livelihoods to the harsh environmental conditions of the tidal flats.
- **Modern Historiography**: Contemporary scholars analyze the expansion of Ming-era salt pans as evidence of deep commercialization and integration of the Pearl River Delta periphery into wider regional markets long before British colonization.
