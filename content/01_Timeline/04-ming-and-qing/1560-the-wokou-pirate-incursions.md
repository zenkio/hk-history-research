## Background
During the **Ming Dynasty (1368–1644)**, the maritime frontiers of Southern China faced severe challenges from maritime raiders known as **Wokou (倭寇)**. While historically characterized as Japanese pirates, later scholars note that these groups were often composed of a heterogeneous mix of Japanese rōnin, disenfranchised Chinese merchants, and local coastal populations marginalized by the **Ming maritime trade ban (海禁)**. Between **1560 and 1570**, the coastal territories of modern-day Hong Kong, particularly areas like **Tuen Mun (屯門)** and **Tai O (大澳)**, became susceptible to these opportunistic incursions.

## What happened
The raids were characterized by swift, amphibious strikes against coastal villages and shipping lanes. These incursions disrupted the local salt and fishing industries, which were vital to the regional economy. In response to the central government's fluctuating ability to provide naval protection, local communities were forced to adapt. Historical evidence suggests that this period saw the emergence of organized village militias and the reinforcement of defensive architecture, such as walled villages and watchtowers. While the **Ming** state attempted to restore order through military campaigns, the geographic complexity of the Hong Kong coastline—with its numerous inlets and islands—often allowed raiding parties to evade capture.

## Significance
The Wokou era represents a formative period for the social structure of the New Territories. The necessity of collective self-defense accelerated the consolidation of kinship groups and clan-based alliances, which would define the region's social landscape for centuries. These raids also underscored the strategic importance of the Pearl River estuary as a gateway for maritime trade and conflict. The defensive measures implemented during this era established a template for local autonomy that persisted through the subsequent Qing policies, including the Great Clearance of the late 17th century.

## Perspectives

- **Imperial Ming/Beijing Perspective**: The Ming central government viewed these incursions primarily as a failure of border management and a direct violation of the maritime trade bans. They focused on military suppression and the restoration of state-mandated order to secure the coastal periphery.
- **Local New Territories Communities**: For the indigenous lineage groups, these raids were an existential threat that necessitated the development of local militia systems (團練). This experience fostered a legacy of self-reliance and community-based governance independent of direct imperial intervention.
- **Contemporary Academic Perspective**: Modern historians argue that the Wokou phenomenon was a symptom of complex regional economic shifts rather than merely 'piracy.' They emphasize the role of trans-regional maritime networks and the socio-economic impact of Ming protectionist policies.
