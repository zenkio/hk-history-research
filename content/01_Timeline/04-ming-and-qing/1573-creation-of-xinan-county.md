## Background

By the mid-**Ming dynasty**, the coastal areas of the Pearl River Delta, including the territory that is now Hong Kong, experienced persistent maritime instability. Smuggling and piracy flourished due to the region's complex geography of islands, deep bays, and proximity to international trade routes. Local administrative control under Dongguan County (東莞縣) was weak because the region was geographically distant from the county seat. Officials found it difficult to levy taxes, maintain garrisons, and police the coastline effectively.

## What happened

In **1573** (the first year of the **Wanli** (萬曆) reign), the Ming central government approved a petition to carve out a new administrative unit from the southern part of Dongguan County. Named **Xin'an County** (新安縣), meaning 'newly pacified', the newly established county encompassed the region covering modern-day Shenzhen and Hong Kong. A walled county seat was subsequently built at **Nantou** (南頭), establishing a permanent local government presence equipped with civil officials and military garrisons to oversee coastal defense.

## Significance

The creation of Xin'an County marked a turning point in the imperial integration of the Hong Kong region. By establishing a localized magistrate, the Ming state sought to exert tighter control over the population, land registration, and maritime traffic. While piracy and smuggling persisted, the administrative framework established in 1573 defined the geopolitical boundaries of the region for centuries to come, directly shaping the ancestral lineages, market networks, and local society of the New Territories.

## Perspectives

- **Ming Imperial Administration**: From the perspective of Beijing and provincial authorities, the creation of Xin'an County was a necessary administrative reform to restore order, secure tax revenues, and suppress coastal banditry that threatened the stability of the empire's southern maritime frontier.
- **Local Lineages and Communities**: For indigenous clans and local communities in the New Territories, the establishment of the county seat at Nantou brought imperial authority closer to home, affecting local taxation, land rights, and the ways powerful lineages negotiated their autonomy with government officials.
