## Background
During the late **Ming dynasty (明朝)**, the coastal regions of Guangdong experienced considerable instability. Lawlessness increased due to the weakening authority of the imperial government, widespread banditry, and the constant threat of incursions by coastal pirates. In the fertile plains of **Yuen Long (元朗)**, the powerful **Tang clan (鄧族)**, who had settled in the region centuries earlier, sought to protect their lineage, property, and agricultural surplus from these mounting security threats.

## What happened
Around **1599**, the Tang clan established and fortified the settlement of **Kat Hing Wai (吉慶圍)**. Local oral history and clan records indicate that the villagers constructed a dense enclosure featuring thick walls made of locally fired red bricks and mud, surrounded by a defensive moat. The village layout was designed with narrow, defensive alleyways and featured four watchtowers at the corners, alongside a prominent wrought-iron entrance gate added later. These defensive measures allowed the residents to spot approaching threats early and mount a coordinated defense.

## Significance
Kat Hing Wai stands as one of the most prominent surviving examples of the walled villages (**wai tsuen 圍村**) that characterize the historical settlement patterns of the **New Territories (新界)**. While defensive walls became increasingly common across Guangdong during periods of unrest—particularly during the chaotic transitions between the Ming and Qing dynasties, and later during the **Great Clearance (遷界)** period of the 1660s—Kat Hing Wai's early construction highlights the proactive steps taken by powerful lineages to secure local autonomy and defense when imperial protection was weak.

## Perspectives

- **Local New Territories Communities**: For the indigenous Tang clan and other walled village communities, the fortification of Kat Hing Wai represents a proud history of self-reliance, unity, and resilience against external threats such as bandits and pirates.
- **Later Historical Scholarship**: Historians view walled villages like Kat Hing Wai not merely as isolated defensive structures, but as complex socio-political institutions that reflected lineage power, land control, and the shifting dynamics of local governance in southern China.
