## Background
For centuries, the shallow, nutrient-rich waters of Tolo Harbour in Tai Po (大埔) were renowned for the production of pearls. By the late **Ming Dynasty (1368–1644)**, the imperial court viewed these waters as a vital source of revenue and status symbols. The local **Tanka (疍家)** people, an indigenous boat-dwelling community, possessed the specialized maritime skills required for deep-sea diving and harvesting. For generations, these communities operated under a complex, often informal, system of tribute and local regulation.

## What happened
Around **1610**, responding to reports of declining yields and perceived tax evasion, Ming imperial officials introduced standardized reforms to the pearl farming tax system in the Tai Po region. These reforms sought to formalize the tribute collection, shifting the burden of quotas onto the local divers. Historical accounts suggest that these measures enforced rigid extraction quotas, effectively stripping the **Tanka** communities of their traditional agency over the pearl beds. The move was likely intended to stabilize state income during a period of increasing fiscal pressure on the Ming throne, but it placed an unsustainable labor demand on the divers who were forced to work in increasingly dangerous conditions.

## Significance
These reforms serve as a critical point of study for understanding the tension between centralized imperial authority and the autonomous nature of Hong Kong's maritime frontier. While state records frame the reforms as a move toward administrative efficiency and regulatory oversight, the human cost was significant. The disruption of traditional livelihoods arguably weakened the social fabric of the Tanka communities, leading to reports of unrest and, according to some local histories, the temporary abandonment of pearl harvesting sites due to exhaustion of the beds or excessive state pressure. This period highlights the vulnerability of local coastal industries to the shifting economic policies of distant central governments.

## Perspectives

- **Ming Imperial Bureaucrats**: Officials viewed the reforms as a necessary administrative measure to curb corruption and ensure that the imperial treasury received its full share of luxury resources. They often framed the pearl harvesting industry as a state-owned resource that required strict discipline to prevent private poaching.
- **Local Tanka Community**: For the Tanka people, the reforms represented an existential threat to their traditional way of life, forcing them into a cycle of debt and physical danger. Many local oral histories characterize this period as one of exploitation by officials who had little regard for the sustainability of the harvest or the survival of the divers.
