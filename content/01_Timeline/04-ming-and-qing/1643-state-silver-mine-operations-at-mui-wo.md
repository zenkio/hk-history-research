## Background
During the late **Ming dynasty** (明朝), the demand for silver as the standard medium for taxation and commerce drove the imperial court and local authorities to exploit mineral resources in peripheral coastal regions. Lantau Island, known historically as Tai U (大嶼), contained geological formations yielding silver ore. In **1643**, the Ming administration authorized state-directed mining operations at Silvermine Bay, known locally as Mui Wo (梅窩), seeking to extract vital bullion amidst growing fiscal and military crises.

## What happened
The establishment of the state silver mine brought a sudden influx of miners, laborers, and imperial supervisors into a predominantly agrarian landscape. The intensive extraction process required significant resources and manpower, directly altering the local economy. Almost immediately, severe taxation disputes arose regarding the assessment and collection of levies on mineral yields. Friction intensified between the newly arrived mining workforce and the resident agriculturalists and villagers, who bore the brunt of the administrative pressure, resource competition, and social disruption caused by the state enterprise. These operations occurred against the backdrop of the collapse of the **Ming dynasty** and the establishment of the **Qing dynasty** (清朝), creating a chaotic regulatory environment for the remote coastal works.

## Significance
The state silver mining operations at Mui Wo illustrate the reach and limitations of late imperial fiscal policy in peripheral coastal territories like modern-day **Hong Kong** (香港). The venture highlights how state resource extraction frequently disrupted local stability, creating enduring social tensions between distinct occupational groups—miners and farmers—in the region. Furthermore, the episode serves as an important regional marker of the profound economic and administrative shifts that accompanied the dynastic transition of the seventeenth century.

## Perspectives

- **Qing and Imperial Administration**: Official imperial perspectives generally framed the Mui Wo operations as a necessary fiscal measure to secure vital bullion for state coffers, viewing resistance from locals and miners primarily as administrative challenges or tax evasion.
