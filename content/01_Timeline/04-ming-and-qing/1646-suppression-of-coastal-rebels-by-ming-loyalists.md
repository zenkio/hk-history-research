## Background
Following the collapse of the Ming Dynasty in **1644**, the establishment of the Qing Dynasty met significant resistance in Southern China. The coastal regions of Guangdong, including what is now the Hong Kong territory, became a volatile frontier. Local gentry and Ming loyalist remnants, often operating with maritime connections, sought to maintain defiance against the advancing Qing forces. This period was characterized by a lack of central administrative stability, as the region became a strategic buffer zone for competing maritime and terrestrial military movements.

## What happened
Around **1646**, as Qing forces moved to consolidate control over the southern provinces, the coastal districts experienced heightened military activity. Various factions—ranging from loyalist military units to independent pirate groups—attempted to utilize the intricate coastline of the Pearl River Delta to harass Qing supply lines and administrative outposts. The resulting vacuum of power led to a cycle of raids and retaliations. Historians suggest that these skirmishes necessitated a defensive posture from local inhabitants, who were caught between the demands of loyalist commanders for resources and the punitive measures taken by the Qing military to suppress anti-Qing sentiment. The volatility of the era caused significant migration, as many villagers abandoned vulnerable coastal settlements to seek refuge in the mountainous interior or on fortified islands.

## Significance
The instability of this period set the stage for the **Great Clearance** (*Qianjie Ling* 遷界令), a formal policy enacted by the Qing government several decades later to cut off maritime support for the Ming loyalist **Zheng Chenggong** (鄭成功) based in Taiwan. While the events of **1646** were localized, they demonstrated the strategic difficulty of controlling the Hong Kong peninsula’s complex topography. The displacement of the population during this time left lasting impacts on the demographic composition of the New Territories, as many clans were forced to reorganize their landholdings and community structures in the aftermath of the transition.

## Perspectives

- **Qing/Beijing Perspective**: Traditional Qing historical narratives often frame these events as the necessary restoration of order against 'rebellious' elements (*dao* 盜). The state viewed the coastal instability as a existential threat to the unification of the empire, justifying strict measures to sever local ties with maritime loyalists.
- **Local Hong Kong and New Territories Clans**: For the indigenous inhabitants, the period is primarily remembered as a time of immense survival struggle. Local genealogical records (*zupu* 族譜) often emphasize the disruption caused by both roaming militias and official government decrees, highlighting the endurance of clans that maintained their lineage through mass relocation.
