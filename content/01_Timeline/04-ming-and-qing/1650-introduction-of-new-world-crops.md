## Background
Prior to the **17th century**, the agricultural economy of the Pearl River Delta and the coastal areas of present-day Hong Kong relied primarily on traditional East Asian crops, such as rice and various root vegetables. Cultivation was largely confined to low-lying alluvial plains and valley floors where water management was predictable. The rugged, acidic soils of Hong Kong's hillsides remained largely unproductive, serving mainly as foraging grounds or defensive buffers during the turbulent transition between the Ming (明朝) and Qing (清朝) dynasties.

## What happened
Between **1650 and 1700**, a major shift occurred as maritime trade networks brought New World crops—specifically maize (corn), sweet potatoes, and chilies—into Southern China via Spanish and Portuguese trading ports. These crops were remarkably resilient and capable of thriving in poor, dry, or sloping soil conditions that were unsuitable for traditional paddy rice. Historical records from the era of the **Great Clearance** (遷界令, 1661–1669) show that as displaced coastal populations returned to their lands, they adopted these new varieties to supplement their diets. The ability to grow these crops on hillside terraces provided a vital hedge against the frequent famines and the extreme instability brought on by the coastal evacuation orders.

## Significance
This botanical introduction fundamentally changed the carrying capacity of the land. By allowing communities to extend farming from the valley floors up into the mountainous interior, New World crops facilitated the expansion of human settlements into previously uninhabited areas of the New Territories. The integration of these crops allowed for more intensive land use, which supported a growing population despite the environmental constraints of the region. This transition not only stabilized local food security but also arguably altered the landscape, as systematic terracing became a permanent feature of the rural topography.

## Perspectives

- **Qing State Perspective**: The Qing government viewed the cultivation of these crops as a sign of successful frontier management and population stabilization following the chaos of the Ming-Qing transition. Officials encouraged the adoption of these hardy plants to prevent further social unrest by ensuring that land could provide sustenance for displaced refugees.
- **Local New Territories Communities**: For the indigenous lineages, the adoption of these crops was a pragmatic survival strategy during a period of state-mandated displacement. Families prioritized crops that required less irrigation and provided high caloric returns, ensuring the survival of their clans while they rebuilt their ancestral villages.
