## Background

Following the establishment of the Qing dynasty (大清) in **1644**, loyalists of the preceding Ming dynasty continued armed resistance from southern coastal bases and the island of Taiwan (臺灣). **Koxinga** (Zheng Chenggong, 鄭成功) posed a particularly significant maritime threat to Qing rule. To neutralize this threat, the Qing court determined that cutting off the rebels' logistical support, financial resources, and intelligence networks derived from the mainland population was necessary. 

## What happened

Beginning in **1661**, the imperial government promulgated the Great Clearance (遷海令), also known as the coastal evacuation. The policy mandated that all inhabitants living along the southeastern coast of China, including the area comprising modern-day Hong Kong, evacuate 50 li (approximately 25 kilometers) inland. Troops destroyed homes, seized lands, and erected boundary markers. Anyone caught remaining in the prohibited zone faced execution. The harsh enforcement transformed fertile agricultural and fishing communities into desolate frontier zones.

## Significance

The Great Clearance devastated the local society and economy of the Hong Kong region, dispersing indigenous lineages and halting maritime trade for years. When the evacuation order was finally lifted in **1669**, many original inhabitants did not return, and those who did found their lands overgrown and ruined. The policy remains a critical watershed in the demographic and social history of the region, fundamentally reshaping lineage settlement patterns in the New Territories.

## Perspectives

- **Qing Court Perspective**: From the perspective of Beijing, the evacuation was a necessary and legitimate military strategy to suppress southern rebels and secure the nascent dynasty against external threats.
- **Local Hong Kong and New Territories Communities**: Local lineages view the edict as a catastrophic disruption that caused immense suffering, displacement, and the destruction of ancestral heritage, leaving generational trauma deeply embedded in local oral history.
