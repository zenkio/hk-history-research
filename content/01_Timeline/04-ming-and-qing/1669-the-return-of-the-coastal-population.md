## Background
In **1661**, at the height of the struggle against the Ming loyalist **Koxinga** (鄭成功), the Qing government issued the Great Clearance order. This radical policy aimed to deny the rebel forces supplies and support by forcibly depopulating the coastal areas of southern China, including present-day Hong Kong. For nearly eight years, the coastal belt was rendered a wasteland. Residents were compelled to move 50 li inland, leaving behind fields and ancestral homes, which were often looted or reclaimed by nature.

## What happened
By **1669**, the military necessity for the clearance had diminished, and the Qing authorities formally lifted the ban, allowing survivors to return to their lands. However, the return was far from straightforward. The land had been abandoned for almost a decade, leading to the loss of property titles and the degradation of irrigation systems. As the original residents—often associated with the Punti (本地) or "local" lineages—began to return, they found that the government, eager to stabilize the region and collect taxes, encouraged the migration of **Hakka** (客家) settlers from inland Guangdong and beyond to cultivate the vacant plots. This influx created an immediate demographic shift that often led to territorial disputes between the long-established lineages and the new arrivals.

## Significance
The return to the coast fundamentally altered the social fabric of the New Territories. The resulting competition for fertile land and water rights fostered a distinct social hierarchy and occasional inter-group friction that would persist for generations. Historians note that this era established the "dual" nature of rural Hong Kong society, defined by the relationship between the Punti people and the Hakka. While the Qing state viewed the resettlement as a success in terms of agricultural restoration and tax revenue, the local experience was characterized by the trauma of displacement and the necessity of navigating complex land rights under the scrutiny of an expanding Qing administrative bureaucracy.

## Perspectives

- **Qing/Beijing administrative view**: The state viewed the resettlement as a vital tactical and economic success. By actively facilitating the movement of new settlers to the coast, the Qing government successfully solidified its control over southern borderlands and restored tax bases that had been devastated by the preceding conflict.
- **Local indigenous lineages (Punti)**: For the established clans, the return was marred by the loss of historical land boundaries and the frustration of sharing their ancestral territory with the Hakka. They often viewed the newcomers as encroachments sanctioned by a distant, insensitive government that did not respect local lineage precedence.
- **Hakka settlers**: For the Hakka, the movement was an opportunity to acquire land and livelihoods in a region prioritized for development by the Qing authorities. They often maintain that their contribution to the agricultural development and economic vitality of the coastal region has historically been undervalued or marginalized in regional narratives.
