## Background
In the mid-17th century, the coastal regions of Guangdong were subjected to the **Great Clearance** (遷界令), an imperial mandate starting in **1661** that forced inhabitants to move 50 li (approximately 25 kilometers) inland to deny resources to anti-Qing naval forces under **Zheng Chenggong** (鄭成功). This policy devastated the Pearl River Delta, leaving the coastal areas of modern-day Hong Kong largely depopulated for nearly a decade. When the order was rescinded in **1669**, the Qing government encouraged resettlement through tax incentives and land grants to restore agricultural production.

## What happened
Upon returning to their former lands or settling in newly opened areas around **1675**, the **Weitou** clans—the earliest wave of Han Chinese settlers in the New Territories—prioritized the construction of ancestral halls (宗祠). These structures served as the physical embodiment of the lineage. By enshrining the names of common ancestors, these clans established a clear, legally recognized claim to specific plots of land. These halls were not merely religious centers; they functioned as local administration buildings, schools, and courtrooms where clan elders mediated disputes and managed the collective resources of the lineage.

## Significance
The proliferation of these halls was a strategy for survival and territorial marking. By formalizing their lineages through these architectural landmarks, the clans created an indelible record of their sovereignty over the land. This helped them defend their holdings against the claims of later migrant groups—such as the **Hakka** (客家) people—who arrived in significant numbers throughout the 18th century. The ancestral hall became the defining feature of the 'walled village' (圍村) culture, cementing a socio-political structure that persisted through the Qing Dynasty and influenced colonial-era land policy.

## Perspectives

- **New Territories Indigenous Inhabitants (Heung Yee Kuk)**: The establishment of ancestral halls is viewed as an ancestral right to land and a historical validation of their status as the 'original inhabitants' (原居民) of the region. They emphasize these halls as evidence of a continuous, uninterrupted cultural lineage that predates the British colonial presence.
- **Qing State Perspective**: From the perspective of the Qing imperial administration, these lineage structures were convenient tools for local governance and tax collection. By facilitating the organization of clans, the state could more effectively manage the frontier regions and ensure internal stability through the 'baojia' (保甲) collective responsibility system.
- **Academic/Historical Scholarship**: Modern historians often view the construction of these halls as a defensive reaction to the demographic instability following the Great Clearance. They argue that the formalization of lineage was less about ancient tradition and more about the strategic necessity of asserting land rights in a post-depopulation frontier economy.
