## Background

Following the stabilization of Qing rule over southern China and the formal revocation of the draconian Great Clearance (遷界) in **1684** (**Kangxi** 23rd year), coastal populations were permitted to return to their lands and rebuild their livelihoods. To re-establish imperial control over maritime traffic, suppress coastal smuggling, and generate revenue from revived trade routes, the Qing court moved to set up official customs monitoring posts along the Guangdong coast.

## What happened

In **1685** (**Kangxi** 24th year), the imperial government established the Kowloon Customs Station (九龍關) in the strategic area of present-day Kowloon. Operating under the overarching authority of the Guangdong Maritime Customs (粵海關), this station was tasked with inspecting merchant vessels, levying duties on goods entering and leaving the Pearl River Delta, and monitoring unauthorized maritime movements. The station utilized local geography to oversee junk traffic navigating the waters between Hong Kong Island and the Kowloon Peninsula.

## Significance

The creation of the Kowloon Customs Station represented an early effort by the Qing state to formalize administrative and fiscal oversight in the territory that would later become Hong Kong. While initially serving traditional imperial fiscal and security functions, the site and its later iterations would become focal points of jurisdictional disputes between British colonial authorities and Qing officials in the late 19th century.

## Perspectives

- **Qing Imperial Administration**: Viewed the customs station as a vital fiscal and defensive instrument necessary to assert sovereignty, collect legitimate maritime revenue, and prevent illicit trade and anti-Qing subversive activities along the coastline.
- **Local Coastal Communities**: Navigated the presence of the customs station pragmatically, often balancing compliance with traditional smuggling networks that had persisted through periods of maritime bans and border shifts.
