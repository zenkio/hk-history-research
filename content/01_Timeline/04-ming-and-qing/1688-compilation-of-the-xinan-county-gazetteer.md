## Background
During the early **Qing dynasty** (大清), the territory comprising modern Hong Kong and Shenzhen was administered under **Xin'an County** (新安縣), which had been established in 1573 during the Ming dynasty. Following the devastating coastal evacuations and the Great Clearance policy of the late seventeenth century, the Kangxi Emperor permitted resettlement. As the region gradually repopulated, local officials recognized the administrative and social necessity of documenting the geography, population, and tax base of the recovering border county.

## What happened
In **1688** (the 27th year of the Kangxi reign), the first comprehensive surviving edition of the **Xin'an County Gazetteer** (新安縣志) was compiled and published under the direction of county magistrate **Lu Xizhe** (魯希哲) and local scholar **Wang Yumai** (王煜寀). This text served as an official government compendium, detailing local topography, administrative divisions, military defense installations, tax registers, ancestral clans, and indigenous customs across the county, including areas that now form the Hong Kong New Territories.

## Significance
The 1688 gazetteer remains an irreplaceable historical anchor for the study of southern Guangdong and Hong Kong. By freezing local social structures and lineage distributions into print, the text offers modern historians a rare baseline for understanding how lineages established themselves in the region after the disruptions of the Great Clearance. It bridges the gap between mythologized clan origin stories and verifiable imperial administration.

## Perspectives

- **Qing Imperial Administration**: From the perspective of Beijing and provincial authorities, the gazetteer was an essential tool for tax collection, social control, and bureaucratic integration of a historically turbulent and pirate-plagued southern frontier.
- **Local New Territories Lineages**: For indigenous clans of the region, the gazetteer is often viewed as a foundational document that legitimized their ancestral land rights, local hegemony, and integration into the imperial scholar-gentry culture.
- **Modern Historiography**: Contemporary scholars analyze the text with caution, noting that gazetteers primarily reflect the interests of the literate elite and imperial bureaucracy, potentially marginalizing minority groups, boat-dwelling populations, and non-dominant lineages.
