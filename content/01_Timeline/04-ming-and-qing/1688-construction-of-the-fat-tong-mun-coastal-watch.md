## Background
Following the lifting of the **Great Clearance (遷界令)** in **1683**, the Qing dynasty sought to restore order and re-establish revenue collection along the southern coast. The region around the **Lyemun Pass (鯉魚門)** and the entrance to **Fat Tong Mun (佛堂門)** had become notorious for piracy and smuggling during the mid-17th century. As maritime trade increased, the Qing authorities recognized the need for a formal military presence to secure these strategic bottlenecks, which served as vital navigational points for merchant vessels entering the **Pearl River Delta**.

## What happened
In **1688**, the Qing administration formalised the construction of a coastal watch and surveillance station at **Fat Tong Mun**. This location was selected for its commanding view of the sea lanes, allowing soldiers to monitor ship movements and signal alarms to nearby garrisons. Historical accounts suggest that this station was part of a broader network of coastal defensive installations intended to replace the informal protection previously provided by local communities or unchecked maritime forces. While the physical structure is often described as a watch post, the exact dimensions and architectural details remain a subject of archaeological debate, as the site has been significantly modified over subsequent centuries.

## Significance
The establishment of the **Fat Tong Mun Coastal Watch** marked an important step in the administrative integration of the region into the Qing coastal defense system. By providing official navigational aids and surveillance, the government aimed to exert state authority over maritime space that had previously existed beyond the reach of the bureaucracy. Modern scholars suggest this move not only addressed the immediate threat of piracy but also reinforced the Qing government’s geopolitical claim over the southern maritime borders. This era represents a transition from a decentralized coastal environment to one defined by centralized state oversight, impacting trade patterns and the security of the local population residing along the coastline.

## Perspectives

- **Qing/Beijing official historiography**: The establishment of the watch station is viewed as a necessary act of statecraft to stabilize the region, protect law-abiding merchants, and exercise sovereign control over the coastal periphery. It is presented as a successful manifestation of the central government’s commitment to re-establishing order after the chaos of the Ming-Qing transition.
- **Local New Territories communities**: Local oral histories often emphasize the strain that Qing military expansion placed on coastal inhabitants, who were caught between state mandates and the realities of maritime survival.
