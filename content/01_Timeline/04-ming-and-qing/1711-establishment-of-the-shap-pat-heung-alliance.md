## Background
During the early Qing dynasty, the New Territories region of Hong Kong experienced significant resettlement following the revocation of the Great Clearance (遷界) in **1669**. As new settlers and existing lineages re-established communities in the fertile Yuen Long plain (元朗平原), local power dynamics began to shift. The **Tang clan** (**鄧族**), who had settled in the area centuries earlier, held substantial economic and social dominance over the surrounding smaller farming communities.

## What happened
In **1711**, representatives from eighteen rural settlements banded together to form the **Shap Pat Heung** (**十八鄉**, literally 'Eighteen Villages') alliance. The coalition established its focal point around the **Tai Shu Ha Tin Hau Temple** (**大樹下天后廟**), dedicated to the sea goddess Tin Hau. By uniting under a shared religious and administrative umbrella, these smaller villages pooled their resources to negotiate land rights, manage water resources, and provide mutual defense against the encroaching influence and taxation demands of the dominant Tang lineage.

## Significance
The formation of the Shap Pat Heung marked a crucial turning point in the social organization of the New Territories. It demonstrated how fragmented farming communities could successfully leverage religious institutions to forge political solidarity and counterbalance established aristocratic lineages. The alliance evolved over the centuries from a defensive pact into a permanent administrative and cultural structure that continues to play a prominent role in local governance and traditional festivals, such as the Yuen Long Tin Hau Festival, to the present day.

## Perspectives

- **Local New Territories Communities**: Indigenous villagers view the alliance as a triumphant assertion of grassroots unity and community self-determination against the overreach of powerful, monopolistic landlords.
- **Later Historical Scholarship**: Modern historians analyze the formation of rural alliances like Shap Pat Heung as pragmatic socio-economic adaptations to weak imperial policing and intense competition for scarce arable land in the Qing frontier.
