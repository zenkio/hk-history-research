## Background
Following the devastating Coastal Evacuation (遷界) of the early Qing dynasty and the subsequent repopulation policies initiated around **1684**, the rural society of the Hong Kong region underwent significant restructuring. As newcomers and established indigenous families cleared the land for agriculture, localized power dynamics began to shift. Powerful multi-village lineages, such as the Tang (鄧) clan of Kam Tin (錦田) and other prominent families, sought ways to regulate trade, secure their economic dominance, and integrate disparate farming communities into a structured regional economy.

## What happened
Around **1711**, the establishment of structured market towns marked a major turning point in rural commerce. The most prominent example from this era was the foundation of the Tai Po Old Market (大埔舊墟), traditionally associated with the Tang clan. These markets operated under the direct control and protection of powerful lineages, which levied taxes, resolved commercial disputes, and managed market stalls. By centralizing the exchange of agricultural produce, salt, and imported goods, these lineage-controlled hubs effectively bypassed smaller, independent farming settlements, integrating the rural economy under hierarchical clan authority.

## Significance
The rise of lineage-controlled market towns laid the institutional foundation for rural society in the New Territories for centuries to come. It cemented the socio-political hegemony of indigenous clans, who acted as intermediaries between local populations and the Qing imperial state. Furthermore, these market networks fostered a distinct regional identity among the inhabitants of the Pearl River Delta, blending commercial enterprise with traditional kinship structures.

## Perspectives

- **Local New Territories Communities**: Indigenous clans view the establishment of markets like the Tai Po Old Market as a testament to the organizational genius, economic foresight, and self-reliance of their ancestors in subduing a challenging frontier environment.
- **Later Historical Scholarship**: Modern historians often analyze these market towns through the lens of lineage expansion and political economy, arguing that control over commerce was a deliberate strategy by powerful clans to monopolize resources and exert social control over smaller farming groups.
