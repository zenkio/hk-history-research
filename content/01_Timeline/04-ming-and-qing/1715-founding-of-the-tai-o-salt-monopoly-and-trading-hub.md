## Background

Before the **18th century**, the coastal areas of modern-day Hong Kong experienced severe disruption due to the **Great Clearance** (**遷界令**) of the early **Qing dynasty** (**清朝**), which forced coastal populations inland to counter anti-Qing resistance. Following the rescission of the clearance edict in **1669**, the imperial government gradually sought to re-establish economic and administrative control over the marginalized coastal islands and estuaries. The muddy tidal flats and sheltered waters around **Tai O** (**大澳**) had long sustained informal fishing and primitive salt-panning, but lacked centralized fiscal administration.

## What happened

Around **1715**, imperial authorities moved to formalize control over the region's salt production by establishing a structured salt monopoly (**鹽場**). This administrative integration brought local salt panners and traders into the Qing fiscal network. Licensed merchants were granted rights to manage the collection and distribution of salt under official supervision, effectively turning the remote settlement into a regulated economic hub. Warehouses, wharves, and administrative offices were developed to facilitate the taxation, storage, and outbound shipping of salt toward the regional markets of the **Pearl River Delta** (**珠江三角洲**).

## Significance

The formalization of the **Tai O** salt trade marked a critical turning point in the integration of outlying islands into the imperial economy. By tethering local extraction industries to bureaucratic oversight, the state transformed **Tai O** from a peripheral, vulnerable fishing hamlet into a permanent trading node. This economic stabilization allowed the local population to grow, laying the social and demographic foundations for the long-term permanent settlement of western **Lantau Island** (**大嶼山**).

## Perspectives

- **Qing Imperial Administration**: Viewed the regulation of salt yards as an essential fiscal measure to secure state revenue, suppress smuggling, and exert administrative control over previously lawless maritime frontiers.
- **Local New Territories Communities**: Remembered the salt monopoly through oral traditions primarily as an era of increased state intrusion and taxation, which constrained traditional livelihoods while offering limited protection against coastal pirates.
