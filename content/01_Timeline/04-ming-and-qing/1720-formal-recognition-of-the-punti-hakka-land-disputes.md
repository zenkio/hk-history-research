## Background
Following the **Great Clearance** (遷界令) of 1662 and the subsequent lifting of the ban in 1669, the Qing government encouraged migration to the coastal regions of Guangdong to restore agriculture. Settlers arrived in two main waves: the **Punti** (本地), who occupied the fertile alluvial plains, and the **Hakka** (客家), who arrived later and typically cultivated the higher, less productive hillside lands. By the period of **1720-1750**, land scarcity and overlapping claims led to heightened friction between these two groups, particularly in the northern reaches of the region later known as the New Territories (新界).

## What happened
The disputes arose primarily from ambiguous land titles and the differing cultural practices regarding land tenure and lineage ancestral halls. As resources grew scarce, minor altercations over irrigation rights, waste land reclamation, and tax obligations escalated into broader territorial conflicts. During the **Yongzheng** and **Qianlong** eras, local magistrates, such as those stationed in **Sun On County** (新安縣), were frequently petitioned to adjudicate these conflicts. These administrative interventions represent the formal recognition of the Punti-Hakka struggle as a matter of local governance, necessitating state mediation to prevent further civil instability.

## Significance
The judicial handling of these disputes profoundly shaped the social geography of the region. By recording settlements and demarcating boundaries, Qing officials created a bureaucratic history of ownership that would influence local land holding patterns for centuries. These disputes highlight the complexities of internal migration in imperial China and the state's reactive role in managing diverse ethnic groups. While some scholars view these conflicts as inherent ethnic rivalries, others suggest they were essentially socio-economic contests for limited resources during an era of rapid population expansion.

## Perspectives

- **Qing/Beijing Administrative view**: The imperial government viewed these disputes as matters of 'law and order' (治安), focusing on maintaining tax revenue and social stability. Magistrates prioritized preventing armed conflict and ensuring that land remained productive for imperial state interests.
- **Local New Territories communities**: For the Punti and Hakka lineages, these disputes were existential contests over ancestral property and survival. Oral histories among many villages often emphasize the legitimacy of their initial land rights and the perceived bias of colonial or imperial magistrates.
- **Modern historical scholarship**: Contemporary historians often interpret these conflicts as a lens to view the 'frontier' nature of early Hong Kong. They emphasize that these disputes were not purely ethnic, but were driven by specific economic pressures and land tenure laws.
