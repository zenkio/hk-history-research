## Background
During the Ming and Qing dynasties, the coastal waters of Guangdong, including the area that would become Hong Kong, were inhabited by communities who lived primarily on boats. Among the most prominent of these groups were the Tanka (蜑家) people, alongside the Hoklo (鶴佬). Historically marginalized by land-dwelling literati and official bureaucracy, the Tanka were often classified as outcasts or mean people (賤民), legally barred from taking the imperial civil service examinations and subjected to social discrimination by the dominant Punti (本地) land lineages.

## What happened
In **1729**, the **Yongzheng Emperor** (雍正帝) issued an imperial edict addressing the status of the boat dwellers. The decree ordered local officials to permit the Tanka people to purchase land, build thatched huts on the shore, and register for household tax booklets (戶籍). This policy shift aimed to integrate the floating population into the formal tax and administrative structure of the Qing Empire, encouraging them to transition from a purely maritime existence to settled agricultural or coastal livelihoods.

## Significance
The edict marked a crucial turning point in the demographic and spatial development of the Hong Kong region. Coastal and island locations such as **Tai O** (大澳) and **Aberdeen** (香港仔) saw gradual integration between boat dwellers and land-based villagers. While many Tanka families continued their traditional fishing and maritime way of life due to economic necessity, the decree provided a legal framework for land-based settlement, laying the foundation for permanent coastal communities and altering local social hierarchies over the subsequent decades.

## Perspectives

- **Qing Imperial Administration**: From the perspective of the imperial court, the edict was primarily an administrative and fiscal measure designed to bring an unregistered, mobile population under state tax collection, population control, and legal jurisdiction.
- **Tanka Communities and Local Historians**: Local and community narratives emphasize that while the decree formally removed legal barriers to land ownership, deep-rooted social discrimination by land-dwelling Punti lineages persisted well into the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.
