## Background
During the early **Qing** dynasty, the central government faced persistent security challenges along the southern coastline, including piracy and unauthorized maritime trade. Following the revocation of the **Great Clearance** (遷界令) in the late 17th century, the coastal areas of **Hong Kong** gradually saw the return of settlers and the re-establishment of administrative control. To better monitor the waters surrounding the Pearl River estuary, authorities identified **Lantau Island** (大嶼山) as a strategic location for establishing a military presence.

## What happened
In **1731** (the 9th year of the **Yongzheng** (雍正) reign), officials proceeded with the construction of defensive installations in the Tung Chung (東涌) area. The fortification was designed to house garrison troops, store provisions, and mount artillery pieces capable of commanding the channel between Lantau Island and the mainland. While early military infrastructure in the area began around this period, historical records suggest that subsequent expansions and stone fortifications occurred later, notably in the **Daoguang** (道光) era in **1817**, leading to the structure that partially survives today.

## Significance
The establishment of the fort reflects the Qing dynasty's evolving strategy for coastal defense, shifting from forced depopulation to active garrisoning and territorial surveillance. It illustrates how the imperial administration sought to project power into the coastal islands and protect vital shipping routes leading into Guangzhou (廣州). Today, the site stands as a physical reminder of the maritime military architecture deployed during the mid-Qing period.

## Perspectives

- **Qing Imperial Perspective**: From the viewpoint of the imperial court, the fort was a necessary measure to suppress piracy, enforce maritime bans, and secure the maritime approaches to Guangzhou against foreign and domestic threats.
- **Local New Territories Communities**: For the indigenous inhabitants and fisherfolk of Lantau Island, the presence of the garrison represented both protection from pirate raids and an extension of state taxation and regulatory control into daily local life.
