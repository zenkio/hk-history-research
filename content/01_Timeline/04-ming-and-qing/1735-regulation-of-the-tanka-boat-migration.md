## Background
During the early **Qing Dynasty**, the coastal regions of Guangdong were subject to varying levels of state control, particularly following the **Great Clearance (遷界令)**, which had forcibly relocated inland residents to combat the southern loyalist forces of Koxinga. Following the lifting of the maritime trade ban, the government turned its attention to the **Tanka (蜑家)** people, an indigenous boat-dwelling community that had long lived and worked on the waters of the Pearl River Delta. Historically, the Tanka occupied a marginalized social status, often restricted from taking civil service examinations or marrying into land-based lineages.

## What happened
In approximately **1735**, under the reign of the **Yongzheng Emperor**—and continuing into the early reign of the **Qianlong Emperor**—the Qing court promulgated policies aimed at integrating these maritime groups into the formal administrative structure. These decrees ordered Tanka populations to register for household taxation and encouraged, or in some cases coerced, their settlement onto land. The state viewed the nomadic nature of the boat people as a potential security risk, as their lack of fixed residence made them difficult to tax and police against the recurring threat of piracy. While some families complied with the offer of land grants, others resisted, preferring the autonomy of life on the water. The enforcement of these decrees was inconsistent, varying significantly depending on the local magistrate's resources and the strength of the regional power structure.

## Significance
The **1735** mandates represent a pivotal, if partially unsuccessful, attempt by the Qing state to modernize coastal governance. By forcing the Tanka onto land, the imperial government sought to transform them from "nomadic drifters" into settled, taxable farmers. The legacy of these regulations is complex; while some scholars argue it was an effort to grant the Tanka basic civil rights and social mobility, others contend it was a mechanism of control designed to strip them of their distinct maritime identity and dismantle the social networks that sustained their economic independence. The persistence of boat-dwelling life well into the **20th century** suggests that these early imperial attempts to end maritime nomadism were met with significant cultural and practical resistance.

## Perspectives

- **Qing State Perspective**: The imperial administration viewed the settlement as a necessary step for social stability, aiming to incorporate 'unruly' maritime populations into the tax-paying agrarian economy. It was presented as a benevolent policy to raise the social standing of a disadvantaged group.
- **Tanka Community Perspective**: Many Tanka families viewed these mandates as an intrusion into their traditional way of life and a threat to their maritime livelihoods. To these communities, the boat was not merely a dwelling but the foundation of their identity and economic survival.
- **Academic Perspective**: Modern historians debate whether the 1735 policies were truly motivated by social equality or primarily by the pragmatic administrative needs of the Qing state. There is ongoing research regarding the extent to which these decrees were actually implemented in remote coastal areas of the New Territories.
