## Background
During the Ming and Qing dynasties, the coastal areas of present-day Hong Kong were largely inhabited by fishing communities, salt panners, and farming clans. Life along the South China Sea was perilous, threatened by frequent typhoons, rough waters, and piracy. To secure safe voyages and bountiful catches, coastal populations widely venerated **Tin Hau** (天后), the Goddess of the Sea and protector of seafarers. Following the disruptions of the Great Clearance (遷界令) in the early Qing period, the coastal population gradually returned, leading to the establishment and rebuilding of various local shrines and community temples.

## What happened
In **1747** (the 12th year of the reign of the **Qianlong Emperor** (乾隆帝)), the **Tai (戴)** family, who were prominent local fisherfolk and landowners in the area then known as **Hong Kong Village** (香港村), initiated the construction of the Tin Hau Temple in Causeway Bay (銅鑼灣天后古廟). According to local tradition, the family discovered a joss (an incense burner) floating in the sea or washed ashore, which they interpreted as a divine sign from Tin Hau. They initially erected a small shrine to house the artifact, which subsequently grew into a more permanent masonry structure supported by donations from neighboring boat people and villagers.

## Significance
The temple stands as a testament to the socio-religious life of early Hong Kong's maritime communities before British colonization. It illustrates how kinship groups like the Tai family anchored their social authority through religious patronage. Over the centuries, as land reclamation pushed the shoreline further north, the temple—originally situated right on the coast—became somewhat landlocked, marking the physical transformation of the Causeway Bay coastline.

## Perspectives

- **Local Hong Kong communities**: View the temple as a foundational site of indigenous maritime heritage, demonstrating the enduring protection of Tin Hau over generations of boat people and Hakka villagers. Local families traditionally organized annual festival celebrations around the deity's birthday to reinforce community cohesion.
- **Qing imperial administration**: Regarded popular local temples through the lens of social stability and orthodox state religion, occasionally incorporating sea goddesses like Tin Hau into official state recognition to pacify coastal populations.
- **Later scholarship**: Analyzes the temple's founding within the broader context of post-Great Clearance repopulation and the commercialization of the Pearl River Delta's coastal economy, noting how maritime cults helped integrate diverse migrant groups.
