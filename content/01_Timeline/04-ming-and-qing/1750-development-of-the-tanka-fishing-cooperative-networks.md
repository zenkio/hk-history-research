## Background
Following the **Great Clearance** (遷界令) of the early **Qing Dynasty** (清朝), which forcibly evacuated coastal populations to combat anti-Qing resistance, the subsequent repeal of these orders in 1684 encouraged a resurgence of maritime activity. The **Tanka** (蜑家) people, a distinct boat-dwelling community, emerged as the primary occupants of these waters. Unlike the sedentary agriculturalists of the New Territories, the Tanka operated outside the traditional land-based taxation and administrative structures, relying instead on autonomous vessel-based communities that moved according to seasonal fishing patterns and trade opportunities.

## What happened
By the mid-**18th century (1750–1800)**, the Tanka developed sophisticated cooperative guilds known as *hong* (行) or informal mutual aid networks. These networks served two primary functions: the regulation of deep-sea fishing grounds and the provision of essential maritime logistics for regional commerce. Because the coastal waters were often plagued by piracy—a significant issue throughout the late 18th century—these cooperatives functioned as defensive units, pooling resources to protect their catch and their boats. The cooperatives also managed the distribution of fish to mainland markets, acting as intermediaries between the offshore fleet and the coastal towns such as **Aberdeen** (香港仔) and **Shau Kei Wan** (筲箕灣).

## Significance
The role of these networks was twofold. Economically, they provided the logistical infrastructure necessary for the survival and growth of coastal settlements that relied on maritime protein. Politically, the Tanka networks presented a challenge to the **Qing** imperial bureaucracy, which struggled to regulate, tax, or police mobile populations. Scholars note that these networks essentially formed the backbone of the region’s early maritime economy, creating a template for the boat-based commercial interaction that would define Hong Kong's waters well into the colonial era.

## Perspectives

- **Qing Imperial Administration**: The Qing state viewed the Tanka as a 'floating population' that was inherently difficult to govern. Bureaucrats often characterized these groups as unruly or prone to collusion with pirates, leading to periodic, though often ineffective, attempts to force them into land-based registration.
- **Modern Maritime Scholarship**: Contemporary historians argue that the Tanka cooperatives were highly organized, sophisticated economic entities. They view the 'stateless' nature of these boat people not as a deficiency, but as a strategic adaptation to a maritime environment that ignored the rigid boundaries imposed by land-based imperial land tenure.
