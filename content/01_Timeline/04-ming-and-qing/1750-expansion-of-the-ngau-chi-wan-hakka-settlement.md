## Background
During the mid-Qing dynasty, specifically around **1750**, the coastal regions of Guangdong experienced significant demographic shifts. Following the revocation of the Great Clearance (遷界) policies in the late 17th century, the Qing government actively encouraged the repopulation and cultivation of coastal areas. Among the migrant groups moving into the Xin'an County (新安縣) region were the **Hakka** people, who traditionally settled in upland and hilly terrains that had not yet been claimed by earlier Cantonese-speaking Punti (本地) settlers.

## What happened
In the area known as Ngau Chi Wan (牛池灣), these incoming **Hakka** communities began intensive land clearance along the slopes. Utilizing advanced terraced farming techniques suited for hilly environments, they cultivated crops such as rice, vegetables, and fruit to sustain themselves and supply nearby markets. This expansion transformed the previously rugged and forested hillsides into an organized agricultural landscape. Over time, these settlements established permanent clan ties, communal spaces, and local governance structures distinct from the older lowland villages.

## Significance
The mid-18th century expansion into areas like Ngau Chi Wan represents a crucial chapter in the agrarian history of Hong Kong. It established a dual demographic and linguistic pattern in the region, where Hakka and Punti communities shared the landscape—sometimes cooperatively, sometimes competitively over land and water rights. Modern scholarship views this period as foundational to understanding the complex social topography of the New Territories prior to British colonization.

## Perspectives

- **Local New Territories Communities**: Local oral traditions and lineage histories often emphasize the competitive nature of land acquisition, recalling delicate negotiations and occasional friction between newly arrived Hakka upland farmers and established Punti lowland clans over scarce resources.
- **Qing/Beijing Administration**: Imperial records generally viewed the migration and land reclamation through the lens of taxation, social order, and the successful pacification and economic revitalization of previously desolate coastal peripheries.
- **Later Scholarship**: Contemporary historians analyze the mid-18th-century settlement patterns as an ecological adaptation, highlighting how Hakka terraced farming altered the natural environment and shaped long-term rural socioeconomic structures in the region.
