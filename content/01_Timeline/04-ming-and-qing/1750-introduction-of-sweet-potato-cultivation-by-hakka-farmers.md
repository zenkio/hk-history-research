## Background

During the mid-Qing dynasty, particularly around **1750**, the population in the coastal regions of Guangdong began to rise following the lifting of the Great Clearance (遷界) and subsequent government encouragement of land reclamation. As fertile coastal lowlands became crowded, incoming **Hakka (客家)** settlers increasingly occupied the rolling, acidic, and infertile uplands of the New Territories that were largely unsuitable for traditional paddy rice cultivation.

## What happened

Originating in the Americas, the sweet potato (番薯) had gradually spread through coastal Fujian and Guangdong during the late Ming and early Qing periods. In the mid-eighteenth century, Hakka farmers in the region widely adopted this hardy crop. Because sweet potatoes could thrive in marginal soils with minimal water and withstand typhoons better than rice, they quickly became a staple food source for upland farming communities across the territory.

## Significance

The widespread adoption of sweet potatoes fundamentally altered the agrarian economy of Hong Kong's interior. It provided a reliable caloric base that mitigated famine risks, reduced reliance on lowland grain imports, and allowed Hakka clans to establish permanent, self-sustaining inland villages. This agricultural adaptation supported a notable local demographic growth phase in the region prior to the British colonial era.

## Perspectives

- **New Territories indigenous communities**: Local oral traditions and lineage histories emphasize the ingenuity and resilience of Hakka ancestors in transforming difficult terrain into productive agricultural land through the cultivation of hardy crops like the sweet potato.
- **Agricultural and economic historians**: Scholars view the integration of New World crops into the Lingnan region as part of a broader imperial demographic shift, noting that sweet potatoes played a critical role in supporting surplus populations on marginal lands during the Qing dynasty.
