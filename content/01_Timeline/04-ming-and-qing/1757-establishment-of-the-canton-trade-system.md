## Background
During the mid-Qing dynasty, the imperial court sought to strictly regulate maritime commerce and foreign relations along China's southern coast. Prior to **1757**, European traders had frequented various ports along the Chinese seaboard, including Zhoushan (舟山) and Xiamen (廈門), leading to concerns among officials regarding coastal security, smuggling, and foreign influence. 

## What happened
In **1757**, the **Qianlong Emperor** (乾隆帝) issued an imperial decree confining all Western maritime trade exclusively to Guangzhou (廣州), also known to Europeans as Canton. Under this regulatory framework, known as the **Canton System** (廣州體制) or Thirteen Factories system, foreign merchants could only conduct business through a licensed monopoly of Chinese merchants known as the **Cohong** (公行). 

Because foreign warships and merchant vessels could not sail directly up the Pearl River without strict imperial permission and piloting, the sheltered bays and islands surrounding the modern Hong Kong territory—such as **Lantau Island** (大嶼山), **Kap Shui Mun** (汲水門), and **Macau** (澳門)—became crucial outer anchorages. Here, foreign ships waited, transshipped goods into smaller craft, and engaged in communication and provisioning before proceeding or returning to Europe and India.

## Significance
The consolidation of trade in Guangzhou fundamentally shaped the economic and geopolitical trajectory of the Pearl River Delta. While the system generated immense revenue for the Qing treasury and controlled foreign access, it also created severe trade imbalances that eventually contributed to diplomatic tensions between the Qing Empire and Western powers, culminating in the First Opium War. For the local waters of Hong Kong, this era cemented their role as an international maritime crossroads.

## Perspectives

- **Qing Imperial Court**: From the perspective of Beijing, the restriction of trade to Guangzhou was a defensive administrative necessity to maintain maritime security, prevent coastal smuggling, and regulate foreign barbarians through carefully supervised intermediaries.
- **Western Merchant and British Colonial Historiography**: Later British and Western accounts often viewed the Canton System as a restrictive monopoly that stifled free trade and necessitated diplomatic or military intervention to open China to broader commercial markets.
- **Local New Territories and Coastal Communities**: For local fishermen, salt producers, and villagers in the Hong Kong region, the surge in maritime traffic offered both economic opportunities through provisioning and smuggling, alongside the disruptions of heightened state naval patrols.
