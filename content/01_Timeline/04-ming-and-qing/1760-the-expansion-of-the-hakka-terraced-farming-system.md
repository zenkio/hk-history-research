## Background
Following the revocation of the **Great Clearance** (遷界令) in 1669, the Qing government encouraged resettlement of the coastal regions of Guangdong. By the mid-18th century, specifically around **1760**, significant numbers of **Hakka** (客家) migrants moved into the inland and hilly regions of the New Territories. These areas had previously been sparsely populated or left uncultivated, as the earlier Punti (本地) populations primarily occupied the fertile lowland plains near the coast.

## What happened
The Hakka people brought with them distinct agricultural practices suited for highland environments. Faced with restricted access to lowland plots, they developed a sophisticated **terraced farming system** (梯田). By carving the hillsides into successive horizontal levels, they were able to retain water and prevent soil erosion, turning steep slopes into viable paddy fields and vegetable gardens. This innovation allowed for the systematic cultivation of marginal lands that were previously considered unproductive. The expansion was gradual and localized, often occurring in rugged terrains such as those found in **Sai Kung** (西貢) and the northern hilly regions.

## Significance
The widespread adoption of these farming techniques fundamentally altered the demographic and economic structure of the region. It enabled the integration of mountainous interiors into the broader regional economy and created a distinct pattern of land use that defined the rural landscape for over a century. While the Hakka integrated into the social fabric of Hong Kong, the environmental footprint of their terracing remains a defining characteristic of the local geography. Scholars suggest this development may have acted as a precursor to more complex social organizations, as the management of irrigation systems for terraced fields necessitated community cooperation and water-sharing agreements among mountain villages.

## Perspectives

- **Qing/Beijing Administration**: The imperial government viewed this migration as a stabilization effort to repopulate the border regions after the devastation of the Great Clearance. Official records from this era generally focused on tax collection and the administrative categorization of new land as 'arable' to bolster regional productivity.
- **Local New Territories Communities**: Local oral histories often emphasize the competition for resources that emerged between established Punti clans and incoming Hakka groups. While the terracing allowed for expansion, it frequently led to territorial disputes over water rights and land boundaries that persisted for generations.
