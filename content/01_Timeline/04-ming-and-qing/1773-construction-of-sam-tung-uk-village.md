## Background
During the early Qing dynasty, the coastal regions of Guangdong underwent severe disruption, notably due to the Great Clearance (遷界) in the late 17th century, which forced coastal populations inland. Following the rescinding of these evacuation orders, the Qing government actively encouraged the resettlement and cultivation of abandoned lands. This policy attracted numerous settlers from inland provinces, particularly Hakka-speaking communities from Guangdong and Fujian, who sought arable land in the hilly terrain of the Xin'an County (新安縣) region.

## What happened
In **1773** (the 38th year of the Qianlong Emperor's reign), the **Chan clan** (陳氏), originating from Fujian via Guangdong, established the fortified village of **Sam Tung Uk** (三棟屋) in the **Tsuen Wan** (荃灣) area. The settlement was constructed as a defensive rural compound. Architectural analysis indicates that the village layout characteristically featured a symmetrical design comprising an ancestral hall, a central hall, and an entrance hall, surrounded by defensive perimeter walls and row houses. The name 'Sam Tung Uk', meaning 'three-beam house', refers to its tripartite structural layout.

## Significance
The construction of Sam Tung Uk serves as a key physical example of lineage-based Hakka settlement patterns in the New Territories. It illustrates how migrant clans adapted to security challenges in the region by building fortified communal dwellings. While initially an agricultural and handicraft-producing village, the site survived subsequent centuries of urban development, eventually being preserved as a historical museum after its residents relocated in the late 20th century.

## Perspectives

- **Local New Territories Communities**: Descendants and local rural committees view Sam Tung Uk as a testament to the pioneering spirit, ancestral heritage, and traditional communal organization of early Hakka settlers in Tsuen Wan.
- **Heritage and Preservation Authorities**: Government conservation bodies and architectural historians emphasize the site's value as a rare, well-preserved vernacular Hakka walled village that provides tangible data on traditional southern Chinese rural architecture and lineage structures.
