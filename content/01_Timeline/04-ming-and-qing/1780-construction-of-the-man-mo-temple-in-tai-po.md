## Background
During the mid-Qing period, the region surrounding Tai Po (大埔) in the New Territories experienced gradual economic growth driven by agriculture and local market trade. As settlements expanded and the population diversified through the influx of various lineage clans, the need for communal institutions to mediate disputes and provide spiritual protection became increasingly acute. Markets like Tai Po Hui (大埔墟) emerged as vital commercial hubs, requiring governance frameworks that transcended individual clan loyalties.

## What happened
Around **1780**, local residents and prominent lineages united to construct the **Man Mo Temple** (文武廟) in Tai Po. The temple was dedicated to **Man Tai** (文帝), the god of literature and civil officials, and **Mo Tai** (武帝), the god of martial prowess and loyalty (often identified with Guan Yu). By establishing a dual-deity temple, the community created a sacred focal point for the market town. The temple served not only as a place of worship but also as a civic institution where local leaders gathered to negotiate agreements, settle community disputes, and administer customary law under the watchful gaze of the deities.

## Significance
The founding of the Tai Po Man Mo Temple illustrates how religious architecture functioned as a cornerstone of local governance and social cohesion in Qing-era Hong Kong. While imperial authorities maintained overarching administrative control through district magistrates in Xin'an County (新安縣), day-to-day order in peripheral market towns was largely managed by local elites through such religious and customary structures. The temple reinforced collective identity among disparate agrarian and trading groups, laying institutional foundations that would persist through subsequent centuries of changing administration.
