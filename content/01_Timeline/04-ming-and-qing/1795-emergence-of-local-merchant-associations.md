## Background
Following the **Great Clearance (遷界令)** of the early Qing dynasty (1662–1669), the coastal regions of what is now Hong Kong underwent a long period of agricultural resettlement. By the late 18th century, the coastal security situation had stabilized sufficiently to allow for the growth of market towns. These markets served as vital nodes for the trade of salt, grain, and marine products, linking the rural population of the New Territories to the burgeoning maritime trade networks of the Canton System (廣州制度).

## What happened
Between **1795 and 1800**, historical evidence suggests a shift in the administration of local trade. Previously, trade was loosely regulated by village lineages. During this period, formal **Merchant Associations (商會)** or market guilds began to coalesce in strategic locations such as **Tai Po (大埔)** and **Yuen Long (元朗)**. These associations were formed by prominent merchant families to oversee market stalls, regulate weights and measures, and mediate disputes. They provided a centralized structure for taxation and protection against the pervasive piracy that threatened the South China Sea. While the exact moment of formation for these associations remains a subject of debate among historians due to the scarcity of surviving early records, the late 18th century marks the point where these bodies began to assert visible influence over local economic life.

## Significance
The emergence of these associations represents a pivotal shift from a subsistence-based agrarian economy to one integrated with the broader **Canton (廣州)** market. By formalizing market rules, these merchants created an institutional framework that could negotiate both with local Qing magistrates and, in later decades, with European interests. This development is often cited as the bedrock of the 'market town' system, which shaped the social and economic landscape of the New Territories for over a century. It suggests that, even before the British arrival, Hong Kong was already developing complex, self-governing mercantile institutions that operated alongside the official imperial bureaucracy.

## Perspectives

- **Qing/Beijing Perspective**: Imperial records often viewed these associations as necessary intermediaries for tax collection and maintaining order in 'troublesome' coastal fringes. Local magistrates were typically satisfied as long as the guilds maintained market stability and kept piracy in check.
- **Local New Territories Perspective**: For the indigenous lineages, these associations were seen as a defense mechanism to protect local wealth and autonomy from both encroaching pirates and over-reaching imperial tax collectors. The guilds were often perceived as extensions of lineage power rather than independent commercial entities.
