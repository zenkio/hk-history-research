## Background
During the mid-to-late **Qing** dynasty, the waters surrounding the Pearl River Delta were increasingly volatile. Smuggling, coastal unrest, and weak imperial naval enforcement created ideal conditions for maritime insurgency. Local coastal communities often had complex, shifting relationships with these seafaring groups, sometimes providing intelligence or supplies in exchange for protection.

## What happened
Around **1795**, **Cheng I** (鄭一) initiated a strategic unification of the disparate pirate fleets operating in the South China Sea. Rather than fighting over territory, he brought competing factions under a centralized leadership structure, eventually forming the renowned Six Fleets distinguished by color-coded flags. These allied groups utilized the secluded bays, rocky inlets, and strategic anchorages of **Lantau Island** (大嶼山) and **Cheung Chau** (長洲) as primary operational bases, allowing them to effectively ambush merchant shipping and evade Qing naval patrols.

## Significance
The consolidation of maritime forces under **Cheng I** laid the groundwork for one of the most powerful pirate confederacies in world history. This syndicate severely disrupted regional trade and challenged imperial authority in the Pearl River Delta, setting the stage for the peak of pirate hegemony in the early **19th century** before internal succession struggles and foreign intervention eventually altered the balance of power along the South China coast.

## Perspectives

- **Qing / Beijing official perspective**: Imperial records viewed these maritime actors strictly as bandits and rebels who threatened state security, disrupted vital coastal commerce, and defied imperial authority, necessitating sustained naval suppression.
- **Local coastal and maritime community perspective**: Many villagers and fishermen in the islands viewed the confederacy through a more pragmatic lens, engaging in protection rackets, trade, or uneasy coexistence with the powerful fleets dominating local waters.
- **Later historical scholarship**: Modern historians analyze the confederacy not merely as criminal enterprise, but as a complex socio-political counter-society with its own internal laws, taxation systems, and geopolitical strategies.
