## Overview

During the Ming (**1368–1644**) and Qing (**1644–1911**) dynasties, the region today known as Hong Kong shifted from a quiet maritime periphery into a highly contested frontier of strategic defense, global trade, and demographic upheaval. Under the Ming dynasty, the imperial state established formal administrative control over the area, recognizing its value both for defense and trade. A major driver of the local economy during this early period was the expansion of the **Guanxiang** (莞香) incense trade, which exported fragrant resin from ports like Tsim Sha Tsui (尖沙咀) to mainland markets and beyond.

The sixteenth century brought Western powers to the region's waters. In **1513**, the Portuguese explorer **Jorge Álvares** landed at Tuen Mun (屯門), marking the first documented European maritime contact with South China. This encounter quickly turned contentious. Rising trade disputes and unauthorized military presence led to armed conflict, culminating in the **Battle of Tunmen** (屯門海戰) in **1521** and the **Battle of Xicaowan** (茜草灣海戰) in **1522**. In both engagements, Ming naval forces successfully repelled the Portuguese, asserting imperial sovereignty over local waters. To strengthen administration and secure the coast against growing maritime threats, the Ming government ordered the creation of **Xin'an County** (新安縣) in **1573**, carving out an administrative zone that encompassed modern-day Hong Kong and Shenzhen.

The transition from the Ming to the Qing dynasty in the mid-seventeenth century brought profound instability. As Ming loyalist forces under Zheng Chenggong (鄭成功, also known as Koxinga) challenged the new Qing regime from Taiwan, the imperial court enacted the draconian **Edict of the Great Clearance** (遷界令) in **1661**. This policy mandated the forced evacuation of coastal residents up to fifty *li* inland to sever supplies to loyalist rebels. Coastal watch posts were erected to enforce this boundary, leaving the Hong Kong region virtually abandoned. The clearance caused immense suffering, destroying local economies and forcing lineages to abandon ancestral lands. Following persistent appeals by local officials and gentry, the policy was rescinded in **1669**, allowing residents to return and rebuild.

To repopulate the devastated region, the Qing government actively encouraged **Hakka** (客家) resettlement. This sparked a wave of migration that altered the area's social makeup. The recovery of local society was documented in successive editions of the **Xin'an County Gazetteer** (新安縣志), first compiled in the late seventeenth century. As communities rebuilt, dominant clans constructed fortified settlements like **Kat Hing Wai** (吉慶圍) and expanded the **Kun Lung Wai** (覲龍圍) settlement to protect themselves against lawlessness, while newer Hakka migrants established villages such as **Sam Tung Uk** (三棟屋). This era also saw the rise of lineage-controlled market towns, such as Yuen Long (元朗), which became centers of local economic power.

By the late eighteenth century, the region was integrated into the global maritime economy under the **Canton Trade System** established in **1757**. However, piracy remained a persistent threat. To defend shipping lanes, the Qing state built fortifications like **Fan Lau Fort** (分流炮台) on Lantau Island. Despite these defenses, the late eighteenth century saw the dramatic rise of **Cheng I's** (鄭一) pirate confederacy, which controlled the South China Sea trade routes and frequently clashed with imperial forces.

## Key Themes

*   **State Policy and Coastal Security:** The region's history was continually dictated by imperial anxieties. The Great Clearance and the subsequent construction of coastal forts demonstrate how imperial Beijing viewed this maritime frontier primarily through the lens of security and defense rather than local economic prosperity.
*   **Demographic Transformation and Kinship Resilience:** The tension between the original Cantonese-speaking Punti (本地) lineages and the newly arrived Hakka settlers shaped the social geography of the New Territories. Strong kinship ties allowed these communities to pool resources, construct defensive walled villages, and establish dominant market monopolies.
*   **Maritime Frontier Dynamics:** Despite official imperial efforts to restrict maritime contact through trade bans and closed ports, the local population remained deeply connected to the sea. This connection manifested in both legitimate commerce, such as the incense trade, and illegitimate activities, including piracy and smuggling.

## How It Shaped Hong Kong

The Ming and Qing eras laid the social, cultural, and physical foundations of Hong Kong's New Territories. The division between Punti and Hakka communities, which persisted into the modern era, originates directly from the post-Clearance resettlement policies. Many of the walled villages built during this period remain inhabited today, standing as symbols of lineage solidarity and local heritage.

Furthermore, the constant threat of piracy and natural disasters fostered unique local religious traditions; for instance, the **Cheung Chau Jiao Festival** (長洲太平清醮) originated during this period as a ritual to placate the spirits of those killed by pirates or plagues. The local power structures solidified by dominant clans through lineage-controlled market towns also established the socio-political networks that British colonial administrators would later have to navigate.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Historians**: This perspective historically portrayed the pre-1841 New Territories as a lawless, neglected maritime fringe dominated by pirates and clan feuds. It emphasized the lack of effective imperial governance to justify the eventual British acquisition and development of the territory as an era of modernization.
- **Imperial Qing and Modern Beijing Historiography**: This view emphasizes the region's historical integration into the Chinese state, framing the Ming defenses against Portuguese incursions and the Qing anti-piracy efforts as vital patriotic defenses of national sovereignty. The Great Clearance is interpreted as a harsh but politically necessary military strategy to secure national unity.
- **Local New Territories Lineages**: Local clans focus on their ancestral resilience, viewing their history not through the lens of changing dynasties, but through their survival of the catastrophic Great Clearance, their subsequent reclamation of land, and the preservation of their lineage structures through walled villages and communal festivals.
- **Later Academic Scholarship**: Modern historians analyze the region as a highly dynamic maritime frontier where state control was frequently negotiated, contested, or bypassed by local lineages, merchants, and pirate confederacies, rather than seeing it as a passive peripheral zone.
