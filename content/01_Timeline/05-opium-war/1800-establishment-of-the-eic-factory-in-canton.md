## Background
At the turn of the **19th century**, the British **East India Company** (EIC) maintained a dominant position in the China trade, operating under the strictly regulated **Canton System** (廣州制度). This framework mandated that all foreign maritime trade be conducted exclusively in **Canton** (廣州) through a select group of licensed Chinese merchants known as the **Cohong** (公行). The EIC operated a 'factory'—a complex of living quarters, warehouses, and offices—along the banks of the **Pearl River** (珠江), specifically within the Thirteen Factories (十三行) district.

## What happened
By **1800**, the EIC factory had evolved into a sophisticated institutional hub for British commercial interests. While the term 'factory' implied manufacturing, it primarily functioned as a trade depot where goods such as tea, silk, and porcelain were exchanged for silver and, increasingly, Indian opium. The relationship between the EIC supercargoes and the Cohong merchants was defined by mutual economic necessity, yet it was frequently strained by conflicting legal systems, differing diplomatic expectations, and the British desire for free trade. The EIC, acting as a quasi-sovereign entity, navigated these tensions while attempting to protect British interests against the Qing Dynasty's rigid administrative controls.

## Significance
The solidification of this trading relationship established a precarious economic foundation. The EIC’s reliance on the Canton System created systemic frictions, particularly the persistent deficit caused by the British appetite for Chinese tea versus the Qing's lack of interest in Western manufactured goods. The subsequent introduction of large-scale opium imports as a way to balance this trade began to destabilize the social and economic order of the region. Scholarship suggests that the structural limitations of the Canton System made a diplomatic or military reckoning nearly inevitable. British merchants and policymakers increasingly viewed the restrictive nature of the trade as an obstacle to their expansionist goals, ultimately driving the shift toward seeking a permanent, autonomous base in **Hong Kong** (香港) to facilitate unrestricted commerce.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial/Mercantile Perspective**: The British viewed the Canton System as an outdated, artificial barrier to free trade that restricted the natural flow of global commerce. They argued that the arbitrary rules and corruption within the Cohong system necessitated the establishment of a British-governed territory to ensure the rule of law and secure trade routes.
- **Qing Imperial Perspective**: The Qing authorities viewed the EIC presence as a tributary-style arrangement, where foreign merchants were guests permitted to trade only under strict supervision to preserve social order and prevent illicit activities. Any British attempt to alter these regulations was seen as a challenge to imperial sovereignty and traditional diplomatic protocol.
