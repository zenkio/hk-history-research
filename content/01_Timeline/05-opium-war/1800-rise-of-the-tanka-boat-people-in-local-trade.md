## Background
Prior to the formal establishment of the British colony, the coastal waters of Hong Kong and the Pearl River Delta were dominated by boat-dwelling communities, primarily the **Tanka** (疍家) and **Hoklo** (鶴佬). These groups lived on traditional vessels known as *danka* (艇家) or *sampans* (舢舨). While land-based administrative records in the Qing Dynasty often marginalized these communities, they were the primary actors in the localized maritime economy of the region. Their existence was defined by a deep knowledge of the complex currents, shoals, and coastal topography of the Hong Kong basin.

## What happened
Throughout the **Canton Trade** (廣州貿易) era (1800–1842), foreign merchant ships from the British East India Company and other Western powers anchored at **Lintin Island** (伶仃島) and **Kap Shui Mun** (汲水門) to offload cargo away from Qing customs scrutiny. The Tanka and Hoklo people acted as the essential "middlemen of the sea." They provided vital pilotage, freshwater transport, and lighterage services—the physical movement of goods between deep-draft ocean vessels and shallow-water ports. During the **First Opium War** (第一次鴉片戰爭) (1839–1842), these boat populations were frequently caught between the conflicting demands of British naval forces, who required supplies and intelligence, and Qing officials, who attempted to maintain blockades and control over coastal trade.

## Significance
The rise of the Tanka in local trade represents a period where indigenous maritime expertise facilitated global commercial interests long before the arrival of colonial administration. Their ability to navigate the coastal waters of the **Pearl River Delta** (珠江三角洲) made them the de facto logistical network for the tea, silk, and opium trades. The informal nature of this maritime economy meant that much of their contribution remained undocumented in formal imperial archives, leaving the extent of their economic autonomy subject to ongoing historical inquiry. By the time of the **Treaty of Nanking** (南京條約) in 1842, the integration of these boat people into the nascent colonial economy had already begun, fundamentally shifting their role from independent coastal operators to participants in the new British maritime order.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial records**: Early British accounts frequently characterized the Tanka as opportunistic laborers or smugglers, focusing on their utility as pilots and suppliers for the Royal Navy. These records often overlooked the long-standing, autonomous internal trade networks that predated the arrival of Western ships.
- **Local indigenous perspective**: Tanka and Hoklo oral histories emphasize their role as master navigators and independent coastal dwellers who survived by utilizing maritime resources outside of traditional land-based feudal structures. Many community descendants view this era as a peak of maritime autonomy before the imposition of rigid colonial and bureaucratic borders.
- **Qing administrative perspective**: Qing officials viewed the maritime populations with suspicion, often classifying them as social outcasts or potential criminal elements due to their mobility and lack of permanent land-based registration. This perspective largely ignored their critical economic function in the regional logistics chain.
