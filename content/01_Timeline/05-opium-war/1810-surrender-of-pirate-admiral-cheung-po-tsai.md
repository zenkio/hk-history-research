## Background
At the turn of the **19th century**, the waters surrounding Hong Kong, Lantau Island (大嶼山), and the Pearl River Delta were heavily dominated by a powerful confederation of pirates. Commanded by leaders such as **Cheung Po Tsai** (張保仔) and **Zheng Yi Sao** (鄭一嫂), the confederation grew to encompass hundreds of junks and tens of thousands of personnel, effectively controlling regional trade and exacting tribute from coastal settlements.

## What happened
Faced with increasing naval pressure from the Qing dynasty (清朝) and the Portuguese navy, alongside internal divisions within the pirate alliance, negotiations for amnesty began in early **1810**. On **April 20, 1810** (Jiaqing 15th year, 3rd month, 17th day), **Cheung Po Tsai** formally surrendered to the Viceroy of Liangguang, **Bai Yanhu** (百齡), at Tung Chung (東涌) on Lantau Island. Under the terms of the surrender, **Cheung** was permitted to retain a portion of his fleet and was subsequently integrated into the Qing naval forces as an official, later helping to suppress other pirate groups.

## Significance
The surrender marked a major turning point in the maritime history of the Pearl River Delta, significantly reducing large-scale piracy and allowing Qing authorities to reassert control over regional shipping lanes. However, maritime banditry did not disappear entirely, and the event remains a pivotal moment in both local Hong Kong folklore and imperial administrative history.

## Perspectives

- **Qing Imperial Administration**: Viewed the surrender as a major military and diplomatic triumph of the imperial government's pacification strategy, transforming a dangerous rebel into a loyal servant of the state.
- **Local Coastal Communities**: Experienced relief from decades of relentless extortion and violence, though some oral histories and local traditions retained ambivalent memories of the pirates' deep local roots and influence.
- **Modern Scholarship**: Analyzes the event through the lens of political negotiation and pragmatism, emphasizing that the pirates' surrender was driven as much by internal confederation fractures and strategic self-preservation as by Qing military might.
