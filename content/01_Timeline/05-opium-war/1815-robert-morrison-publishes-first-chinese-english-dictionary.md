## Background

During the early **19th century**, European and American presence in the Pearl River Delta was heavily restricted by the Qing dynasty's Canton System. Foreign traders and officials conducted business through licensed Chinese merchants known as Co-hong (公行) and relied entirely on local interpreters. Learning the Chinese language was strictly forbidden for foreigners by imperial edict, and teaching Chinese to outsiders was punishable by death. Despite these formidable legal and cultural barriers, Protestant missionary societies sought ways to gain access to the Chinese interior and translate Christian texts.

## What happened

In **1815**, **Robert Morrison** (馬禮遜) successfully published the first volume of his monumental work, *A Dictionary of the Chinese Language*, in Macau (澳門). Printed at the East India Company's press, the multi-part dictionary project spanned several years. Morrison compiled the work by drawing upon extensive native tutoring, access to rare Chinese texts, and assistance from Chinese linguistic helpers who faced considerable personal risk. The dictionary categorized characters by radicals and included thousands of entries reflecting both classical literature and colloquial Cantonese and Mandarin usages prevalent in trade and daily life.

## Significance

Morrison’s dictionary fundamentally transformed the nature of cross-cultural communication in the region. By providing Western diplomats, merchants, and missionaries with a systematic tool to understand written and spoken Chinese, the publication dismantled the language monopoly previously held by local interpreters. This linguistic foundation directly facilitated later Western diplomacy during the First Opium War and the subsequent establishment of the British colony of Hong Kong, marking a turning point in Western engagement with East Asia.

## Perspectives

- **British and Western colonial perspective**: Viewed the dictionary as an extraordinary scholarly triumph that liberated Westerners from reliance on unreliable local interpreters, thereby enabling more effective diplomacy, commerce, and the spread of Christianity in Asia.
- **Qing imperial and traditional scholar perspective**: Generally regarded such Western linguistic efforts with deep suspicion, viewing them as intrusions into a closed empire and dismissing foreign lexical works as crude attempts to master a sacred literary tradition for commercial and political subversion.
