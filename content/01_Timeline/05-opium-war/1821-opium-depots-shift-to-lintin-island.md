## Background

By the early **19th century**, the trade of foreign opium into China had grown exponentially despite official imperial bans. Western and country traders, notably operating through the **British East India Company** and private firms like **Jardine, Matheson & Co.**, sought to bypass tightening restrictions imposed by Qing authorities at Canton (廣州). As smuggling networks expanded, the traditional method of small-scale coastal distribution became increasingly hazardous under stricter imperial enforcement.

## What happened

In **1821**, following renewed and more aggressive anti-opium edicts and naval patrols by Qing officials around the Pearl River Delta, foreign merchants altered their logistics. They moved their operations out of the immediate river ports and anchored heavily armed receiving hulks—former merchant vessels stripped of sails and converted into floating warehouses—permanently off Lintin Island (內伶仃島) in the estuary surrounding modern-day Hong Kong waters. These hulks served as secure offshore depots where large quantities of Indian opium could be stored offloaded from incoming clippers. Smaller native smuggling boats, known locally as fast crabs or centipedes, would rendezvous with the hulks at night to purchase and distribute the drug along the Chinese coast.

## Significance

The establishment of the Lintin depot system fundamentally transformed the geography of trade and security in the region. By anchoring these armed vessels just outside direct Qing administrative reach, foreign traders created an extraterritorial smuggling hub that operated with near impunity. This perpetual offshore presence heightened tensions between British commercial interests and Qing sovereignty, laying the groundwork for the systemic conflicts that would culminate in the **First Opium War (1839–1842)** and the eventual British acquisition of Hong Kong Island.

## Perspectives

- **Qing/Beijing Perspective**: Imperial authorities viewed the anchored foreign hulks at Lintin Island as an intolerable violation of national sovereignty and a direct defiance of imperial law. The shifting of depots closer to Hong Kong waters was seen as a dangerous escalation of criminal activity that was draining China's silver reserves and corrupting local officials.
- **British Mercantile Perspective**: Foreign merchants and private traders regarded the Lintin receiving ships as a necessary and pragmatic response to what they considered arbitrary and restrictive Qing trade regulations. They viewed the offshore depots as legitimate commercial enterprises operating in international waters to satisfy a high domestic demand in China.
- **Local Hong Kong and New Territories Communities Perspective**: For coastal villagers and fisherfolk in the surrounding waters, the arrival of the heavily armed foreign hulks and the proliferation of fast smuggling boats brought both economic opportunities in illicit lighterage and severe disruption. Local communities often found themselves caught between aggressive Qing patrols and heavily armed syndicates operating in their traditional fishing grounds.
