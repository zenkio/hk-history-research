## Background
In the early 19th century, the **Canton System** (廣州制度) strictly regulated foreign maritime trade. International merchants, referred to as 'factory' dwellers, were confined to the **Thirteen Factories** (十三行) located in the **Shamian** (沙面) and **Canton** (廣州) riverfront area. Under the oversight of the **Cohong** (公行), a guild of Chinese merchants, foreign traders were permitted to reside only during the trading season. This arrangement was designed by the Qing administration to maintain social order and control over fiscal revenue, though foreign merchants increasingly perceived these regulations as cumbersome and precarious.

## What happened
In **1822**, a catastrophic fire broke out in the vicinity of the foreign factories. The inferno decimated a significant portion of the European trading quarters, destroying vast inventories of tea, silk, and silver bullion. While the exact ignition point remains a subject of historical uncertainty, the fire spread rapidly through the densely packed wooden structures. The inability of the local administration to provide adequate fire protection or security for foreign assets left mercantile firms in a state of deep economic distress. The reconstruction of the area took several months, during which time trade was severely disrupted, forcing international companies to confront the logistical limits of the **Canton** hub.

## Significance
The **1822** fire served as a critical turning point in the strategic thinking of British and other Western mercantile houses. The disaster demonstrated that relying exclusively on the goodwill and stability of the Qing authorities was a high-risk venture. Consequently, major firms began to explore the potential of offshore anchorages that were beyond the direct oversight of the **Canton** bureaucracy. The vulnerability exposed by the fire is frequently cited by historians as a primary catalyst for the eventual shift toward the development of **Hong Kong** (香港) as an independent trading port, providing a more secure harbor that would eventually eclipse the traditional factory system.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Perspective**: The British viewed the 1822 disaster as proof that the Qing administration was incapable of protecting international commerce. This fueled the narrative that a 'free port' under British legal jurisdiction was essential for the security of global trade.
- **Qing/Beijing Perspective**: Qing officials viewed the factories as a controlled space meant to prevent foreign encroachment on the Chinese interior. The fire was seen as an unfortunate accident, and the subsequent mercantile restlessness was interpreted as a sign of foreign ingratitude toward the hospitality of the Emperor.
