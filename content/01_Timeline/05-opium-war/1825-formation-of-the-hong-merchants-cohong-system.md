## Background
During the early 19th century, the Qing Dynasty maintained a restrictive maritime policy known as the **Canton System**. Foreign trade was legally confined to the port of **Guangzhou (廣州)**, then known to Westerners as Canton. To manage this trade, the imperial government authorized a guild of Chinese merchants known as the **Cohong (公行)**, or the Thirteen Hongs (十三行). This system was designed to ensure tax collection, regulate foreign behavior, and maintain state control over essential commodities, most notably tea, silk, and porcelain.

## What happened
By **1825**, the **Cohong** had become the exclusive channel through which foreign merchants, primarily those representing the **British East India Company (英國東印度公司)**, could conduct business. All foreign ships were required to anchor at **Whampoa (黃埔)**, and foreign traders were restricted to the **Thirteen Factories (十三行商館)** area on the waterfront. Every foreign trader had to secure a 'security merchant' from the **Cohong** who would act as a guarantor for their ship’s taxes and legal compliance. The **Cohong** merchants—such as the prominent **Howqua (伍秉鑒)**—held immense wealth and responsibility, often facing severe punishment if foreign traders breached Qing regulations.

## Significance
The **Cohong** system functioned as both a facilitator and a bottleneck for international trade. For the Qing government, it was an effective tool for keeping foreign influence at bay and taxing the lucrative tea trade. However, as the 1820s progressed, the system faced increasing strain. Foreign merchants grew frustrated with the **Cohong**'s monopoly on prices and the arbitrary fees imposed by imperial officials. Meanwhile, the trade imbalance—driven by the British demand for tea and the limited Chinese demand for European goods—led to the rampant smuggling of **opium (鴉片)**. This economic friction, channeled through the rigid structures of the **Cohong**, became one of the primary catalysts for the diplomatic breakdowns that eventually culminated in the **First Opium War (第一次鴉片戰爭)**.

## Perspectives

- **British Mercantile View**: British traders viewed the Cohong as a restrictive, corrupt monopoly that artificially inflated prices and stifled 'free trade' principles. They argued that the system was an obstacle to modern diplomacy and sought to bypass the merchant guild to deal directly with the Qing bureaucracy.
- **Qing Imperial View**: The Qing authorities viewed the Cohong as an essential mechanism for national security and social order. By channeling foreign interactions through a select group of licensed merchants, the state could prevent cultural disruption and ensure that maritime trade remained under strict imperial oversight.
- **Contemporary Scholarship**: Modern historians often point out that the Cohong were not merely passive intermediaries but skilled international financiers who navigated complex legal landscapes. Scholars note that the system was a sophisticated, if ultimately unsustainable, attempt at managing globalization during a period of imperial isolationism.
