## Background
By the early **1800s**, the Qing Dynasty maintained a restrictive trade policy known as the Canton System (廣州制度), which funneled all foreign maritime trade through the port of Canton (廣州). The British East India Company (英國東印度公司) held a near-monopoly on this commerce for several decades. However, following the American Revolutionary War, merchants from the young United States sought to establish their own direct routes to China to satisfy domestic demand for tea, silk, and porcelain.

## What happened
By **1828**, American merchant houses, such as **Russell & Company** (旗昌洋行), had become firmly established at the Thirteen Factories (十三行) in Canton. Unlike their British counterparts, American traders were often private entrepreneurs rather than representatives of a state-chartered monopoly. They operated ships that were frequently faster and more agile than the heavily laden British East Indiamen. By leveraging neutral status during the Napoleonic Wars and focusing on high-value, low-volume goods, American merchants successfully carved out a significant share of the market. This period saw increased activity in the Pearl River Delta as American ships frequently docked at Lintin Island (伶仃島), an unofficial anchorage where goods were transshipped to avoid the strict scrutiny of Qing customs officials.

## Significance
The arrival of American competition fundamentally altered the dynamics of the Canton System. It created a more pluralistic, albeit still highly regulated, trading environment. From the British perspective, this competition was an unwelcome challenge to their established trade hegemony, while for the Qing authorities, the influx of diverse foreign actors complicated their ability to monitor and tax trade. Later historical scholarship suggests that this commercial diversification helped push the existing system toward a breaking point, as the combined pressure of British and American interests intensified demands for treaty-based trade rights, eventually contributing to the tensions leading up to the **First Opium War (1839–1842)**. Whether the American entry was a deliberate attempt to undermine the Qing monopoly or merely a byproduct of global capitalist expansion remains a subject of academic debate.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Perspective**: The British viewed American presence as an irritating encroachment on their long-held commercial rights and a complicating factor in their ongoing negotiations with the Qing authorities for more favorable trade terms.
- **Qing/Beijing Perspective**: The Qing court largely viewed all 'barbarian' merchants through the lens of the tributary system, perceiving the increase in American traders as an additional layer of administrative burden that required stricter regulation and containment.
- **Modern Scholarship**: Many historians argue that American competition acted as a catalyst for systemic change, highlighting that the diversification of commercial actors made the rigid Canton System unsustainable in the face of burgeoning global market pressures.
