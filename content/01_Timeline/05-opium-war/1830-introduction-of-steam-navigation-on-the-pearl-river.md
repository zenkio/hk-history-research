## Background
During the early 19th century, the **Canton System** (廣州制度) governed maritime trade between the Qing Empire and foreign powers. European merchant vessels were heavily dependent on seasonal monsoon winds, which dictated the schedule of the trade season at the **Canton** (廣州) factories. Ships typically arrived in the summer and departed by early spring, creating a rigid annual rhythm that constrained the volume and pace of commerce.

## What happened
Around **1830**, foreign merchants began testing the feasibility of steam-powered vessels in the **Pearl River Delta** (珠江三角洲). While the heavy, coal-reliant steamships of the era were not immediately capable of transoceanic travel, they proved effective for maneuvering through river channels and navigating against unfavorable winds. The arrival of these craft, notably the *Forbes* (associated with the house of **Russell & Company**), signaled a technical departure from the sail-powered junks and East Indiamen that had dominated the waterways for centuries. These initial experiments were often met with skepticism from local authorities and maritime guilds, who viewed the smoke-belching vessels as disturbances to the established order of the waterway.

## Significance
Historians widely agree that steam navigation fundamentally altered the strategic landscape of the South China coast. By decoupling maritime movement from the monsoon cycle, steam power increased the reliability of communication and cargo logistics between the **Canton** factories and the outer anchorages like **Lintin Island** (伶仃島). This technological evolution is frequently cited as a catalyst for the eventual British focus on acquiring a deep-water port capable of supporting larger, more advanced naval infrastructure. The ability to navigate regardless of wind conditions made locations like **Hong Kong** (香港) increasingly attractive to Western trading interests, as these areas provided the deep-water harbor necessary for the growing steam fleet. While steam was not the primary driver of the **First Opium War** (第一次鴉片戰爭), it served as an essential technological facilitator for the logistical advantages held by British forces in the subsequent decade.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial/Mercantile**: Viewed steam navigation as a triumph of industrial progress that overcame the constraints of nature to expand free trade. They interpreted the technology as a necessary evolution for efficiency and the security of trade routes in the Far East.
- **Qing/Imperial Authorities**: Regarded the introduction of steamships as an intrusion that defied the established maritime regulations of the Canton System. The vessels were seen as potential security threats that bypassed traditional customs monitoring and disrupted the local riverine economy.
