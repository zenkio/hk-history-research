## Background
For over a century, the British **East India Company** (廣東省東印度公司) held a royal monopoly over British trade with Qing China under the Canton System (一口通商). By the early **1830s**, mounting economic pressures in Britain, demands for free trade, and growing friction between Company supercargoes and Chinese imperial authorities led Parliament to reconsider this arrangement. The British government decided to terminate the Company's commercial privilege, effective in **1833**, necessitating a new mechanism to manage trade and diplomatic relations in South China.

## What happened
To replace the private corporate oversight, the British Crown appointed official superintendents of trade. The newly formed **Select Committee of Superintendents of Trade** was dispatched to Canton (廣州) with instructions to negotiate directly with Chinese officials and protect British commercial interests. However, the Qing imperial court, operating under the traditional tributary worldview, refused to recognize these officials as diplomatic equals, insisting they were merely head merchants (Taipans) bound by local Chinese laws and subject to the authority of the Viceroy of Liangguang (兩廣總督).

## Significance
The transition from corporate monopoly to Crown representation fundamentally altered the nature of Sino-British relations. By placing official state prestige behind British merchants, the committee structure reduced the buffer that the East India Company had previously provided. This direct involvement of the British state escalated tensions regarding extraterritoriality, trade imbalances, and diplomatic parity, setting the stage for the outbreak of the **First Opium War** (第一次鴉片戰爭) later that decade.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Perspective**: British authorities and merchants viewed the establishment of the committee as a necessary modernization to secure free trade, protect British subjects, and establish rightful diplomatic equality with the Qing Empire.
- **Qing / Beijing Perspective**: Imperial officials regarded the appointment of Crown superintendents as an arrogant breach of established tributary protocol, viewing the British representatives not as diplomats, but as commercial headmen who were expected to obey local Chinese laws.
