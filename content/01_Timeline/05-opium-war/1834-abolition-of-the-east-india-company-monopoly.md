## Background

For decades, the British **East India Company** (often called the **John Company**) held a royal monopoly on British trade with China, operating through the restricted **Canton System** (廣州貿易體系). Under this arrangement, foreign trade was strictly channeled through licensed Chinese merchant guilds known as the **Cohong** (公行), and foreign merchants were subjected to numerous regulations governing their residence and movement in **Guangzhou** (廣州) and **Macao** (澳門).

By the early 19th century, free-trade advocates in Britain increasingly viewed the monopoly as an outdated restriction on economic expansion. Industrialists and private traders argued that ending the company's exclusive rights would open vast new markets for British manufactured goods. Despite resistance from company directors who valued the stability of the managed system, the British Parliament enacted legislation to terminate the monopoly, taking effect on **April 22, 1834**.

## What happened

With the formal expiration of the monopoly, a flood of private British and European commercial houses—most notably **Jardine, Matheson & Co.** (怡和洋行) and **Dent & Co.** (寶順洋行)—entered the **Pearl River Delta** (珠江三角洲) trade directly. These private operators proved far more aggressive than the East India Company in expanding imports, particularly of Indian opium, in defiance of Qing prohibitions.

To manage this new era of free trade and oversee British subjects, the British government dispatched **Lord Napier** (律勞卑) to **Macao** (澳門) as the first Chief Superintendent of Trade in China. Napier attempted to bypass traditional diplomatic channels and communicate directly with Qing Viceroy **Lu Kun** (盧坤) on terms of equality. Qing officials, operating under the traditional tributary worldview, rejected direct and equal correspondence with foreign officials, leading to a diplomatic stalemate and a temporary halt in trade.

## Significance

The abolition of the monopoly fundamentally transformed the dynamics of Sino-British relations. By replacing a cautious corporate entity with aggressive private traders backed by the growing military might of the British Empire, the decision removed institutional restraints on trade disputes. The resultant surge in the illicit opium trade and the unresolved diplomatic friction directly accelerated the slide toward the **First Opium War** (第一次鴉片戰爭) (1839–1842) and the subsequent acquisition of **Hong Kong** (香港).

## Perspectives

- **British Commercial View:** Private merchants and industrialists viewed the abolition as a long-overdue victory for free trade and enterprise, essential for breaking stagnant markets and expanding British global commerce.
- **Qing Official View:** Imperial authorities regarded the influx of unregulated private traders and the refusal of British officials to follow established protocols as reckless disruptions to the tributary order and regional stability.
- **Modern Historical Scholarship:** Historians emphasize that the transition from corporate monopoly to private enterprise privatized the risks of trade while socializing the diplomatic and military costs, effectively laying the groundwork for gunboat diplomacy.
