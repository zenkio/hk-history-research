## Background
During the early 19th century, the Canton System (廣州制度) strictly regulated foreign trade. A critical imbalance emerged as British merchants sought tea, silk, and porcelain, while the Chinese market showed little interest in European goods. To bridge this trade deficit, British merchants increasingly turned to **opium** (鴉片), imported from British India. This shift transformed the trade balance, causing a massive drainage of silver (白銀) out of the Qing Empire to pay for the narcotic, which the government viewed as a threat to both state fiscal health and social stability.

## What happened
By **1836**, the Qing imperial court, under the **Daoguang Emperor** (道光帝), faced intense pressure to act. The drain of silver had caused the exchange rate between copper coins (used by the common people for daily transactions) and silver (used for taxes and large-scale trade) to fluctuate violently. As silver became scarce, its value rose against copper, effectively imposing a crushing tax increase on the peasantry and urban laborers who earned in copper but paid taxes in silver. In response, officials proposed various solutions, ranging from legalizing and taxing the opium trade to total prohibition. The government eventually moved toward stricter enforcement of prohibitions, aimed at curtailing the illegal outflow of silver to foreign merchants at the **Thirteen Factories** (十三行) in Guangzhou.

## Significance
The monetary crisis exacerbated the already tense diplomatic environment in southern China. The lack of a stable currency hindered regional commerce and impoverished many. Historians generally agree that these fiscal anxieties served as a catalyst for the hardening of the Qing stance against foreign traders, ultimately leading to the appointment of **Lin Zexu** (林則徐) as Imperial Commissioner in 1839 and the subsequent outbreak of the **First Opium War** (第一次鴉片戰爭). The instability of the 1830s demonstrated the Qing state's growing difficulty in managing a globalized economy while maintaining traditional isolationist policies.

## Perspectives

- **Qing Imperial Administration**: The court perceived the outflow of silver as a parasitic threat to the national treasury and a symptom of moral decay caused by opium. They viewed strict prohibition as a sovereign duty to restore fiscal order and social discipline.
- **British Mercantile Interests**: British traders argued that the silver drain was a natural result of the market demand for opium and the limitations of the Canton System. They viewed the Chinese crackdown as an interference with free trade and an escalation of diplomatic tensions.
