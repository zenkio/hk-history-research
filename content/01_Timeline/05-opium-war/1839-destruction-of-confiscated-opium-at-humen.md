## Background
By the early **19th century**, the British East India Company and other foreign traders were exporting vast quantities of opium from British India into Qing Empire (大清) territory to offset the European trade deficit for Chinese tea, silk, and porcelain. The resulting addiction crisis and massive silver outflow prompted escalating alarm within the Qing court. In **1839**, Emperor Daoguang (道光帝) dispatched the staunchly anti-opium official **Lin Zexu** (林則徐) to Guangzhou (廣州) as Imperial Commissioner with a mandate to eradicate the trade entirely.

## What happened
Upon arrival, **Lin Zexu** blockaded foreign factories and compelled foreign merchants—led by Chief Superintendent **Charles Elliot**—to surrender over **20,000 chests** of opium. Beginning on **June 3, 1839**, this massive cache of confiscated narcotics was systematically destroyed over the course of more than three weeks at Humen (虎門), near the mouth of the Pearl River (珠江). Workers dug massive trenches, filled them with seawater and salt to dissolve the opium, mixed in lime to destroy it chemically, and then flushed the resulting slurry into the river.

## Significance
The event demonstrated the Qing government's resolute administrative enforcement of its laws to domestic observers, but it severely aggravated diplomatic tensions with Great Britain. By seizing and destroying private property belonging to British subjects without compensation, **Lin Zexu** firmly closed the door to diplomatic compromise. The destruction at Humen is widely viewed by historians as a direct catalyst that made the **First Opium War** (第一次鴉片戰爭) inevitable, leading directly to British military retaliation and the subsequent acquisition of Hong Kong island.

## Perspectives

- **Qing / Official Chinese historiography**: Framed as a patriotic and righteous act of national sovereignty against foreign poisoning and illegal drug smuggling, led by the heroic anti-opium statesman Lin Zexu.
- **British 19th-century perspective**: Viewed the destruction as an unacceptable assault on free trade and the illegal seizure of British private property, which justified military reprisal to protect British subjects and national honor.
- **Modern and critical scholarship**: Examines the event within the broader context of global imperialism, silver economics, and the complex domestic politics of the Qing court's internal debates over legalization versus prohibition.
