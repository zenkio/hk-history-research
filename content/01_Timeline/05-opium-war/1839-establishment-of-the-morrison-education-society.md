## Background
In the early 19th century, Canton (廣州) and the surrounding Pearl River Delta region served as the primary nexus for foreign trade and limited cultural exchange between the Qing Empire and Western nations. Amidst strict trade regulations such as the Canton System (公行制度), foreign Protestant missionaries and merchants sought ways to deepen mutual understanding, disseminate Western knowledge, and facilitate communication. Robert Morrison (馬禮遜), a pioneering Protestant missionary and translator in China, advocated strongly for educational initiatives that could bridge the linguistic and cultural divides.

## What happened
Following Morrison's death, supporters established the **Morrison Education Society** in **1839**. The organization was officially formed by a group of foreign merchants and missionaries operating in the region. Its primary objective was the advancement of English and Western education among Chinese youth. The society subsequently established schools, notably the Morrison Education Society School, which initially opened in Macao (澳門) before eventually relocating to Hong Kong (香港) in the 1840s following the shifting geopolitical landscape of the First Opium War.

## Significance
Historians view the society as a foundational institution in the educational history of the region. By instructing Chinese students in both Western sciences and classical subjects, the institution produced some of the earliest Chinese graduates fluent in English, notably Yung Wing (容閎), who later became the first Chinese student to graduate from a United States university. While small in scale, the society's legacy reflects the complex interplay of missionary zeal, mercantile interests, and early educational cross-pollination at the dawn of the Hong Kong colonial era.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial and missionary perspective**: Proponents viewed the society as a benevolent and enlightened initiative that introduced modern scientific and linguistic education to Chinese youth, fostering mutual understanding and laying intellectual groundwork for future governance and trade.
- **Qing official and traditional scholarly perspective**: Imperial authorities and conservative literati generally regarded foreign-run educational societies with suspicion, viewing them as instruments of cultural intrusion and potential subversion operating outside the traditional imperial examination system.
