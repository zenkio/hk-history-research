## Background
In the summer of **1839**, tensions escalated dramatically between the British and the Qing administration in Guangdong over the trade of opium and issues of legal jurisdiction. Imperial Commissioner **Lin Zexu** (林則徐) enforced strict measures to halt the narcotics trade, demanding that foreign merchants surrender their opium stocks and sign bonds agreeing not to smuggle under pain of death. After initial standoffs at Canton (Guangzhou, 廣州) and the expulsion of Chief Superintendent of Trade **Charles Elliot** (義律) and British subjects from factories, the foreign community temporarily relocated to the Portuguese-administered enclave of Macau (澳門).

However, as Qing pressure mounted on local Portuguese authorities—including food embargoes and threats of military force—the governor of Macau requested that the British residents leave. This untenable situation left the displaced merchants and their families with no land-based sanctuary along the South China coast, forcing them to look seaward.

## What happened
On **August 26, 1839**, the British community officially evacuated Macau. Stripped of access to land accommodations, **Charles Elliot** orchestrated the relocation of hundreds of British merchants, sailors, and dependents onto a flotilla of merchant vessels, warships, and opium clippers anchored safely within the deep waters of the Hong Kong Anchorage (香港錨地) in Victoria Harbour (維多利亞港).

Living conditions aboard the crowded ships were cramped and difficult, compounded by the tropical summer heat and the constant threat of Qing fireboats or naval attacks. Despite these hardships, the floating community managed to sustain basic commerce and communication. Supplies were procured from local villagers in the surrounding islands and Kowloon (九龍) peninsula, demonstrating a pragmatic reliance on local Chinese traders who continued to provide provisions despite imperial prohibitions.

## Significance
The encampment of the British community in Victoria Harbour marked a critical turning point in the prelude to the First Opium War. Historians note that the successful occupation of the anchorage underscored the immense natural advantages and strategic maritime value of Hong Kong Island (香港島) as a defensible deep-water port. The logistical viability of operating entirely from shipboard headquarters directly influenced British war aims, ultimately leading to the demand for the permanent cession of the territory under the Convention of Chuenpi and subsequent treaties.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial perspective**: Viewed the evacuation as a necessary and resilient tactical maneuver in the face of arbitrary Qing despotism. The survival of the merchant community aboard the fleet validated the superior maritime capability of the British and highlighted the urgent need for a secure, permanent territorial base in the region.
- **Qing and Beijing perspective**: Regarded the floating encampment as an act of lawless defiance by unmanageable foreign traders who refused to submit to Chinese sovereignty and trade regulations. The withdrawal to the islands was seen as a temporary maritime nuisance that warranted continued embargoes and naval vigilance.
- **Local Hong Kong and New Territories communities perspective**: Experienced the foreign presence as an influx of heavily armed interlopers, yet local boat people and villagers often engaged in pragmatic commerce, selling food and supplies to the anchored ships despite the potential risks of running afoul of Qing authorities.
