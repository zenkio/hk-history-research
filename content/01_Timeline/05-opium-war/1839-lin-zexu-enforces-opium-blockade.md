## Background
By the early 19th century, the illegal trade of British-imported opium from India into China had created a severe silver drain and widespread addiction crisis within the Qing Empire. After years of internal debate between proponents of legalization and suppression, the Daoguang Emperor appointed **Lin Zexu** (林則徐) as Special Imperial Commissioner in late 1838 with a mandate to eradicate the trade completely.

## What happened
Arriving in Canton (廣州) in March **1839**, **Lin Zexu** immediately targeted foreign merchants and Chinese compradors alike. On **March 24, 1839**, he imposed a strict blockade on the foreign factories (Thirteen Factories), cutting off food supplies, water, and Chinese servants. Under intense pressure, the British Chief Superintendent of Trade, **Charles Elliot** (義律), ordered merchants to surrender their stocks to the Chinese authorities. Over the following weeks, more than 20,000 chests of opium were confiscated and publicly destroyed near Humen (虎門).

## Significance
The blockade and subsequent destruction of property dealt a severe economic blow to foreign traders and outraged the British government, which viewed the action as an infringement on sovereignty and free trade. This confrontation served as the immediate catalyst for the First Opium War (1839–1842), which fundamentally altered East-West relations and led directly to the ceding of Hong Kong Island.

## Perspectives

- **Qing Court / Imperial Administration**: From the perspective of Beijing, the blockade was a necessary and morally justified enforcement of imperial law to protect the health and economy of the populace against destructive foreign contraband.
- **British Mercantile and Colonial**: British traders and officials viewed the blockade as an unprovoked imprisonment of British subjects, an assault on private property, and a direct insult to national honor that required military redress.
