## Background

In the summer of **1839**, tensions escalated sharply between the British and the Qing Empire in **Canton (廣州)** over trade restrictions, legal jurisdiction, and the opium trade. Following the expulsion of British Superintendent of Trade **Charles Elliot (義律)** and the British community from Canton to **Macao (澳門)**, and subsequently to the anchorage off **Hong Kong (香港)** Island, supplies of food and fresh water for the British ships were cut off by local Chinese authorities under Imperial Commissioner **Lin Zexu (林則徐)**. 

## What happened

On **September 4, 1839** (**September 27th of the 19th year of the Daoguang Emperor**), **Charles Elliot** sailed to **Kowloon (九龍)** Bay with an armed cutter and several ship's boats, known as pinnaces, to demand the restoration of provisions. Accompanied by an ultimatum, negotiations with the local Chinese naval commander broke down over British demands to purchase food freely. 

Shortly after negotiations failed, hostilities commenced. British armed boats opened fire on a squadron of Qing war junks anchored off the coast. The engagement involved close-range artillery and musket fire between the agile British pinnaces and the larger, more cumbersome Chinese war junks supported by shore batteries. After a prolonged exchange that inflicted casualties on both sides and forced some Qing vessels to retreat, **Elliot** ultimately withdrew his forces without securing a permanent agreement for food supplies or resolving the broader diplomatic impasse.

## Significance

The engagement is historically significant as the opening military skirmish of the First Opium War in the waters surrounding **Hong Kong (香港)**. While tactically inconclusive—neither side achieved a decisive strategic victory—it demonstrated the vulnerability of traditional Qing naval forces to Western armed boats in coastal maneuvers and hardened resolve on both sides, paving the way for larger fleet actions later in the conflict.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial perspective**: British accounts framed the action as a regrettable but necessary defensive measure to secure vital provisions and fresh water for the civilian and naval population after local authorities unjustly embargoed basic sustenance.
- **Qing and Beijing perspective**: Imperial Chinese records viewed the engagement as a piratical and unprovoked assault by foreign interlopers who dared to violate territorial waters and defy imperial prohibitions against illegal trade and unauthorized landing.
- **Local Hong Kong and New Territories perspective**: Coastal villagers and local communities witnessed the sudden disruption of regional trade, fishing, and daily life as foreign warships brought the escalating geopolitical conflict directly to their doorstep.
