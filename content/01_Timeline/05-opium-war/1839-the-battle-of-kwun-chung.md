## Background

By late **1839**, tensions between the Qing Empire and Great Britain had reached a critical juncture. Following Imperial Commissioner **Lin Zexu** (林則徐) confiscating and destroying British opium in Canton (Guangzhou), British merchants and naval forces withdrew to the anchorage at Hong Kong and the surrounding waters. Demands for compensation, extraterritoriality, and secure trading rights created an increasingly volatile environment, with local provisions blockaded and naval patrols intensifying.

## What happened

On **November 4, 1839** (**1839-11-04**), a clash occurred off Kwun Chung (官涌), located on the Kowloon peninsula, between Qing naval forces commanded by Admiral **Guan Tianpei** (關天培) and British warships, notably the *HMS Volage* and *HMS Hyacinth*. Qing war junks attempted to enforce restrictions and assert sovereignty over the coastal waters, while British naval commanders sought to break blockades and protect their merchant shipping lanes. The engagement involved artillery exchanges and tactical maneuvering. While contemporary British accounts generally claimed a tactical success that cleared or dispersed the attacking junks, Qing reports emphasized the defensive posture and resilience of their naval units in confronting superior Western armament.

## Significance

The engagement at Kwun Chung served as a crucial military prelude to the First Opium War, demonstrating that hostilities were no longer confined to diplomatic disputes or trade embargoes, but had escalated into active naval warfare. It highlighted the widening technological and tactical gap between Qing coastal defenses and the Royal Navy, while hardening resolve on both sides. The battle underscored the strategic importance of the Kowloon peninsula and the surrounding anchorages, which would soon become focal points for British territorial ambitions and subsequent military operations.

## Perspectives

- **Qing Imperial / Beijing perspective**: Imperial authorities viewed the naval patrols and engagements as a righteous defense of national sovereignty against foreign drug traffickers and armed intruders violating maritime laws.
- **British colonial perspective**: British naval and commercial accounts framed the actions as necessary measures to protect British subjects, secure free trade routes, and counter unlawful blockades imposed by local authorities.
