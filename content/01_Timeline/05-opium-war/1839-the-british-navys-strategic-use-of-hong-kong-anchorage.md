## Background
In the early **1830s**, the British trade presence in the Pearl River Delta was strictly confined to the **Canton** (廣州) system. Merchants were restricted to the **Thirteen Factories** (十三行) and lacked a permanent base for their fleets. The waters surrounding the island of **Hong Kong** (香港) had long served as a seasonal anchorage for Tanka (疍家) fishing communities and occasional merchant ships seeking shelter from typhoons, but it possessed no formal colonial infrastructure or military facilities.

## What happened
As tensions between the British and the Qing administration reached a breaking point in **1839**—following the arrival of High Commissioner **Lin Zexu** (林則徐) and the subsequent destruction of opium at **Humen** (虎門)—British ships were expelled from the Pearl River. Seeking a safe harbor to refit and maintain communication lines, the British fleet moved to the southern side of the Hong Kong island. The deep, protected waters of the harbor, later known as Victoria Harbour, provided a secure environment for the Royal Navy to congregate. While the British initially utilized the anchorage for logistical support and as a bargaining chip for maritime security, the presence of these foreign warships fundamentally altered the local landscape, drawing the attention of both the British Foreign Office and the Qing authorities.

## Significance
The strategic realization of Hong Kong’s potential as a 'harbor of refuge' marked the beginning of its transition from a quiet maritime outpost to a center of global commerce and military activity. British planners began to view the island not merely as an anchorage, but as a defensible stronghold. This period set the precedent for the formal acquisition of the island during the **First Opium War** (第一次鴉片戰爭). By the time the **Treaty of Nanking** (南京條約) was finalized in **1842**, the military utility of the harbor had become the primary justification for the British demand for the island's cession, shifting the regional balance of power permanently.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Perspective**: The anchorage was viewed as a providential discovery of a 'natural harbor,' which the British believed was necessary to protect free trade and ensure the safety of their merchant fleet against Qing administrative hostility.
- **Qing/Beijing Perspective**: Qing officials viewed the British occupation of the anchorage as an illegal incursion into sovereign waters, interpreting the movement of warships as an act of piracy and a direct threat to the regional stability and traditional tribute system.
- **Local and New Territories Communities Perspective**: For the indigenous Tanka and Punti (本地) populations, the sudden influx of foreign warships disrupted established fishing grounds and the local maritime economy, causing significant anxiety regarding both resource competition and the erosion of local autonomy.
