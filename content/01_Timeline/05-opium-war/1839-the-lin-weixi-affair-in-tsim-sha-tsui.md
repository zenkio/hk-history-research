## Background
In the summer of **1839** (道光十九年), tensions between the British merchant community and Qing authorities in the Pearl River Delta were already reaching a breaking point over the opium trade and the enforcement of Chinese law. British Chief Superintendent of Trade **Charles Elliot** (義律) and Imperial Commissioner **Lin Zexu** (林則徐) were locked in a tense standoff at Guangzhou (廣州), with Qing authorities demanding the surrender of foreign offenders to local courts.

## What happened
On **1839-07-07**, a group of British and American sailors from anchored merchant vessels went ashore at Tsim Sha Tsui (尖沙咀) in the Kowloon peninsula. A violent altercation erupted, resulting in the fatal beating of a local Kowloon villager named **Lin Weixi** (林維喜). 

Commissioner Lin Zexu demanded that British authorities hand over the murderers for trial under Qing penal law, which included the principle of life for a life. **Charles Elliot** refused, citing British extraterritoriality and a deep distrust of the Qing judicial system. Instead, Elliot held a makeshift trial aboard a British ship, fining and imprisoning several sailors, but refusing to surrender any British subjects to Qing jurisdiction. He also offered compensation to the victim's family, though the legal standoff remained unresolved.

## Significance
The refusal of **Charles Elliot** to hand over British subjects enraged Qing officials, leading to further escalation, including cutting off food supplies to the British community in Macau (澳門) and eventually forcing their expulsion. This jurisdictional deadlock exposed irreconcilable differences in legal philosophies regarding sovereignty and criminal responsibility, serving as a critical catalyst for the outbreak of open hostilities later that year in the First Opium War.

## Perspectives

- **Qing / Beijing perspective**: Qing authorities viewed the refusal to surrender the killers as a direct challenge to imperial sovereignty and Chinese territorial jurisdiction, insisting that all persons within the empire's borders must be subject to imperial law.
- **British colonial perspective**: British officials argued that Qing legal procedures were barbaric and lacked due process, asserting that British subjects must remain under British jurisdiction and legal protection even while abroad.
- **Local Hong Kong communities perspective**: Indigenous villagers in the New Territories and Kowloon felt deeply vulnerable to foreign lawlessness and viewed the foreign presence as a threat to local safety and imperial protection.
