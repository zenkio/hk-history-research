## Background

During the First Opium War, British forces under **Captain Charles Elliot** (義律) and **Sir Gordon Bremer** occupied Hong Kong Island on **January 26, 1841**, following the breakdown of the Convention of Chuenpee. Seeking to establish administrative control and reassure the local population—predominantly fishermen, farmers, and stonecutters—the British authorities needed to prevent mass flight and secure local cooperation for the nascent colony.

## What happened

On **January 29, 1841**, **Captain Charles Elliot** issued an official proclamation to the inhabitants of Hong Kong. The document formally declared that the island was temporarily placed under British rule. To pacify residents and maintain economic stability, the proclamation guaranteed that the local population would be governed according to native laws and customs, assuring protection of private property, personal safety, and religious practices. It also declared that native inhabitants would be subjected to no higher taxation than what had previously been customary under Qing administration, though it asserted British sovereignty over the harbor and land.

## Significance

The proclamation represents the foundational administrative document of British Hong Kong, setting an early precedent for indirect rule and the accommodation of local customs alongside British legal frameworks. It marked the transition from a purely military occupation to the establishment of a civil administration, laying the groundwork for the island's rapid development as a free port. However, the document's long-term implications remain a subject of varied historical interpretation regarding the balance between colonial authority and indigenous rights.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial**: Viewed as a pragmatic and enlightened measure that successfully established the rule of law, protected private property, and fostered a stable commercial environment crucial for Hong Kong's early growth.

- **Local Hong Kong Communities**: Seen as a necessary survival mechanism that provided temporary security and protection from arbitrary British military requisition, while allowing traditional community structures to endure.
