## Background

Following the British occupation of Hong Kong Island in **January 1841** during the First Opium War, the colonial administration under **Charles Elliot** (義律) sought to establish a permanent urban layout to support trade and military administration. The rugged, steep northern coastline of Hong Kong Island presented immediate logistical challenges for establishing settlements, godowns, and administrative offices.

## What happened

In **June 1841**, British military engineers, supported by local Chinese labourers, began the difficult task of cutting through the steep hillsides and paving what would become Queen's Road (皇后大道). This initial path hugged the base of the northern shoreline, connecting various early settlements, military encampments, and commercial plots. The construction relied heavily on manual labour, utilizing traditional tools and techniques adapted to the challenging terrain.

## Significance

Queen's Road served as the backbone of early colonial Hong Kong, shaping the spatial development of Victoria City (維多利亞城). As the island's first major road, it dictated the orientation of subsequent property sales, commercial expansion, and urban infrastructure. While initially conceived to serve British military and mercantile interests, the road also facilitated the movement of the local Chinese population and merchants who migrated to the island to participate in the burgeoning entrepôt economy.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial**: Viewed the construction of Queen's Road as a necessary and triumphant engineering feat that established law, order, and modern infrastructure along an undeveloped and rugged coastline.
- **Qing and Beijing official**: Regarded the infrastructural activities on Hong Kong Island as illegal intrusions and unauthorized construction on sovereign Chinese territory following coerced military actions.
- **Local Hong Kong communities**: Experienced the construction through the daily toil of local labourers and stonecutters who performed the physical work, while also adapting to the new spatial boundaries imposed by the colonial administration.
