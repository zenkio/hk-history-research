## Background

Following the outbreak of the First Opium War, British forces under **Charles Elliot** (義律) occupied Hong Kong Island in early 1841. The local population consisted primarily of fishing, farming, and salt-producing communities spread across various coastal settlements and villages. To secure the island and prevent active resistance from the indigenous inhabitants, British authorities recognized the necessity of stabilizing local administration and reassuring the populace regarding their safety and livelihoods.

## What happened

On **February 2, 1841**, **Charles Elliot** issued formal proclamations addressing the residents of Hong Kong Island. The declarations guaranteed that the native inhabitants would be governed according to their native customs and laws, and that their private property and religious practices would be respected and protected by the British occupying forces. This pragmatic approach aimed to facilitate a peaceful occupation and establish immediate administrative order without provoking widespread insurgency among the local Chinese population.

## Significance

This early policy laid the foundational framework for legal pluralism in British Hong Kong, wherein Chinese customary law—particularly regarding marriage, inheritance, and land tenure—continued to operate alongside imported English common law. The accommodation of local customs proved crucial in integrating the rural population of Hong Kong Island and later the New Territories into the colonial administrative structure, shaping social and legal dynamics for decades to come.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial perspective**: Viewed the guarantees as a pragmatic and enlightened administrative measure necessary to secure peaceful occupation, maintain public order, and establish the rule of law with minimal friction.
- **Local indigenous perspective**: Regarded the assurances as essential protections for their ancestral lands, social structures, and religious traditions against the disruptions of foreign colonial rule.
