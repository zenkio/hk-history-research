## Background

During the early stages of the First Opium War, British forces occupied Hong Kong Island in **1841**. As the British military and naval presence expanded, along with an influx of merchants, laborers, and sailors, the need for organized religious institutions grew. Prior to this, Catholic pastoral care in the region was limited and primarily administered through the Diocese of Macau (天主教澳門教區). With the geopolitical shift accompanying the British takeover, the Vatican sought to establish a formal ecclesiastical presence to serve the burgeoning population.

## What happened

On **April 22, 1841**, Prefect **Theodore Joset** (西奧多·約瑟特) officially established the Roman Catholic mission in Hong Kong, following his appointment as Prefect Apostolic. Operating under the jurisdiction of the Propaganda Fide, **Joset** and his early missionaries began organizing pastoral services. The mission aimed to cater not only to the spiritual needs of European soldiers and sailors stationed in the new colony but also to the local Chinese Catholic and non-Catholic communities residing on the island and surrounding coastal areas.

## Significance

The establishment of the mission marked the formal beginning of institutional Roman Catholic presence in British-administered Hong Kong, laying the groundwork for future educational, medical, and social welfare institutions run by the Church. It reflected the broader integration of religious infrastructure into early colonial Hong Kong. While initially small and constrained by resources, the mission expanded rapidly as the colony grew into a major entrepôt, eventually evolving into a significant hub for Catholic activities in East Asia.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Perspective**: Colonial authorities generally welcomed the establishment of established religious missions, viewing them as stabilizing institutions that contributed to the moral order and social welfare of soldiers, sailors, and the emerging civilian workforce.
- **Catholic Church Perspective**: The Vatican and Church historians viewed the establishment as a vital expansion of the global apostolate, seizing the opportunity provided by the new port to reach both European expatriates and the local Chinese population.
