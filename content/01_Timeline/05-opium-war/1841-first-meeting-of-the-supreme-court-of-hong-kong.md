## Background
Following the British occupation of Hong Kong Island in **1841** during the First Opium War, colonial administrators recognized an urgent need to establish formal legal institutions. Captain **Charles Elliot** (義律) had initially governed the territory under temporary arrangements, but the growing population of merchants, sailors, and local residents necessitated a structured judicial system. The British Crown sought to implement English common law to regulate trade, property rights, and criminal matters in the newly claimed territory, setting it apart from the legal traditions of the Qing Empire.

## What happened
On **October 30, 1841**, the Supreme Court of Hong Kong (香港最高法院) held its first official meeting. The proceedings marked the formal commencement of British judicial authority in the colony. While the initial court structure and personnel underwent various adjustments in subsequent ordinances, this first session symbolized the transplantation of British jurisprudence to a Chinese coastal enclave.

## Significance
The establishment of the Supreme Court was a pivotal moment in the institutional development of Hong Kong. It laid the groundwork for a legal system based on English common law, which would evolve independently of the legal codes of mainland China. This judicial separation became a cornerstone of Hong Kong's distinct administrative and social identity under colonial rule, influencing commercial confidence and governance for over a century.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial perspective**: Viewed the introduction of the Supreme Court as a necessary and progressive step to bring order, rule of law, and commercial security to a previously ungoverned territory.
- **Qing and mainland perspective**: Regarded the imposition of British colonial courts on Chinese soil as an illegal exercise of extraterritoriality and an infringement upon imperial sovereignty.
- **Local community perspective**: navigated the new English common law courts alongside traditional Chinese community mediation and customary laws, adapting pragmatically to an alien judicial framework.
