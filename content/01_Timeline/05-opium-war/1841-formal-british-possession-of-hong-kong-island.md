## Background

By the early 19th century, growing trade tensions between the British Empire and Qing dynasty China (大清) centered on commercial access, diplomatic equality, and the opium trade. Following the breakdown of negotiations and armed clashes, British plenipotentiary **Charles Elliot** (義律) sought a permanent territorial base for British merchants operating in South China. This objective led to the drafting of the Convention of Chuenpi in January 1841, through which local Qing officials and Elliot provisionally agreed to the cession of Hong Kong Island, though the agreement was ultimately disowned by both the British government and the Daoguang Emperor (道光帝).

## What happened

On **January 26, 1841**, **Commodore Sir Gordon Bremer** landed a naval party at a rocky promontory on the northern shore of Hong Kong Island. The British forces raised the Union Jack and claimed the territory in the name of **Queen Victoria**. A Royal Marines detachment fired a feu de joie, and ships of the Royal Navy discharged a 21-gun salute to inaugurate British colonial rule. The landing site was subsequently named Possession Point. **Charles Elliot** issued a proclamation assuring local inhabitants that they would be governed according to native laws and customs pending final ratification by the Crown.

## Significance

This event established the physical and administrative foothold that would transform Hong Kong into a major global entrepôt and free port. Although the immediate local population consisted largely of fishermen, farmers, and charcoal burners residing in small coastal hamlets, the British occupation set the stage for the formal, permanent cession ratified later under the Treaty of Nanking in 1842. The date remains a foundational milestone in the historiography of Hong Kong's colonial era, though its interpretation varies sharply depending on political and historical frameworks.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Perspective**: Traditionally viewed as a triumphant and pragmatic step toward establishing a secure trading post and spreading free trade and rule of law in East Asia.
- **Qing and Mainland Chinese Perspective**: Regarded as an act of imperialist aggression and gunboat diplomacy, stemming from an unequal convention that violated China's territorial sovereignty.
- **Local and Scholarly Perspective**: Emphasizes that the local indigenous and fishing communities were largely treated as passive subjects while imperial powers negotiated their fate, with the early colonial administration having minimal initial impact on the daily lives of most rural villagers.
