## Background

Following the outbreak of the First Opium War, British forces under **Charles Elliot** sought a secure base for merchant shipping and military operations near the Pearl River Delta. British merchants had long complained about the restrictive trade practices and heavy duties imposed by the Qing administration under the Canton System. While the Convention of Chuenpee in early 1841 had initially ceded Hong Kong to Britain, the agreement was later repudiated by both the British government and the Daoguang Emperor, leaving the status of the island uncertain during the ongoing hostilities.

## What happened

On **June 7, 1841**, **Charles Elliot** unilaterally issued a proclamation declaring Hong Kong (香港) a free port. The declaration guaranteed that vessels of all nations could trade in the harbor without paying customs duties or tonnage dues, subject only to British police regulations. This move was designed to immediately attract foreign merchants away from rival ports like Canton (廣州) and Macao (澳門) by undercutting protectionist tariffs and providing a secure legal and physical environment for commerce.

## Significance

The declaration of the free port laid the institutional cornerstone for Hong Kong's rapid development as a major global entrepôt. By removing tariff barriers, the policy encouraged an influx of foreign capital, merchant houses, and maritime traffic. However, the long-term impact on local inhabitants, including indigenous farmers and fisherfolk, and the extent to which the free port policy alone drove economic growth remain subjects of ongoing historical analysis.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Perspective**: Viewed as a visionary stroke of free-market policy that unlocked immense commercial potential, turned a barren island into a bustling metropolis, and established the rule of law and free trade as the bedrock of prosperity.
- **Qing / Modern Chinese Perspective**: Regarded as an illegal act of colonial aggression and a direct violation of Chinese sovereignty, transforming the island into a base for the opium trade and foreign exploitation of China.
- **Local and Revisionist Scholarship**: Emphasizes that local Chinese merchants, junk traders, and indigenous communities were the primary drivers of actual economic activity, often navigating complex colonial and indigenous legal frameworks rather than relying solely on British institutional design.
