## Background
Following the landing of British forces on Hong Kong Island in January 1841 during the First Opium War, the nascent settlement lacked a formal administrative and legal framework. **Captain Charles Elliot** (義律), the British Plenipotentiary, sought to establish order among the growing population of merchants, sailors, and local Chinese residents. At the time, formal sovereignty had not yet been ceded by the Qing Empire, making the legal status of the territory ambiguous under British constitutional law.

## What happened
On **April 30, 1841** (道光二十一年四月九日), **Captain Charles Elliot** issued provisional criminal and civil regulations to govern the British settlement. This act marked the introduction of the first colonial legal code in Hong Kong. The regulations were designed to maintain public order, protect commercial interests, and enforce basic judicial oversight through appointed magistrates before the formal establishment of a Supreme Court or legislative council.

## Significance
This early legal enactment laid the groundwork for the eventual transplantation of English common law into Hong Kong, a cornerstone of the territory's modern legal system. While initially conceived as a temporary wartime measure, it signaled the permanent intent of British occupation and set a precedent for executive-led colonial governance prior to the formal ratification of the Treaty of Nanking (南京條約).

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Viewed the introduction of provisional laws as an administrative necessity to protect British subjects, suppress crime, and establish the rule of law in a newly acquired territory.
- **Qing Court Perspective**: Regarded such unilateral legal codes as unlawful impositions by foreign invaders acting without imperial sanction on sovereign Chinese soil.
- **Local Communities**: Experienced the new legal codes as a sudden imposition that largely disregarded traditional Chinese laws and customs, creating a dual system of regulation.
