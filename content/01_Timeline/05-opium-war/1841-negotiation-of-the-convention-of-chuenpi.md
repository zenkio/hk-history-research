## Background

By **January 1841**, the First Opium War had reached a critical juncture. Following military escalations in the Pearl River Delta, including the capture of the Chuenpi forts by British forces, the Daoguang Emperor appointed the Manchu statesman **Qishan** (琦善) to negotiate with the British plenipotentiary, **Charles Elliot** (義律). British strategic aims had long included securing a permanent base for trade and naval operations in Southern China, leading Elliot to demand territorial concessions alongside financial indemnities.

## What happened

On **January 20, 1841**, Elliot and **Qishan** preliminarily agreed to the terms of the Convention of Chuenpi (穿鼻草約). The agreement stipulated the direct cession of Hong Kong Island (香港島) and its natural harbor to the British Crown, an indemnity of six million silver dollars, the resumption of direct official trade on an equal footing, and the restoration of Chuenpi to Chinese control. Shortly thereafter, on **January 26, 1841**, British forces formally landed at Possession Point (水坑口) to take formal possession of the island.

However, the agreement was never officially ratified by either sovereign government. **Qishan** acted without explicit imperial sanction, leading the Daoguang Emperor to reject the treaty furiously, dismiss **Qishan**, and order his arrest. Simultaneously, the British government under Lord Palmerston felt Elliot had asked for too little—desiring the entirety of the Chusan archipelago rather than just Hong Kong Island—and recalled **Elliot** in favor of **Henry Pottinger** (璞鼎察). Despite its unratified status, the Convention set the immediate territorial precedent that was later formalized in the Treaty of Nanking (南京條約).

## Significance

As a diplomatic milestone, the Convention of Chuenpi marks the exact moment Hong Kong Island entered the British imperial sphere. While short-lived as a formal treaty, it established the physical foothold from which the colonial administration grew. The rapid repudiation by both the Qing court and London highlights the immense friction between traditional Chinese tributary worldview and 19th-century British gunboat diplomacy.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial historiography**: Traditionally viewed the agreement as the necessary and legitimate first step in securing a vital commercial entrepot and safe harbor in East Asia, blaming Qing intransigence for delaying formal ratification.
- **Qing and Mainland Chinese historiography**: Characterizes the convention as an illegal, coercive document forced under military duress by imperialist aggressors upon an unauthorized and panicked official who was subsequently punished for treason.
- **Local Hong Kong scholarship**: Focuses on the immediate disruption and adaptation experienced by the indigenous fishing and farming populations on Hong Kong Island, who suddenly found themselves subjects of the British Crown without local consultation.
