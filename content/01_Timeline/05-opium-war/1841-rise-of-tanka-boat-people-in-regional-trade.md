## Background
In the early **19th century**, the social structure of the Pearl River Delta (珠江三角洲) was strictly stratified. The **Tanka (蜑家人)**, often referred to as the "boat people," occupied the lowest rung of this hierarchy. Unlike the land-based Punti (本地人) or Hakka (客家人) populations, the Tanka were largely marginalized by the Qing administration, frequently forbidden from settling on land or participating in the imperial examination system. This systemic exclusion forced them to rely entirely on maritime activities, including fishing and small-scale regional trade, making them highly mobile and less tethered to the land-based social constraints of the era.

## What happened
During the First Opium War (**1839–1842**), and specifically around **1841** as the British military and mercantile interests sought to establish a permanent presence on Hong Kong (香港), the logistical needs of the British fleet grew. The Royal Navy required intimate knowledge of the local currents, shoals, and hidden inlets to navigate the coast safely and effectively. The Tanka, possessing unmatched expertise in these waters, began providing critical pilotage services to the British.

Beyond navigation, the Tanka established a vital supply chain. They provided the British with fresh water, livestock, and seafood, often acting as the primary intermediaries between the foreign ships and the wary inland farmers. While many land-based villagers avoided contact with the British to avoid retribution from Qing officials, the Tanka's boat-based existence allowed them to move between the two powers with greater flexibility. This cooperation transformed the Tanka from marginalized fishers into essential logistical partners in the early regional trade network.

## Significance
The role of the Tanka was instrumental in the viability of Hong Kong as an anchorage. Without their logistical support and navigational guidance, the British would have struggled to sustain their forces and operate within the complex waterways of the delta. This period demonstrates a significant instance of indigenous social flexibility; by leveraging their marginalized status and specialized skills, the Tanka were able to carve out a new economic niche. Their involvement marked the beginning of a shift in regional dynamics, where maritime expertise became a highly tradable commodity during the transition to colonial rule.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial**: Viewed the Tanka as helpful local allies and a reliable source of labor and intelligence who were naturally predisposed to support the new administration.
- **Qing/Beijing**: Regarded such cooperation as a betrayal of the empire, often attributing it to the low social standing and perceived lack of loyalty of the Tanka population.
- **Modern Scholarship**: Interprets the Tanka's actions as a strategic survival mechanism, utilizing their unique mobility to navigate the conflict between two opposing powers for economic gain.
