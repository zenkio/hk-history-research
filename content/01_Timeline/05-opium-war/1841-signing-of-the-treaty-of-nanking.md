## Background

By the early 19th century, trade between the **Qing Empire** (大清) and **Great Britain** was heavily restricted to the Canton System (廣州十三行). Escalating tensions over trade imbalances, the British importation of opium, and differing legal frameworks eventually led to open conflict in 1839, marking the outbreak of the First Opium War. British naval and military forces utilized superior firepower to pressure Qing authorities along the southeastern coast, ultimately leading to negotiations near the end of the conflict.

## What happened

On **August 29, 1842**, British plenipotentiary **Sir Henry Pottinger** and Qing imperial commissioners **Qiying** (耆英), **Ilibu** (伊里布), and **Niu Jian** (牛鑑) boarded the British warship HMS *Cornwallis*, anchored off Nanjing. There, they signed the **Treaty of Nanking** (南京條約). The treaty formally concluded hostilities, opened five treaty ports to foreign trade, imposed a large indemnity on the Qing government, and stipulated the unconditional ceding of **Hong Kong Island** (香港島) in perpetuity to the British Crown.

## Significance

The Treaty of Nanking is widely viewed by historians as the first of the "unequal treaties" that marked the beginning of China's century of humiliation. For the British Empire, it established a permanent territorial foothold in East Asia that would facilitate commercial expansion and colonial administration. Locally, the cession transformed Hong Kong Island from a sparsely populated fishing and agricultural community into a strategic global port, setting the stage for more than a century of British colonial rule.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial historiography**: Traditional British accounts often emphasized the treaty as a triumph of free trade, modernization, and the expansion of international law, framing the acquisition of Hong Kong as a vital commercial hub for the British Empire.
- **Qing and modern mainland Chinese historiography**: Modern Chinese scholarship views the treaty as the opening act of foreign imperialist aggression, highlighting national sovereignty, military coercion, and the unequal nature of the imposed terms.
- **Local Hong Kong perspective**: Local historians frequently note that the treaty was concluded without the consultation or consent of the indigenous inhabitants of Hong Kong Island, whose lives and governance were abruptly altered by distant imperial powers.
