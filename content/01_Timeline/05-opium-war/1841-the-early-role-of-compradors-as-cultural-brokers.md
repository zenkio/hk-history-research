## Background
Prior to the **First Opium War (1839-1842)**, foreign trade in China was strictly regulated by the Canton System (廣州體制). Under this framework, Western merchants were restricted to the port of Canton (廣州) and required to deal exclusively with a guild of licensed Chinese merchants known as the Cohong (十三行). These merchants acted as the sole intermediaries, managing customs and ensuring compliance with Qing (清) regulations. However, as British interests pushed for "free trade" and direct access to markets, the rigid structure of the Cohong became a focal point of diplomatic and military tension.

## What happened
Following the British occupation of Hong Kong (香港) in **1841**, the administrative and economic landscape shifted. While the Cohong system was formally abolished by the Treaty of Nanking (南京條約) in **1842**, the necessity for local intermediaries remained. This period saw the emergence and professionalization of the comprador (買辦)—specialized agents employed by foreign firms to manage the "Chinese side" of business operations.

These individuals performed critical roles as cultural brokers. They navigated the linguistic divide, translated legal documents, and managed the recruitment of local labor for the nascent settlement of Victoria City (維多利亞城). Because the British authorities lacked knowledge of local land tenure and social hierarchies, they relied on these intermediaries to negotiate with village elders and landowners. The compradors handled currency exchange, credit arrangements, and the procurement of goods, effectively bridging the gap between the British colonial administration and the local population during a period of high instability.

## Significance
The role of the comprador was fundamental to the survival and growth of the early Hong Kong settlement. By acting as a buffer, they reduced the friction of cultural misunderstanding, though they often operated in a precarious position between two competing sovereign powers. Their activities laid the groundwork for the later development of the "Hongs" (洋行), the great trading houses that would dominate the region's economy for a century. The evolution of this role reflects the transition from a state-controlled trade monopoly under the Qing to a more decentralized, capitalist-driven model under British colonial rule.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial**: Viewed compradors as essential partners in progress who brought efficiency to trade and helped implement "civilized" commercial laws in a previously restrictive environment.
- **Qing/Beijing**: Often regarded these intermediaries as opportunistic collaborators or traitors who undermined imperial authority and facilitated the illegal opium trade.
- **Later Scholarship**: Analyzes compradors as strategic actors who utilized their 'middleman' status to accumulate wealth and power, navigating the tensions of globalization and colonialism.
