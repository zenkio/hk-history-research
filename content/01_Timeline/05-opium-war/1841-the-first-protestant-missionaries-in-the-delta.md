## Background
During the early 19th century, the Qing (清) dynasty maintained a restrictive policy toward foreign residence and the propagation of Christianity. Under the "Canton System," foreign traders and residents were confined to the Thirteen Factories (十三行) in Canton (廣州). Protestant missionaries, primarily from the **London Missionary Society**, operated on the margins of this system. Early figures such as **Robert Morrison** worked semi-secretly, focusing on linguistic studies and the translation of the Bible to bypass prohibitions against preaching to the Chinese population.

## What happened
As the First Opium War escalated and British forces occupied Hong Kong (香港) in **1841**, the geopolitical landscape shifted. The subsequent Treaty of Nanking (**1842**) granted the British a permanent base and opened several treaty ports. This provided Protestant missionaries with a legal and secure foothold in the region. No longer confined to the periphery of Canton, missionaries began to establish permanent residences and institutions within the boundaries of Hong Kong.

These early missions were not solely focused on proselytization. By the mid-**1840s**, they began integrating social welfare into their operations. They established early schools and clinics to serve both the expatriate community and the local population. This period marked the transition from the "itinerant" missionary model—where agents moved between Macau (澳門) and Malacca—to a settled institutional presence in the Pearl River Delta.

## Significance
The arrival of these missionaries established a long-term social infrastructure that would define Hong Kong's colonial civil society. Because the early British colonial government provided limited social services, the vacuum was filled by missionary organizations. The introduction of Western medicine and English-language education created a professional class and a healthcare network that persisted for over a century. This infrastructure acted as a bridge between the colonial administration and the local Chinese community, though it remained deeply entwined with the religious goals of the mission societies.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial**: Viewed the missionary presence as a 'civilizing mission' that brought enlightenment through modern science, medicine, and the Gospel to the region.
- **Qing/Beijing**: Regarded missionaries as agents of foreign encroachment and disruptors of the traditional Confucian social order and imperial law.
- **Later Scholarship**: Analyzes missionaries as complex intermediaries who facilitated cultural exchange but functioned as the 'soft power' arm of British imperial expansion.
