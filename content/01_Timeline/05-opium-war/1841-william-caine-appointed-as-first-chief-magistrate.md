## Background

Following the landing of British forces on Hong Kong Island in early 1841 during the First Opium War, British plenipotentiary **Charles Elliot** (義律) sought to establish a formal system of governance. With the occupation of the territory, the British authorities faced the immediate necessity of maintaining public order among a mixed population of European merchants, British military personnel, and local Chinese villagers and fishermen.

## What happened

On **April 30, 1841**, Captain **William Caine** (凱恩) of the 26th Regiment of Foot was officially appointed as the first Chief Magistrate of Hong Kong. In this dual role, **Caine** was tasked with heading both the nascent magistracy and the early police force. He set up a makeshift court to administer British criminal and civil law, dealing primarily with petty crimes, property disputes, and maintaining order in the burgeoning settlement around areas like **Hong Kong Island** (香港島) and **Victoria** (維多利亞城).

## Significance

The appointment marked a foundational step in the establishment of a British colonial legal system in Hong Kong. By imposing British judicial administration over both European and Chinese residents, the magistracy laid the groundwork for the territory's future legal framework. However, the early administration struggled to reconcile English common law with local Chinese customs and legal traditions, setting the stage for long-term complexities in colonial governance.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Viewed the appointment as an essential and successful step in bringing the rule of law, order, and civilized governance to a previously chaotic or lawless region.
- **Qing official perspective**: Dismissed British legal institutions on the occupied island as illegal incursions of foreign jurisdiction and sovereignty onto imperial Chinese territory.
- **Local indigenous and resident communities**: Navigated the imposition of unfamiliar Western laws while attempting to preserve local customs, often viewing the early British magistracy with caution and suspicion.
