## Background

Following the outbreak of the **First Opium War** and the initial British landing on Hong Kong Island in **1841**, the colonial administration sought to establish permanent infrastructure to govern the newly claimed territory. Under the leadership of early British officials, including **Sir Henry Pottinger** (璞鼎釵), the administration operated out of temporary quarters while planning formal government buildings. The selection of a site on the slopes above Central aimed to project authority and provide a healthy elevation above the congested waterfront.

## What happened

In **1842**, construction commenced on the First Government House, located on Pottinger Street (砵甸乍街). The building was designed to serve both as the official residence and the administrative office for the colonial governor. Due to the rapid pace of early colonial expansion and the humid local climate, the initial structure was modest and reportedly faced structural and maintenance challenges. It served as the center of executive power during the formative years of British Hong Kong before later being replaced by a more grandiose structure on the current site in Upper Albert Road.

## Significance

The construction of the First Government House served as a tangible symbol of the permanent physical anchoring of British colonial rule on Hong Kong Island, even prior to the formal ratification of the Treaty of Nanjing (南京條約). While British authorities viewed the edifice as essential infrastructure for efficient governance and the rule of law, local Chinese populations and Qing officials regarded it as an intrusive monument to foreign occupation and imperial encroachment on Chinese sovereignty.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Viewed the construction as a necessary and progressive step to establish orderly governance, rule of law, and administrative permanence in the newly acquired territory.
- **Qing dynasty officials**: Saw the building of permanent colonial offices as an intolerable assertion of foreign authority on sovereign Chinese soil following an unjust war.
- **Later historical scholarship**: Analyzes the site selection and architecture of the early Government House as a physical manifestation of spatial segregation and colonial dominance over the local landscape.
