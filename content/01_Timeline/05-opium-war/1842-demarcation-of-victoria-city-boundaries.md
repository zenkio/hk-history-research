## Background

Following the landing of British forces on Hong Kong Island in **1841** and the subsequent signing of the Treaty of Nanking in **1842**, the colonial government faced the immediate task of organizing the newly acquired territory. The northern shore of Hong Kong Island, possessing a deep-water harbor, was selected as the primary site for commercial and administrative activities. To manage land sales, taxation, and policing, the colonial administration recognized the necessity of establishing clear municipal boundaries for the emerging settlement, which was named Victoria City (維多利亞城) in honor of the British monarch.

## What happened

In the wake of the cession, surveyors and colonial officials set about mapping the terrain along the coastline and lower slopes of Hong Kong Island. The initial demarcation divided the settlement into distinct administrative and commercial sectors. Queen's Road (皇后大道), the first major thoroughfare, served as the primary east-west axis around which lots were auctioned off to British and foreign mercantile houses. While the exact initial boundary markers varied and evolved through subsequent ordinances in the decades that followed, the **1842** initiatives established the core urban footprint from which the city would expand westward toward Sheung Wan (上環) and eastward toward Wan Chai (灣仔).

## Significance

The demarcation of Victoria City's boundaries represents a crucial turning point in the transformation of Hong Kong from a collection of rural and fishing communities into a colonial entrepôt. By imposing a grid-based and legally defined urban structure, the British administration created a spatial hierarchy that separated European commercial zones from Chinese residential quarters. This early town planning profoundly shaped the physical, social, and economic landscape of Hong Kong for generations, creating enduring patterns of urban segregation and land tenure.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Viewed the demarcation as an essential administrative necessity to establish the rule of law, facilitate orderly land sales, and foster economic growth through secure property rights.
- **Local indigenous and fishing communities**: Experienced the rapid urban planning and boundary-setting as a disruptive imposition that disregarded traditional land usage, Fung Shui principles, and long-standing local community patterns along the coast.
