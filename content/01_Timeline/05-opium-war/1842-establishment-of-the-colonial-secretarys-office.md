## Background
Prior to 1842, the British presence in the region was primarily commercial, centered on the 'Canton System' and the operations of firms like **Jardine, Matheson & Co.** The cessation of hostilities during the **First Opium War** and the drafting of the **Treaty of Nanking** (南京條約) in **1842** necessitated a transition from naval oversight to a civilian administrative structure. The British sought to transform the island of Hong Kong (香港島) from a temporary military base into a permanent Crown Colony capable of facilitating trade and enforcing British law.

## What happened
In **1842**, following the arrival of **Sir Henry Pottinger** (砵甸乍), the colonial government began organizing the civil bureaucracy required to administer the new territory. The **Colonial Secretary's Office** (輔政司署) was established to act as the central executive hub of the government. This office was tasked with managing official correspondence, overseeing the nascent civil service, and coordinating the implementation of administrative policies decreed by the Governor. It functioned as the primary channel between the local administration and the Colonial Office in London. The establishment provided a structured mechanism for land sales, the registration of residents, and the imposition of colonial legal frameworks.

## Significance
The creation of the Colonial Secretary's Office signaled the formal start of a colonial civil society in Hong Kong. It shifted the administration away from ad-hoc naval governance toward a bureaucratic model characterized by record-keeping, legal codification, and centralized authority. This transition allowed for the eventual expansion of colonial infrastructure, including schools, hospitals, and judicial courts. The office became the bedrock upon which the executive branch of the Hong Kong government was built, influencing the administrative trajectory of the territory for over 150 years.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Viewed the office as a necessary instrument of 'civilization' and 'order' required to integrate Hong Kong into the global mercantile system. It was considered the essential first step in creating a stable, law-abiding society out of a 'barren rock'.
- **Qing/Beijing Perspective**: Regarded the establishment of formal British administrative structures as an illegitimate encroachment on sovereign territory. The administrative expansion was viewed as a blatant violation of local authority and an extension of the 'unequal treaty' system imposed upon China.
- **Local Communities**: Existing inhabitants and maritime populations often experienced these new administrative layers as a disruption to long-standing customs and local governance. For many, the office represented an alien bureaucracy that imposed taxes and regulations without local consultation.
