## Background

Following the British occupation of Hong Kong Island in **January 1841** during the First Opium War, the colonial administration and military forces established temporary encampments along the northern coast. The damp, subtropical environment, characterized by extensive paddy fields, marshlands, and dense vegetation, provided an ideal breeding ground for mosquitoes. Contemporary medical understanding of vector-borne diseases was virtually nonexistent, and troops were quartered in poorly ventilated tents and mat-shed structures without adequate drainage or sanitation.

## What happened

Beginning in **June 1842**, a severe outbreak of illness—collectively termed "Hong Kong fever," which modern medical historians identify as a combination of malaria, typhus, and dysentery—struck the British military garrisons and civilian population with alarming intensity. Hundreds of soldiers, sailors, and civilian personnel fell ill, overwhelming the rudimentary medical facilities available at the time. Mortality rates among the troops were exceptionally high, rendering a significant portion of the occupying force unfit for duty.

In response to the crisis, colonial authorities began to reassess their infrastructural planning. The epidemic prompted the initial construction of permanent military cantonments on higher, better-drained ground, particularly in areas like **Victoria City (維多利亞城)**. It also forced the administration to introduce rudimentary sanitation policies, clear brush, and improve surface water drainage, laying the groundwork for early public health administration in the territory.

## Significance

The epidemic severely tested the resolve of the early British administration and demonstrated the immense environmental and health challenges of establishing a colonial settlement in South China. The high mortality figures forced a shift from temporary encampments to permanent, planned urban and military infrastructure. It also highlighted the vulnerability of unacclimatized European populations to tropical diseases, influencing subsequent military deployments, naval operations during the remainder of the Opium War, and the long-term urban planning of the colony.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Viewed the epidemic as a severe environmental obstacle and an administrative crisis that necessitated urgent infrastructural investments, better military housing, and the implementation of rudimentary public health measures to secure the viability of the colony.
- **Qing and Beijing perspective**: Regarded the high mortality among the foreign occupiers as a natural consequence of occupying malarial southern terrain and an illustration of the unhealthy, precarious nature of the unauthorized foreign foothold.
- **Local Hong Kong communities**: Experienced the health crisis alongside the foreign forces, though indigenous populations often relied on traditional herbal remedies and local ecological knowledge to navigate the seasonal fevers.
