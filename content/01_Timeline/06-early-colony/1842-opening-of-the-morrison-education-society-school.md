## Background
Named in honor of the pioneering Protestant missionary **Robert Morrison** (馬禮遜), the Morrison Education Society was established in China in **1836** to promote English education and the dissemination of useful knowledge. Initially operating a school in Macao, the society recognized the shifting geopolitical landscape following the First Opium War. With the British occupation of Hong Kong Island, the institution decided to move its operations to the burgeoning new settlement to better serve the growing foreign community and local populations under British administration.

## What happened
In **1842**, the school officially reopened in Hong Kong. Under the guidance of educators such as **Samuel Robbins Brown** (勃朗), the institution offered a curriculum that blended Western academic subjects, including mathematics, geography, and science, with classical Chinese studies. The school aimed to bridge cultural divides by producing students proficient in both English and Chinese, equipping them for administrative, commercial, and translational roles in the colonial framework. Among its earliest and most notable pupils was **Tong King-sing** (唐景星), who would later become a prominent comprador and industrialist.

## Significance
The relocation and operation of the Morrison Education Society School represented a foundational moment in the history of Hong Kong's educational development. It stood as one of the earliest organized attempts to establish formal Western-style education within the territory. By training some of Hong Kong's first bilingual and bicultural intermediaries, the school played an indirect yet vital role in shaping the colony's early professional class, influencing commerce, governance, and cross-cultural interaction during the formative years of British rule.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial perspective**: Colonial administrators and missionary proponents viewed the school as a benevolent and progressive civilizing mission that introduced enlightenment, modern sciences, and useful skills to the region. It was seen as essential for training trustworthy local intermediaries necessary for the administration and commercial growth of the new port.
- **Qing and later Chinese nationalist perspective**: Imperial authorities and subsequent nationalist historians often viewed early missionary-led educational institutions with suspicion, seeing them as cultural outposts of Western imperialism designed to undermine traditional Confucian values and exert ideological influence over Chinese youth.
- **Local Hong Kong and modern scholarly perspective**: Modern historians often analyze the school through a pragmatic lens, noting that while it served missionary goals, local families enrolled their sons primarily to acquire valuable linguistic and technical skills for socioeconomic advancement in a changing colonial economy.
