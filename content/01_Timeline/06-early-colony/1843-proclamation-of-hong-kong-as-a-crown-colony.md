## Background

The status of Hong Kong Island was initially established under the **Convention of Chuenpi** in January 1841, a preliminary agreement negotiated during the First Opium War between **Charles Elliot** and the Qing imperial commissioner **Qishan (琦善)**. Neither government initially ratified the convention, as both **Emperor Daoguang** and the British Foreign Secretary **Lord Palmerston** were dissatisfied with the terms. However, military occupation proceeded, and the island served as a British naval base.

Formal cession was later ratified by the Qing Empire and the British Crown under the **Treaty of Nanking** on **August 29, 1842**. 

## What happened

On **June 26, 1843**, the ratifications of the Treaty of Nanking were exchanged in Hong Kong. On the same day, **Sir Henry Pottinger** was formally sworn in as the first Governor and Commander-in-Chief of the colony of Hong Kong. 

By a royal charter issued earlier that year, the colony was established with an Executive Council and a Legislative Council to advise the Governor. Pottinger issued the official proclamations detailing the administrative framework, and Hong Kong began operating under British common law, adapted to local circumstances.

## Significance

This event transformed a temporary military encampment into a permanent British Crown Colony. It laid the institutional and legal foundations for Hong Kong's development as a global entrepôt. For the Qing dynasty, it represented the first of the 'unequal treaties' that would reshape China's sovereignty and foreign relations over the following century.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Viewed the proclamation as the lawful establishment of order, rule of law, and free trade in a previously undeveloped or sparsely populated region.
- **Qing imperial perspective**: Regarded the cession and subsequent colony as a humiliating imposition resulting from military defeat, forced under unequal terms that infringed upon territorial sovereignty.
- **Local and later scholarship**: Emphasize that long before and during the formal proclamation, local coastal populations, fishermen, and indigenous villagers navigated changing authorities, often maintaining their own social and economic networks independently of British or Qing edicts.
