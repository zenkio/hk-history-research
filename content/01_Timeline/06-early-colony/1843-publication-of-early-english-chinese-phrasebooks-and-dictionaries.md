## Background

Following the Treaty of Nanking in **1842**, Hong Kong became a British Crown colony. The sudden influx of British administrators, merchants, and military personnel into a predominantly Cantonese-speaking region created an immediate and pressing linguistic barrier. Communication between the colonial authorities and the local Chinese populace, as well as with Qing (清朝) officials, was severely constrained. To administer the territory, collect taxes, and facilitate trade, colonial officials and missionaries recognized the urgent need for reliable reference materials.

## What happened

Around **1843**, scholars, missionaries, and colonial officials began compiling and publishing the first systematic English-Chinese phrasebooks, vocabularies, and dictionaries in Hong Kong and nearby Guangzhou (廣州) and Macao (澳門). These early works were designed to capture the spoken Cantonese dialect used in daily commerce alongside written Chinese characters. They covered essential administrative terms, legal concepts, maritime language, and everyday conversational phrases. While many early compilers were Western missionaries or government interpreters seeking practical utility, their works laid the groundwork for future lexicography in the region.

## Significance

These early linguistic guides facilitated vital commercial and administrative communication during the formative years of the colony. By enabling British officials to issue directives and local compradors to conduct business, the phrasebooks helped stabilize the burgeoning colonial economy. Furthermore, these publications shaped how the English-speaking administration understood local society, institutionalizing certain translations and interpretations of Chinese social structures, legal terms, and geographic locations that persisted for decades.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Perspective**: Colonial administrators and merchants viewed the phrasebooks and dictionaries as indispensable practical tools necessary for efficient governance, the rule of law, and the expansion of trade in the new port city.
- **Qing and Local Perspectives**: Imperial Qing authorities and local Chinese communities often viewed these early Western-compiled linguistic tools with suspicion, seeing them as instruments of foreign encroachment and colonial control designed to facilitate unequal trade and administration.
