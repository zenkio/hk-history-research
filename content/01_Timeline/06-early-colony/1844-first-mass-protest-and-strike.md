## Background

Following the British occupation of Hong Kong Island and the formal cession under the Treaty of Nanjing, the early colonial administration faced significant financial and administrative challenges. To generate revenue and exert greater control over the predominantly Chinese population, the government sought to implement census and taxation measures. In late **1844**, Governor **Sir John Francis Davis** introduced a registration ordinance alongside a poll tax aimed at the local inhabitants.

## What happened

Local residents, particularly Chinese merchants and laborers, viewed the registration and taxation scheme as intrusive, culturally alien, and financially burdensome. On **November 4, 1844**, opposition culminated in a widespread cessation of commerce and labor. Shops closed, cargo handling ceased, and domestic workers withheld their services, effectively paralyzing the nascent settlement's economy. The sudden and unified withdrawal of Chinese labor caught the colonial authorities largely unprepared, demonstrating the critical reliance of the British administration on the local workforce.

Faced with severe economic disruption and potential civil unrest, the colonial government suspended and ultimately repealed the contested ordinance. Governor **Davis** and the administration recognized the limits of their coercive power in the early colony, leading to a temporary retreat from direct heavy-handed taxation and registration of the general Chinese populace.

## Significance

This event is widely recognized by historians as the first major instance of collective action and labor resistance against British rule in Hong Kong. It set an early precedent regarding the limits of colonial authority, highlighting how economic non-cooperation by Chinese residents could effectively check government policy. The strike underscored the complex dynamics between the colonial state and the local population, prefiguring future tensions over governance, taxation, and legal jurisdiction in the territory.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Colonial officials initially viewed the registration ordinance as a necessary administrative tool for maintaining public order and raising revenue, but ultimately pragmatic leaders realized they lacked the military and police capacity to enforce unpopular measures against a united local populace.
- **Local Chinese Community**: Chinese merchants and laborers perceived the new taxes and mandatory registration as arbitrary oppression by an alien government, utilizing traditional guild networks and collective withdrawal of labor to successfully defend their economic interests and customary practices.
- **Modern Historiography**: Later scholars emphasize the 1844 strike as a foundational moment demonstrating the agency of the local Chinese population and the practical constraints placed upon early British imperialism in East Asia.
