## Background
Following the **Treaty of Nanking** (南京條約) in **1842**, Hong Kong was formally ceded to the British Empire. Initially, the territory was governed under the direct authority of the colonial governor, who held substantial executive power. As the population of European merchants increased and the administrative demands of the port grew, there was pressure to create a more formal legislative framework to assist the governor in drafting laws and managing revenue. The colonial administration sought to transition from a military-led outpost to a functional administrative center for British commercial interests in East Asia.

## What happened
In **1844**, the British government authorized the establishment of the Legislative Council (立法局). This body was designed to advise and consent to the laws passed by the governor. The initial composition of the council was restricted exclusively to senior British officials. Sir **Henry Pottinger** (璞鼎查), the first Governor of Hong Kong, oversaw its preliminary formation. The council functioned primarily as a mechanism for the governor to consult with key figures within the administration. It was not intended to be a representative body for the general population; rather, its structure ensured that colonial policy remained aligned with the interests of the Crown and the commercial objectives of the British mercantile class.

## Significance
The formation of the Legislative Council represents the establishment of the bedrock of Hong Kong's colonial governing structure. While it held very little power to check the governor’s authority in its early years, it established the precedent for an institutional, statutory approach to governance. Later scholarship often debates whether this body served as a genuine instrument of civil governance or merely as a veneer for the consolidation of executive power. The structure of the council evolved slowly over the subsequent decades, eventually incorporating non-official members from the merchant class, though it remained largely inaccessible to the local Chinese population for much of the 19th century. This institutional design shaped the political landscape of the colony for over a century, setting the standard for how legislation was drafted and passed within the colonial administration.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: The administration viewed the Legislative Council as a necessary tool to bring order, legal consistency, and professional governance to a new colony. It was perceived as a step toward mirroring British parliamentary traditions and ensuring administrative stability for the growing merchant community.
- **Qing Dynasty Officials**: To the Qing government, the formation of such a council was a internal colonial affair relevant only to the British occupation of Hong Kong Island. It was largely ignored as an illegitimate exercise of power within territory that had been ceded under duress.
- **Local Communities and Later Scholarship**: Many historians view the early council as a manifestation of an exclusionary 'executive-led' government that served elite interests while ignoring the rights and needs of the local Chinese population. This perspective highlights the lack of representation and the stark power imbalance that defined colonial rule during this era.
