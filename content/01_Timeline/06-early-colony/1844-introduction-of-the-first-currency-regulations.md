## Background
Following the Treaty of Nanking in 1842, the newly established colony of **Hong Kong** (香港) functioned as a free port. However, it lacked a unified monetary system. Trade was conducted using a chaotic mix of Spanish, Mexican, and South American silver dollars, alongside British sterling and Chinese copper cash. This variety caused significant uncertainty for merchants and officials, as there was no standardized exchange rate, leading to frequent disputes over the purity and value of circulating coins.

## What happened
In **1844**, the colonial administration, led by the first governor **Henry Pottinger** (砵甸乍), attempted to impose order on the circulating currency. The government introduced regulations aimed at establishing a legal tender framework. The primary goal was to mandate the use of recognized silver dollars as the standard medium for public and private transactions, attempting to curb the circulation of debased or counterfeit coinage that plagued the local markets. Despite these efforts, the regulations struggled to gain immediate traction due to the entrenched reliance on diverse international silver standards and the refusal of local traders to abandon familiar, albeit non-standardized, coins.

## Significance
The **1844** regulations represent the earliest attempt by the British colonial government to assert financial sovereignty over the territory. By attempting to formalize the use of silver as legal tender, the administration laid the early groundwork for the eventual development of a centralized banking and note-issuing system. While the immediate success of these regulations was limited, they highlighted the tension between a colonial government seeking fiscal order and a mercantile community deeply integrated into complex, multi-currency international trade networks. The difficulty in enforcing these standards also demonstrated the limits of early colonial control over the local economy, which remained heavily influenced by traditional trade practices and the wider economic currents of the Pearl River Delta.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial perspective**: The colonial administration viewed the introduction of currency regulations as a necessary step toward civilizing the local market and ensuring the stability required for a flourishing British port. They argued that standardized coinage was essential to prevent fraud and to provide a predictable financial environment for British firms.
- **Local merchant and Qing perspective**: Many local merchants and Qing officials viewed the currency regulations as an unnecessary interference in established trade practices. They often preferred familiar, high-purity silver dollars that were widely recognized in the region, seeing the colonial attempt at standardization as an intrusive measure that failed to account for the actual needs of cross-border commerce.
