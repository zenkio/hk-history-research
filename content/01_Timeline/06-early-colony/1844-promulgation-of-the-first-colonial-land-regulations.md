## Background

Following the British occupation of Hong Kong Island in 1841 under the Convention of Chuenpi, the early settlement lacked a structured land administration. Land was initially occupied informally, leading to speculative chaos and disputes. The British administration, led by early colonial officials such as **Henry Pottinger** (砵甸乍), recognized the urgent need to establish legal order and generate revenue to sustain the fledgling colony.

## What happened

In **1844**, the colonial government formally promulgated the First Colonial Land Regulations. This framework instituted a system of Crown leases, typically sold at public auction with fixed annual quit-rents. Rather than outright freehold ownership, purchasers acquired long-term leaseholds—initially set at 75, 99, or 999 years. This mechanism allowed the government to retain ultimate ownership of the land while encouraging private investment in commercial and residential infrastructure, particularly around Victoria City (維多利亞城).

## Significance

These regulations laid the legal and financial bedrock for Hong Kong's urban property development. The revenue generated from land sales and quit-rents became the primary pillar of colonial public finance, funding government operations and infrastructure. Moreover, the leasehold model established in 1844 created a structural precedent that heavily influenced the territory's modern real estate market and land tenure system, echoes of which persisted well beyond the colonial era.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Officials viewed the regulations as an essential tool to establish the rule of law, eliminate chaotic speculation, and secure a reliable, self-sustaining revenue stream for the administration without relying heavily on the British Treasury.
- **Local Chinese Residents and Merchants**: Many local inhabitants and merchants had to navigate unfamiliar Western legal concepts of property and leaseholds, which often disrupted traditional customary land use patterns and favored foreign mercantile houses.
- **Qing Imperial Government**: Imperial authorities viewed British administrative acts in Hong Kong as illegitimate impositions on Chinese territory, though Qing officials largely focused on broader diplomatic and military containment along the coast.
