## Background

Following the British occupation of Hong Kong in **1841** and the formal cession under the Treaty of Nanking in **1842**, the fledgling colony experienced a rapid influx of migrants from neighboring Guangdong province. Among these arrivals were members of various Chinese oath-bound brotherhoods, commonly known as Triads or heaven and earth societies (天地會). Originally emerging in mainland China with diverse anti-Qing or local defense origins, these groups quickly established a foothold in the new British territory, engaging in mutual aid, labor organization, and eventually illicit activities such as protection rackets, gambling, and extortion.

## What happened

Faced with growing lawlessness and the challenge of policing a rapidly expanding population with limited resources, the colonial administration under Governor **John Francis Davis** (戴維斯) took decisive legislative action. On **March 1, 1845**, the Legislative Council enacted **Ordinance No. 1 of 1845**, which specifically targeted unauthorized secret societies. The law declared such organizations illegal, authorized the deportation of individuals associated with them, and empowered law enforcement to suppress meetings and dismantle their networks. This statute laid the foundational legal framework for decades of subsequent anti-society legislation in the colony.

## Significance

This enactment marked the official commencement of the colonial state's long-running struggle against organized crime syndicates. While the British intended to impose strict rule of law and secure public order, the legislation pushed Triad networks further underground rather than eradicating them. Modern scholarship and historical analysis suggest that these early measures initiated a complex, evolving dynamic of surveillance, corruption, and uneasy accommodation between colonial authorities and underground leaders that would persist for generations.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Authorities viewed the outlawing of Triads as an essential step to establish public order, protect property, and assert the rule of law over a newly acquired, volatile population.
- **Local Chinese communities and underground networks**: Many migrants initially viewed brotherhoods through the lens of traditional mutual aid and local self-defense, while underground leaders adapted to the new laws by deepening their integration into local trade and labor networks.
- **Qing/Beijing perspective**: Imperial Qing officials observed the British handling of anti-Qing secret societies with cautious interest, as these same networks posed continuous subversive threats to dynastic rule across southern China.
