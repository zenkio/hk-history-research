## Background
During the early years of British rule following the **1842** Treaty of Nanking, Hong Kong gained a reputation as a "white man's grave." Military personnel and civilian settlers faced extreme health crises, primarily due to outbreaks of malaria, dysentery, and other tropical diseases. The rapid construction of the nascent capital, Victoria (維多利亞城), often involved the excavation of hillsides, which many contemporary observers believed released "miasma" and exacerbated the incidence of endemic fevers. By the mid-1840s, the high death rate among soldiers and civil servants threatened the stability and economic viability of the new colony.

## What happened
In **1845**, the colonial government took a significant step toward formal health administration by establishing the Colonial Surgeon's Office. This office was tasked with overseeing public health, managing hospital facilities, and providing medical care to government officials and military personnel. The first Colonial Surgeon, **William Morrison**, was appointed to lead these efforts. While the office initially focused on the immediate survival of the British expatriate population, it marked the beginning of a institutionalized approach to public health in Hong Kong. The office functioned as the precursor to the later medical department, setting precedents for medical reporting and sanitary inspections that would gradually expand over the following decades.

## Significance
The formalization of medical oversight reflected the British administration's realization that colonial expansion required a sustainable health infrastructure. The office became the hub for early sanitary data collection, which was essential for justifying later infrastructure projects aimed at improving drainage and water supply. However, the scope of these early medical services remained largely centered on the needs of the colonial administration and the military. For much of the indigenous population and the growing community of Chinese migrants, modern medical care remained largely inaccessible, as healthcare delivery often followed the segregated social and ethnic structures of the time. This period established a precedent of centralized medical governance that would define Hong Kong's public health policy for the remainder of the nineteenth century.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Perspective**: The colonial administration viewed the Surgeon's Office as a vital instrument of modernization and civil administration. They interpreted the establishment as a successful effort to impose order and biological safety upon a challenging, 'unhealthy' colonial frontier.
- **Local and Qing Period Perspective**: For the indigenous Cantonese populations and the Chinese laborers living in the colony, the colonial medical apparatus often seemed detached from their own traditional health practices. Many viewed the new 'Western' sanitary regulations with suspicion, interpreting them as foreign impositions that lacked cultural understanding of traditional hygiene and social organization.
