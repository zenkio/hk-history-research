## Background
Following the formal cession of Hong Kong Island to Great Britain via the Treaty of Nanking (南京條約) in 1842, the British government faced the challenge of governing a territory with a diverse and transient population. By the mid-1840s, the colonial administration sought to establish more formal bureaucratic control to facilitate taxation, urban planning, and the maintenance of public order. The early settlement was primarily concentrated around the areas that would later become Central and Western District.

## What happened
In **1845**, colonial authorities initiated the first systematic enumeration of the population residing on Hong Kong Island. The count was an administrative exercise intended to provide the Governor and the Legislative Council with a clearer understanding of the number of individuals under their jurisdiction. The census returned a figure of approximately **24,000** residents. This population was not uniform; it was primarily composed of Chinese laborers, boat people, and merchants, alongside a small but influential cohort of British civil servants, military personnel, and Western merchant families.

## Significance
The **1845** census serves as a historical milestone, marking the transition from a loosely administered military outpost to a formal colonial civil administration. It provided the first quantitative data for colonial planning, helping the government identify demographic pressures and the distribution of ethnic groups. However, the accuracy of this data remains a point of scholarly debate. Historians note that the rapid mobility of the Chinese population, particularly those living on boats in the harbor, made it notoriously difficult to achieve an precise headcount, suggesting the figure of **24,000** may have been an underestimation of the actual number of people passing through or working in the territory at the time.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: The census was viewed as a necessary tool of 'civilized' governance, enabling the British to impose order, collect revenue, and manage urban development effectively. They regarded the data as a triumph of administrative rigor in a frontier environment.
- **Local Chinese communities**: For the indigenous and migrant Chinese populations, the census was often viewed with suspicion or indifference, as it was perceived primarily as a precursor to taxation or state interference in the traditional autonomy of village and boat-dwelling life.
