## Background
In the years immediately following the British occupation of Hong Kong in **1841**, the newly established colony suffered from devastating disease outbreaks, particularly malaria and dysentery, which earned the settlement a grim reputation for high mortality. The initial burial sites near the central settlement proved inadequate and posed severe sanitary hazards to the living population. By **1845**, colonial authorities recognized the urgent need for a dedicated, large-scale burial ground away from the main residential and commercial districts, leading to the selection of the Happy Valley (跑馬地) area, which was then a swampy valley prone to flooding.

## What happened
In **1845**, the Happy Valley Cemetery was officially opened to receive the massive numbers of deceased soldiers, sailors, merchants, and colonial administrators who succumbed to tropical diseases. The site was intentionally laid out to accommodate multiple religious denominations, reflecting the diverse nationalities and faiths of the early colonial community, including Anglicans, Catholics, and Parsis, alongside distinct sections for military personnel. While the government oversaw its establishment, the early years of the cemetery were marked by the sheer volume of interments, as the colony continued to struggle with sanitation, clean water access, and effective medical care.

## Significance
Later scholarship often examines the cemetery not only as a response to a public health crisis but also as a physical manifestation of the rigid social hierarchy and mortality risks of early British imperialism in Asia. The multi-faith nature of the site highlighted the cosmopolitan yet deeply segregated demographic makeup of early Hong Kong. Furthermore, the establishment of the cemetery marked a permanent alteration of the Happy Valley landscape, which would later evolve from a burial ground and malaria-ridden swamp into a prominent recreational and sporting hub for the colony.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial authorities**: Colonial administrators viewed the establishment of the cemetery as an essential administrative and sanitary measure necessary to maintain public order and address the unprecedented death toll among European troops and civilians.
- **Later scholarship**: Modern historians analyze the cemetery as a physical archive of early colonial society, emphasizing how mortality rates exposed the harsh realities of imperial expansion and the failure of early tropical medicine.
