## Background
Following the formal cession of Hong Kong Island to Great Britain under the **Treaty of Nanking** (南京條約) in **1842**, the colonial government faced the immediate challenge of establishing a formal administrative infrastructure. Initially, the communication of laws, land grants, and government appointments was irregular, relying on notices pinned in public places or private merchant newspapers. As the settlement of Victoria (維多利亞城) began to take shape, officials sought a more reliable method to ensure that colonial regulations were legally binding and accessible to both the British mercantile community and the local population.

## What happened
In **1845**, the colonial government authorized the publication of the **Hong Kong Gazette** (香港憲報). While the precise inaugural issue date remains a subject of ongoing historical investigation due to gaps in early archival records, it functioned as the official record of the Hong Kong government. The publication served as a vehicle for the Governor and the Legislative Council (立法局) to disseminate proclamations, judicial notices, and government statistics. It established a precedent for administrative transparency, requiring that new ordinances be published in the official record to achieve legal validity within the colony.

## Significance
The introduction of the **Hong Kong Gazette** marked a transition from a provisional military outpost to a more structured colonial administration. It provided a permanent record of the colony's legal evolution and colonial governance, offering historians a primary source for understanding the development of early land policies and tax structures. For the contemporary colonial administration, it provided a necessary framework for standardizing the rule of law. The *Gazette* continues to function today as the definitive source for the enactment of Hong Kong’s legislation, maintaining a direct historical link to the administrative practices established in the mid-19th century.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Perspective**: The publication is viewed as an essential step in establishing 'the rule of law' and efficient colonial bureaucracy. It represented the civilizing mission's commitment to orderly governance and administrative transparency.
- **Local and Qing Perspective**: The local population often viewed such publications as tools for foreign control rather than public service. From the Qing (清朝) administrative viewpoint, these notices were illegitimate documents issued by an occupying power on territory still considered part of the Chinese empire.
