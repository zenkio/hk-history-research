## Background
During the early years of the British colony, following the **1842** Treaty of Nanking, Hong Kong experienced a rapid influx of migrants from mainland China. The colonial administration, led by early governors, viewed this mobile population with increasing concern. British officials struggled to establish formal administrative control over a populace they perceived as transient and potentially linked to rising crime rates and secret society activities. By the late **1840s**, the British government sought to establish a more rigid system of social control to secure the nascent port city.

## What happened
In **1848**, the colonial authorities pushed through the Registration Ordinance. This law mandated that all Chinese residents within the territory register with the government, effectively creating a system of mandatory identification. The process required individuals to provide personal information to colonial officials, which was intended to monitor the movement of residents and deter criminal elements. The ordinance was met with immediate resistance from the Chinese merchant class and the general populace, who viewed the measure as an intrusive form of surveillance and a burden on their freedom of movement.

## Significance
The Registration Ordinance remains a pivotal moment in the history of Hong Kong's social management. It marked one of the first major attempts by the British administration to implement a comprehensive demographic record system over the local Chinese population. For the colonial government, it was a necessary step for public security and urban planning. Conversely, for the local community, it reinforced a sense of alienation from the ruling power. Later scholarship often highlights this ordinance as an early manifestation of the 'divide and rule' mentality, where the administration sought to separate and manage the Chinese population as distinct from the European residents. The enforcement of the ordinance was fraught with difficulties, as the transient nature of the population and the resistance from local leaders made consistent implementation challenging throughout the mid-19th century.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: The government viewed the registration as an essential mechanism for maintaining law and order in a turbulent port city. They argued that identifying residents was necessary to prevent illicit activities and ensure the stability of the colony.
- **Local Chinese Community**: Many Chinese residents and merchants perceived the Ordinance as discriminatory and an unnecessary interference in their daily lives. They resisted the measure, fearing that such records could be used for arbitrary taxation or as a means of intimidation by the colonial authorities.
