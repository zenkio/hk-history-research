## Background

Following the British acquisition of Hong Kong in 1841-1842, the young colony rapidly developed into a meeting point between the British Empire and the Qing Empire. While the colonial administration focused primarily on maritime trade and port infrastructure, foreign missionaries sought deeper engagement with Chinese language, philosophy, and society. To understand the local population and facilitate proselytization, Western scholars recognized an urgent need for reliable access to traditional Confucian texts.

## What happened

In **1853**, the missionary and sinologist **James Legge** (理雅各) commenced the publication of his comprehensive translation of the Chinese classical texts. Working from his base in Hong Kong, Legge collaborated with local Chinese scholars, most notably the scholar **Wang Tao** (王韜), who later joined him to assist with textual analysis and commentary. This ambitious publishing effort aimed to render complex philosophical works, such as the Confucian Analects and Mencius, into accessible English while retaining the scholarly rigor demanded by both Western academia and traditional Chinese literati standards.

## Significance

Legge’s translation project laid foundational groundwork for modern sinology and cross-cultural academic exchange. By presenting Chinese thought through a bilingual framework, the publications gradually altered how Western intellectuals perceived Chinese history and philosophy. At the same time, the project highlighted Hong Kong's emerging role not merely as a commercial entrepôt, but as a unique conduit where Western printing technology and Chinese scholarship converged.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Perspective**: Colonial administrators and Western observers viewed the translation projects as evidence of British intellectual stewardship, bringing Eastern philosophy into the global library of the British Empire.
- **Qing / Literati Perspective**: Traditional Qing scholars often maintained mixed views, sometimes appreciating the preservation of textual studies while remaining cautious about foreign interpretations of sacred Confucian canons.
- **Local Hong Kong Perspective**: Local Chinese assistants and scholars, such as Wang Tao, saw the collaborative enterprise as an important platform for projecting Chinese culture outward to the Western world through the unique sanctuary of Hong Kong.
