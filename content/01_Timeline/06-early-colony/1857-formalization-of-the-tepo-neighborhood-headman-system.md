## Background
During the early decades of British colonial rule following the Treaty of Nanking in **1842**, the Hong Kong government faced a rapidly growing Chinese population with limited administrative resources and a severe language barrier. Colonial authorities struggled to maintain public order, collect taxes, and gather intelligence within densely populated Chinese settlements like Victoria City (維多利亞城). To address these challenges, the government increasingly relied on existing informal community structures and indigenous governance traditions.

## What happened
In **1857**, Governor **John Bowring** (寶寧) officially formalized the Tepo (地保) system. Under this arrangement, local Chinese neighborhood headmen, or Tepos, were integrated into the colonial administrative framework. Their responsibilities included acting as intermediaries between the British government and the Chinese population, assisting with local security, reporting crimes, and helping to manage urban administration. This system allowed the colonial state to extend its reach into Chinese communities without deploying large numbers of British police officers or administrators.

## Significance
The formalization of the Tepo system laid the early foundation for indirect rule and community policing in colonial Hong Kong. It established a precedent of co-opting local elites and traditional structures to govern the Chinese majority—a pragmatic approach that would shape British colonial governance for decades to come, influencing later institutions such as the Tung Wah Group of Hospitals (東華醫院) and the District Watch Force (保良局 / 街坊代表制度).

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Colonial officials viewed the Tepo system as a pragmatic and efficient administrative necessity to maintain law and order, bridge the language divide, and extend governance over a large, transient population with minimal financial expenditure.
- **Local Chinese Communities**: For local residents and community leaders, the system provided an official channel of communication with the British authorities, allowing recognized headmen to negotiate community interests and maintain a degree of traditional autonomy.
- **Later Scholarship**: Modern historians analyze the Tepo system as an early manifestation of indirect rule and co-optation, highlighting how colonial authorities utilized traditional social hierarchies to construct a stable apparatus of social control.
