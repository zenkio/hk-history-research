## Background

During the early years of British colonial rule, the administration of Hong Kong relied heavily on intermediaries who understood local languages, customs, and networks. **Daniel Caldwell** (高德), who served as the Registrar General and Protector of Chinese, wielded immense influence due to his fluency in Cantonese and his extensive local connections. However, rumors and accusations regarding his close association with the powerful and notorious Chinese merchant and suspected pirate **Ma-chow Wong** (麥周黃, commonly known as Wong Ma-chow 麥亞周) had circulated within the colony for years.

## What happened

The simmering tensions boiled over in **1858** when Attorney General **Thomas Chisholm Anstey** (安思地) formally brought charges of corruption and collusion against **Daniel Caldwell**. **Anstey** alleged that **Caldwell** was not only protecting **Ma-chow Wong** from prosecution but was also actively sharing in the profits of piracy, extortion, and illegal gambling syndicates operating in and around Hong Kong. The accusations led to intense public hearings, official investigations, and a bitter feud within the upper echelons of the colonial government, pitting **Anstey** directly against **Caldwell** and Governor **Sir John Bowring** (寶寧), who defended **Caldwell**.

## Significance

The scandal laid bare the fragility and compromised integrity of the early colonial governance structure, where reliance on intermediaries often blurred the lines between law enforcement and criminal enterprise. While subsequent official inquiries yielded mixed verdicts and the British Colonial Office ultimately handled the fallout cautiously to preserve administrative stability, the affair severely undermined public trust in colonial institutions. It remains one of the most prominent institutional crises of nineteenth-century Hong Kong, highlighting the inherent tensions of governing a Chinese populace through a small, foreign bureaucratic elite.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Officials at the time often viewed the scandal as a disruptive personal vendetta by Attorney General Anstey against a valuable and uniquely qualified colonial officer, fearing that Caldwell's removal would paralyze the administration's ability to manage the Chinese population.
- **Later historical scholarship**: Modern historians generally view the affair as a symptom of systemic corruption and institutional weakness in early Hong Kong, demonstrating how British reliance on compromised compradors and fixers undermined the rule of law.
