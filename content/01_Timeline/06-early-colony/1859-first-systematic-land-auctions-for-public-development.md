## Background
Following the **Treaty of Nanking** (1842) and the **Convention of Peking** (1860), the British colonial administration faced the immediate necessity of funding the development of the new port city. Initially, land allocation was largely informal and influenced by military and commercial speculation. By the late **1850s**, the colonial administration sought to regularize land management to ensure a predictable revenue stream for public works and administrative costs. This led to the transition from ad-hoc grants to a formalized system of government-led land auctions.

## What happened
Around **1859**, the government of **Sir Hercules Robinson** began implementing more systematic procedures for the public sale of land. While land sales had occurred previously, this period marked a move toward the institutionalization of land as a primary fiscal tool. Auctions became the primary mechanism by which the colonial state disposed of land, granting long-term leases—often for **75 or 99 years**—to the highest bidder. This effectively claimed the land as 'Crown Land' (later termed 'Government Land'), asserting state ownership over the territory’s limited developable space.

## Significance
This institutional shift established the 'high land price' policy. By auctioning land to developers and wealthy merchants, the colonial government generated significant capital, which lowered the necessity for high direct taxation on residents. However, critics argue this reliance on land revenue incentivized the government to keep land supply constrained to maintain high market values. This fundamental reliance on land auctions to fund the budget has remained a defining, and often debated, feature of Hong Kong's fiscal policy from the colonial era through the present day.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: The administration viewed land auctions as a pragmatic, market-driven necessity to generate revenue for infrastructure without imposing heavy taxation on a fledgling mercantile population. It was seen as a fair and transparent way to allocate scarce resources to those best equipped to develop them for the colony's growth.
- **Local Merchant Community**: Local and expatriate merchants often viewed the auction system with ambivalence, as it rewarded those with existing capital while fueling property speculation. While many prospered under this system, others expressed concern that it concentrated wealth and limited access to land for smaller businesses or residential needs.
