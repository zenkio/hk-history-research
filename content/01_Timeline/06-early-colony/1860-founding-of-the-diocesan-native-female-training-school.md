## Background

During the early colonial period, the British administration and missionary groups sought to establish educational institutions to serve the local population. While boys' education received initial priority, educational pioneers recognized a pressing need to provide instruction for girls, who largely lacked access to formal schooling under traditional local norms. In **1860**, efforts culminated in the establishment of the school under the auspices of the Church of England.

## What happened

The institution began as the Diocesan Native Female Training School, later evolving into the Diocesan Girls' School (協恩中學 / 拔萃女書院). It was set up to shelter, Christianize, and educate destitute and local Chinese girls, providing them with basic literacy, religious instruction, and practical domestic skills. The school operated under early colonial missionary frameworks, relying on coordination between church leaders and colonial authorities to secure land and resources.

## Significance

The founding of the school represented a crucial milestone as one of the earliest formal attempts to introduce Western-style education for females in Hong Kong. It laid foundational groundwork for women's social mobility and education in the decades that followed, gradually altering the traditional educational landscape for local Chinese inhabitants.
