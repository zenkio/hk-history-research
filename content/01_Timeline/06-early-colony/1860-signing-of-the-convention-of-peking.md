## Background
Following the conclusion of the Second Opium War, Anglo-French forces pushed for further concessions from the Qing Empire (大清). While the Treaty of Tientsin had been negotiated earlier, hostilities resumed, leading to the occupation of Beijing. British authorities in Hong Kong had long recognized the strategic and military vulnerability of holding only Hong Kong Island without the opposite coastline of the Kowloon Peninsula (九龍半島).

## What happened
On **October 24, 1860**, the **Convention of Peking** (北京條約) was signed by **Lord Elgin** (額爾金) representing Great Britain and **Prince Gong** (恭親王) representing the Qing Dynasty. Under the terms of the convention, the Qing government agreed to cede the southern portion of the Kowloon Peninsula—extending down to what is now **Boundary Street** (界限街) and including Stonecutters Island (昂船洲)—in perpetuity to the British Crown, alongside the opening of Tianjin as a trade port and an increase in indemnity payments.

## Significance
This territorial expansion fundamentally changed the geography and strategic capacity of Hong Kong. By bringing the northern shore of Victoria Harbour under British administration, the colony gained crucial space for military defense, warehousing, and future urban expansion. The acquisition set a precedent for further territorial expansion in the region, culminating in the 1898 Convention for the Extension of Hong Kong Territory, which leased the New Territories.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial perspective**: Viewed as a necessary and pragmatic measure to secure the defensibility of Victoria Harbour, protect burgeoning maritime trade, and provide much-needed space for the growing colonial population.
- **Qing imperial perspective**: Regarded as one of the unequal treaties forced upon a weakened empire under military duress, symbolizing national humiliation and the erosion of territorial sovereignty by Western powers.
