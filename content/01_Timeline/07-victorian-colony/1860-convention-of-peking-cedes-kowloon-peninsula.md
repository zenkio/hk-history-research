## Background

Following the outbreak of the Second Opium War (Arrow War), Anglo-French forces advanced toward Beijing. The British colonial administration in Hong Kong had long viewed the southern tip of the Kowloon Peninsula (九龍半島) as essential for the military defense of Victoria Harbour (維多利亞港) and the regulation of maritime traffic. Without control of the opposite coastline, the British felt vulnerable to potential hostile actions or smuggling operations.

## What happened

On **October 24, 1860**, the **Convention of Peking** (**北京條約**) was signed, concluding the hostilities between Qing China and Great Britain. Under Article 6 of the treaty, the Qing Empire ceded the portion of the Kowloon Peninsula south of present-day **Boundary Street** (**界限街**), along with **Stonecutters Island** (**昂船洲**), to the British Crown "in perpetuity." Previously, in 1860, British troops had already occupied the area under a provisional lease, which was formalized by the treaty.

## Significance

This territorial expansion marked the second major phase in the creation of modern Hong Kong, following the cession of Hong Kong Island in 1841. It provided the colonial government with vital space for military installations, storage, and eventual urban expansion. However, it also set a precedent for further territorial acquisition, culminating decades later in the 1898 lease of the New Territories under the Convention for the Extension of Hong Kong Territory.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Viewed the acquisition as a vital and pragmatic necessity for harbor defense, public health, and commercial stability, transforming a strategic vulnerability into a secure imperial outpost.
- **Qing imperial court**: Regarded the cession as another humiliating unequal treaty forced upon a weakened dynasty under military duress during a period of profound national crisis.
- **Later historical scholarship**: Analyzes the event as a critical stepping stone in British imperial expansion in East Asia, illustrating how localized security concerns steadily drove broader territorial annexation.
