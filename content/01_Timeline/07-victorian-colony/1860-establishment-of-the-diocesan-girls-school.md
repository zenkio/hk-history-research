## Background

During the early decades of British colonial rule following the **Convention of Peking** in **1860**, the educational landscape of Hong Kong was largely shaped by missionary societies and colonial authorities. Initially, formal schooling was sparse and primarily catered to boys, while education for girls, particularly local Chinese girls, received limited institutional support. Christian missionary groups played a pivotal role in filling this gap, seeking to provide moral instruction, basic literacy, and practical skills.

## What happened

In **1860**, the **Diocesan Native Female Training School** was established under the auspices of the **Diocese of Victoria**. The institution was created to provide shelter, care, and education for destitute and orphaned girls, offering them instruction in English, Chinese, and domestic arts. Over subsequent decades, the school underwent several administrative and structural changes, eventually evolving into the **Diocesan Girls' School** (拔萃女書院) and relocating to its prominent campus on **Fairfield Lane** and later **Gascoigne Road**.

## Significance

The founding of the school is widely recognized by historians as a foundational milestone in the history of female education in Hong Kong. By creating pathways for girls to receive structured academic instruction, the institution contributed to the gradual emergence of a literate female workforce and educated elite within the colony. It reflected the broader intersection of Victorian missionary zeal, colonial social policy, and the evolving role of women in Hong Kong society.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial and Missionary Perspective**: Colonial administrators and church officials viewed the establishment of the school as a philanthropic success that brought civilization, Christian values, and educational opportunities to underprivileged local girls.
- **Local and Modern Scholarship**: Later historians and local scholars often examine the school through a more critical lens, noting how early mission schools functioned as agents of cultural assimilation while simultaneously empowering generations of Chinese women through literacy and leadership opportunities.
