## Background

Following the British occupation of Hong Kong Island, the rapidly expanding urban population faced severe freshwater shortages. Natural streams and wells were increasingly insufficient and susceptible to contamination, leading to public health concerns such as cholera outbreaks. Early colonial authorities debated various methods to secure a reliable water supply, eventually settling on the construction of an impounding reservoir in the **Pok Fu Lam** valley, leveraging the area's natural topography and catchment potential.

## What happened

Construction began in the early 1860s, overseen by colonial engineers who utilized both European construction techniques and local labor. The project involved building a masonry dam across the Pok Fu Lam valley to create an artificial lake, alongside a system of catchwaters, tunnels, and piping to convey the water down to the urban areas of **Victoria City**. While precise completion dates and construction casualty figures remain somewhat obscure in surviving administrative records, the reservoir became operational around **1863**, marking a foundational moment in the colony's public works.

## Significance

The completion of the **Pok Fu Lam Reservoir** (薄扶林水塘) fundamentally transformed the sanitary and infrastructural landscape of early colonial Hong Kong. By providing the first centralized, engineered water supply, it enabled further urban expansion and population growth. The project established a precedent for subsequent large-scale hydraulic engineering works in the region, such as the Tai Tam and Aberdeen reservoirs, shifting the colony's approach from reactive crisis management to systematic municipal planning.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Viewed the reservoir as a triumph of modern engineering and a necessary civilizing measure that demonstrated administrative competence and improved public health in a challenging tropical environment.
- **Local residents and laborers**: Experienced the project through the realities of land use changes, labor demands, and the introduction of western hydraulic infrastructure that altered traditional access to streams and hillside resources.
