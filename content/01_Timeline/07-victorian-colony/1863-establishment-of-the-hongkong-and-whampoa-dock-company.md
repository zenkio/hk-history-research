## Background
Following the Convention of Peking in 1860, which ceded the Kowloon Peninsula (九龍半島) to the British, the colonial government sought to expand maritime infrastructure to support the growing trade volume in the region. Prior to this, ship-repair facilities were scattered and smaller in scale, notably including operations at Whampoa near Guangzhou and early ventures in Aberdeen (香港仔) and on the newly acquired Kowloon shoreline.

## What happened
On **July 1, 1863**, businessman **John Sutherland** and key associates formally established the Hongkong and Whampoa Dock Company (香港黃埔船塢公司). The firm consolidated existing dockyards, bringing together capital, land, and technical expertise. Over the subsequent decades, the company expanded its footprint significantly, establishing major dry docks in Hung Hom (紅磡) and at Cosmopolitan Dock in Tai角咀 (Tai Kok Tsui), thereby creating one of the largest industrial enterprises in the British Empire east of Suez.

## Significance
The creation of the dock company marked a pivotal transition for Hong Kong from a purely commercial entrepôt into a sophisticated industrial and maritime hub. It mobilized a vast workforce of Chinese migrant laborers and local artisans, fostering the development of early trade unionism and industrial skills in the colony. While the enterprise generated immense wealth for British mercantile houses, it also integrated Hong Kong deeply into global steamship networks.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration and investors**: Viewed the company's formation as a triumph of Victorian enterprise and engineering that cemented Hong Kong's status as a vital strategic and commercial entrepôt for the British Empire.
- **Local Chinese laborers and community**: Experienced the dockyards as a site of grueling manual labor and early industrial exploitation, while also participating in the formation of an emerging, urban Chinese working class in Kowloon.
