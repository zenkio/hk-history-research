## Background
During the 1860s, the British colony of Hong Kong (香港) faced severe public health challenges, particularly high rates of venereal diseases among the garrisoned British military and naval forces. Influenced by similar legislation in the United Kingdom, such as the Contagious Diseases Acts, colonial authorities sought a system to control the spread of infection. Prostitution was widespread in the rapidly growing urban center, particularly in districts like Tai Ping Shan (太平山) and Sheung Wan (上環), operated largely outside formal state oversight.

## What happened
On **July 19, 1867**, the Legislative Council enacted the Venereal Diseases Ordinance. This controversial legislation established a system of state regulation, requiring brothels to be licensed and registered. Prostitutes working within these establishments were subjected to mandatory, regular medical examinations. Those found infected were forcibly detained in Lock Hospitals for treatment. The implementation of the ordinance marked a significant expansion of colonial state power into the intimate lives and bodies of the local population, primarily affecting Chinese women.

## Significance
The ordinance became a focal point of intense moral, legal, and political debate that lasted for decades. While British military planners viewed the measure as a pragmatic necessity for national defense, critics raised severe humanitarian and ethical objections. The law institutionalized a dual standard of morality and placed a heavy regulatory burden almost exclusively on Chinese women, reflecting the racial and gender hierarchies of the Victorian colony. The controversy eventually contributed to the repeal of the system in the late 19th century, leaving a complex legacy regarding public health, state control, and colonial governance.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Authorities**: Administrators and military officials defended the ordinance as a vital, pragmatic public health measure necessary to maintain the combat readiness and fighting strength of the British armed forces stationed in the region.
- **Local Chinese Communities and Moral Reformers**: Critics, including local community leaders and British social reformers, argued that state-regulated prostitution amounted to official sanction of immorality and subjected vulnerable women to draconian medical policing and human rights abuses.
- **Later Historical Scholarship**: Modern historians frequently analyze the ordinance as a prime example of how colonial governance intersected with gender, race, and biopolitics, demonstrating how the health of the colonizers was prioritized through the systemic control of colonized bodies.
