## Background
Following the Second Opium War and the Convention of Peking in **1860**, the British colonial government formally took possession of the Kowloon Peninsula south of Boundary Street. Initially, the newly acquired territory, known administratively as **New Kowloon**, was sparsely populated, consisting primarily of fishing villages, farming settlements, and granite-quarrying sites. Policing these newly ceded areas was initially handled through occasional patrols from Hong Kong Island rather than a permanent presence.

## What happened
In **1868**, the colonial administration established the first permanent police station in **Tsim Sha Tsui (尖沙咀)**. The facility was modest, designed to house a small detachment of officers tasked with maintaining public order, curbing smuggling, and dealing with piracy along the active maritime routes of **Victoria Harbour (維多利亞港)**. The establishment of this station represented the physical footprint of British law enforcement extending beyond the original limits of **Victoria City (維多利亞城)**.

## Significance
The creation of the Tsim Sha Tsui Police Station is widely viewed as a crucial step in the territorial consolidation of the colony. It laid the groundwork for further infrastructural and administrative penetration into the Kowloon Peninsula, anticipating the much larger territorial expansion that would follow with the Convention for the Extension of Hong Kong Territory in **1898**. However, the expansion of colonial policing also brought new regulatory pressures on local indigenous communities, altering traditional local governance and maritime economies.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Colonial officials viewed the establishment of the police station as a necessary and progressive measure to secure the frontier, protect commerce, and suppress rampant piracy in adjacent waters. It was framed as bringing the rule of law and order to an otherwise volatile and under-governed region.
- **Local Communities and Qing Perspective**: Local villagers and Qing dynasty authorities often regarded expanding British police patrols with suspicion as an aggressive encroachment upon traditional jurisdictions and local autonomy. For coastal and farming communities, the increased presence of colonial law enforcement meant heavier oversight and potential disruption of traditional livelihoods and junk trade.
