## Background
By the **1860s**, the British administration in Hong Kong faced the challenge of governing a rapidly expanding population governed by traditional Chinese social customs. Among these was the 'Mui Tsai' system, a practice where young girls from impoverished families were placed into the households of wealthier families to work as domestic servants. While intended to alleviate family poverty, the status of these girls often placed them in a precarious position between familial adoption and servitude. As the colony expanded following the **1860** Convention of Peking (北京條約), British authorities sought to balance the enforcement of English common law with the preservation of social stability through the accommodation of existing Chinese customs.

## What happened
In approximately **1870**, the colonial government, acting with a degree of pragmatic tolerance, opted to treat the Mui Tsai system as a form of apprenticeship or domestic contract rather than slavery. The colonial authorities interpreted these arrangements as benign employment within the Chinese household structure. By acknowledging the practice, the administration implicitly provided a legal space for the transfer and employment of these minors. While some colonial officials expressed private discomfort regarding the treatment of these individuals, the government generally refrained from direct intervention, preferring to permit local community leaders and family patriarchs to manage household affairs without external oversight.

## Significance
The decision to permit the Mui Tsai system became one of the most enduring humanitarian controversies in Hong Kong’s colonial history. It highlighted the friction between the British colonial government’s self-proclaimed status as a 'civilizing' liberal power and its pragmatic reliance on the stability of traditional Chinese social hierarchies. The system remained a focal point for social reformists, missionaries, and eventually the Anti-Mui Tsai Society (反對蓄婢會) well into the 20th century. Academics remain divided over whether this period of colonial accommodation was a deliberate strategy to appease the local Chinese elite to ensure trade stability or a genuine administrative failure to recognize the human rights implications of the domestic service status.

## Perspectives

- **Colonial Administration**: Authorities viewed the Mui Tsai system as a cultural necessity that prevented female infanticide and abandonment in extreme poverty. They argued that regulating the system through local norms maintained social order and minimized cultural friction between the government and the Chinese mercantile class.
- **Local Reformers and Later Scholarship**: Critics within the colony and modern historians often describe the colonial legalization as a failure to uphold basic human rights. Scholars argue that the government prioritized political expediency and economic stability over the protection of vulnerable minors, effectively shielding exploitative practices under the guise of 'cultural tradition'.
