## Background
By the mid-19th century, the expansion of the British Empire and the booming China trade created an urgent demand for faster communication between London and Asian outposts. Traditional mail carried by steamships took months to complete the journey around the Cape of Good Hope or via the overland route through Egypt. The development of submarine telegraphy in the 1850s and 1860s offered a technological solution to bridge this vast geographic distance.

## What happened
On **April 18, 1871**, the submarine telegraph cable officially reached Hong Kong, linking the colony to Singapore, India, and ultimately Great Britain through the enterprise of the **Great Northern Telegraph Company** and the **Eastern Extension, Australasia and China Telegraph Company**. Messages that previously required weeks or months to transmit could now be sent and received in a matter of hours, fundamentally transforming commercial and administrative correspondence.

## Significance
The arrival of the telegraph cable accelerated the integration of Hong Kong into the global capitalist economy. Information regarding commodity prices, shipping movements, and political developments could now flow instantaneously, reducing the risks of international trade and tightening the administrative grip of the British Colonial Office over its distant possession. It cemented Hong Kong's status as a premier entrepôt in East Asia, shifting the tempo of daily commercial life from the arrival schedule of mail steamers to real-time market fluctuations.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Viewed the telegraph as a triumph of modern engineering and administration that enhanced imperial security, economic efficiency, and governance by enabling instantaneous communication with London.
- **Qing officialdom**: Regarded foreign telegraph lines with suspicion as potential instruments of foreign intrusion and espionage, though local officials gradually recognized their practical utility for commerce.
