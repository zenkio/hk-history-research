## Background

By the late **1860s**, the British colonial administration in Hong Kong recognized the need for a dedicated public green space and a scientific facility to study regional flora. Situated on the slopes above Central, the site was chosen to provide both a healthful promenade for European residents seeking relief from the congested lower districts and a practical station for acclimatizing economically valuable plants.

## What happened

Construction of the gardens proceeded in phases, culminating in the formal opening on **August 6, 1871**. Designed under the supervision of colonial officials and early superintendents, the layout featured terraced gardens, aviaries, and greenhouses. The grounds were planted with a mix of native species and exotic flora imported from other parts of the British Empire, serving as an experimental hub for botanical research and forestry.

## Significance

The gardens quickly became a central fixture of Victorian social life in the colony, offering a controlled environment that mirrored domestic British parks while adapting to the subtropical climate. From a scientific perspective, the institution contributed to the understanding of regional botany and the propagation of useful crops. Modern scholarship often examines the gardens as a physical manifestation of colonial authority, landscape domestication, and Victorian notions of public health and leisure.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Viewed the gardens as a civilizing amenity that improved public health, demonstrated imperial botanical prowess, and provided orderly recreation for the colony's residents.
- **Local Chinese and regional perspective**: Interpreted the creation of restricted colonial pleasure grounds alongside urban development as part of the broader spatial segregation and landscape transformation imposed by the British authorities.
