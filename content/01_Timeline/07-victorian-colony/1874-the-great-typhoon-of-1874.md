## Background
In the late 19th century, the British colony of Hong Kong (香港) was rapidly expanding as a global maritime trading hub. However, meteorological science was still in its infancy, and early colonial authorities lacked systematic methods for predicting extreme weather events or issuing advance warnings to ships anchored in Victoria Harbour (維多利亞港) and the surrounding region.

## What happened
On **September 22, 1874**, a catastrophic typhoon struck the region without adequate prior warning. The storm packed ferocious winds and a massive storm surge that battered the coastline, wreaking unprecedented havoc. Numerous European sailing vessels, Chinese junks, and steamships were sunk, driven ashore, or smashed to pieces within the harbor. On land, thousands of buildings collapsed, particularly in lower-lying districts and vulnerable coastal villages. Estimates of the death toll vary widely, but historical accounts indicate that thousands of lives were lost, predominantly among Chinese sailors, fishermen, and local residents.

## Significance
The disaster exposed severe vulnerabilities in Hong Kong's disaster preparedness and maritime infrastructure. In the aftermath, colonial authorities, commercial interests, and scientific advocates intensified calls for a reliable storm-warning system. These efforts eventually contributed to the establishment of more systematic meteorological observations and the eventual founding of the Hong Kong Observatory (香港天文台), transforming the colony into a pioneer of regional typhoon tracking.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Colonial officials viewed the disaster as a tragic wake-up call regarding infrastructure fragility, prompting urgent investments in harbor defenses, building regulations, and meteorological science to protect imperial trade interests.
- **Local Chinese community**: Local fishermen, laborers, and residents suffered the heaviest casualties and property destruction, relying on traditional community networks and temples for disaster relief and mourning in the absence of comprehensive social welfare.
- **Qing officialdom**: Imperial Qing authorities viewed the devastation through the lens of traditional dynastic historiography, documenting the natural disaster alongside coastal relief efforts while observing the growing foreign presence in the treaty port.
