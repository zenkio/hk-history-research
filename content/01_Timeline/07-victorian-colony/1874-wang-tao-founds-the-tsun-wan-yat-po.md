## Background

By the early 1870s, Hong Kong had developed a growing Chinese print media sector, though many early publications were either backed by Western missionaries or colonial interests. **Wang Tao** (王韜), a scholar and journalist from Jiangsu who had fled to Hong Kong following his involvement with the Taiping Heavenly Kingdom and subsequent suspicion by Qing authorities, recognized the potential of the colony's relative freedom of the press. With financial backing from local merchants, particularly **Ng Choy** (伍才, later known as Wu Tingfang), Wang sought to create an independent platform to voice Chinese perspectives on local and international affairs.

## What happened

On **February 5, 1874**, the *Tsun Wan Yat Po* (循環日報) commenced publication in **Hong Kong** (香港). As editor and manager, Wang Tao utilized the daily paper not only to report commercial and local news but also to publish influential editorials advocating for institutional reform, modernization, and self-strengthening within China. The newspaper quickly became a vital conduit of political and social thought, bridging traditional Chinese scholarship with Western concepts of governance and technology.

## Significance

The founding of the *Tsun Wan Yat Po* is widely recognized as a turning point in Chinese journalism. It established a precedent for Chinese-run newspapers operating outside direct mainland censorship, allowing intellectuals to critique both Qing dynastic conservatism and certain aspects of colonial governance. The essays published by Wang Tao in the paper laid foundational arguments for late-Qing reform movements, deeply influencing subsequent generations of Chinese reformers and revolutionaries.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Perspective**: Colonial authorities generally viewed the burgeoning Chinese-language press through the lens of public order and economic utility, welcoming the commercial integration it fostered while monitoring editorials for potential anti-British incitement.
- **Qing Official Perspective**: Imperial authorities in Beijing and Guangdong often regarded Wang Tao and his publications with suspicion, viewing the newspaper as a vehicle for seditious ideas and a safe haven for anti-dynastic elements operating under British protection.
- **Local Reformist and Scholar Perspective**: Chinese intellectuals and local merchants saw the Tsun Wan Yat Po as a crucial voice for the Chinese community in Hong Kong and a necessary catalyst for modernizing China through intellectual and institutional reform.
