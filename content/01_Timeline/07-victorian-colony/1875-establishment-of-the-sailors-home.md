## Background
By the mid-1870s, Hong Kong had solidified its position as a primary international entrepôt. As the volume of steamship traffic increased, the colony saw a significant influx of merchant sailors who were often left without formal accommodation or support networks while their vessels were in port. The district of West Point (西營盤), characterized by its proximity to the docks, became a hub for transient maritime workers. Concerns grew among colonial administrators and religious organizations regarding the lack of regulated boarding facilities, as sailors were frequently exposed to exploitative environments and social instability.

## What happened
In **1875**, a purpose-built facility known as the Sailors' Home (海員之家) was formally established. The project was championed by a committee of local merchants and religious leaders, including the **Bishop of Victoria**, **John Shaw Burdon** (包爾騰). The primary objective was to offer affordable, respectable lodging, reading rooms, and recreational activities to provide a moral alternative to the taverns and boarding houses located elsewhere in the city. The institution operated under a board of trustees, aiming to secure the welfare of seamen while maintaining order in the busy port area. The site served as a vital node for communication and respite, functioning as a bridge between the transient lives of sailors and the more settled life of the colonial port.

## Significance
The establishment of the Sailors' Home marked an early attempt by the colonial authorities and civic groups to manage the social consequences of maritime trade. By providing a controlled environment, the Home aimed to reduce incidents of disorder and illness among the transient population. While intended as a philanthropic endeavor, the institution also served a pragmatic function in protecting the commercial stability of the port by ensuring that maritime crews were fit and ready for service. The Home remains a notable example of the paternalistic social welfare structures that emerged during the Victorian era to integrate the transient maritime labor force into the colony’s developing social and economic fabric.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: The administration viewed the Home as a necessary civilizing influence that reduced public disorder and managed the 'floating population' of sailors. It was seen as a success in modernizing the colony's social infrastructure to meet the standards of a major British port.
- **Later Historical Scholarship**: Critics and modern historians often note that the Home served to enforce social discipline among marginalized transient workers. They argue it reflected the Victorian-era preoccupation with moral reform and control over non-settled maritime populations.
