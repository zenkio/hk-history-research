## Background
During the mid-to-late **19th century**, the British colonial administration in Hong Kong sought to develop structured educational institutions to serve the growing population of the growing trading port. While initial educational efforts were largely driven by various missionary groups, the demand for secondary education tailored to boys from diverse backgrounds—including European, Eurasian, and local Chinese communities—prompted further institutional expansion.

## What happened
In **1876**, the **De La Salle Brothers** (Christian Brothers) took over the running of the existing St. Saviour's College, which eventually led to the establishment and formal growth of **St. Joseph's College** (聖若瑟書院). The institution relocated to prominent sites over time, notably Caine Road, providing structured Western-style secondary education that emphasized commercial subjects, languages, and sciences alongside moral instruction. 

## Significance
The founding of St. Joseph's College marked a major milestone in the development of Western education in **Hong Kong** (香港). By expanding secondary schooling opportunities for boys, the institution contributed to the training of a bilingual local elite who would later serve in administrative, commercial, and legal roles across the British colony and the wider region.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Colonial authorities viewed the expansion of mission schools like St. Joseph's College as a cost-effective way to provide education and foster a loyal, bilingual administrative class without over-relying on direct government funding.
- **Lasallian Brothers and Catholic Church**: The religious orders regarded the establishment of the college as a vital mission to provide moral and academic instruction, expanding Catholic educational outreach to the youth of the rapidly developing colony.
