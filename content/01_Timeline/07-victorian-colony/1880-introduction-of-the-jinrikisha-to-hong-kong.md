## Background

By the late **18th century**, Hong Kong's urban center on Hong Kong Island presented a distinct geographical challenge. Steep hillsides and narrow streets made wheeled transport difficult, leaving the Sedan chair (轎) as the primary mode of personal transport for the affluent, while the working classes traveled largely on foot. As the colony's population and commercial activity expanded following the Convention of Peking in **1860**, colonial authorities and local businessmen sought more efficient and affordable means of mass transit to navigate the growing settlement.

## What happened

Around **1880**, the jinrikisha (人力車), a two-wheeled passenger cart pulled by a runner, was introduced to Hong Kong from Japan, where it had been invented in the late **1860s**. The new vehicles offered a middle ground between the expensive sedan chairs and walking, rapidly gaining popularity among merchants, clerks, and travelers. The colonial government established regulatory frameworks, including licensing systems and fare structures, to manage the burgeoning fleet of rickshaws and the men who pulled them.

## Significance

The arrival of the jinrikisha transformed the streetscape and daily rhythms of colonial Hong Kong. It provided a faster, more accessible form of public transportation across the flat districts of Victoria City and later Kowloon. Simultaneously, the trade became a vital source of livelihood for thousands of working-class male laborers, often referred to at the time as coolies. However, the profession was physically grueling, raising early concerns regarding labor conditions, health, and social welfare among the colony's urban poor.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Colonial officials viewed the jinrikisha as a progressive modernization of urban infrastructure that improved traffic flow, commerce, and municipal organization through systematic licensing.
- **Local working-class pullers**: For the impoverished laborers, rickshaw pulling offered a crucial, albeit grueling, means of economic survival in a rapidly expanding colonial economy characterized by high living costs and limited alternative employment.
- **Later historical scholarship**: Modern historians examine the rickshaw trade within the broader framework of Asian technological transfer, labor exploitation, and the social stratification of Victorian-era Hong Kong.
