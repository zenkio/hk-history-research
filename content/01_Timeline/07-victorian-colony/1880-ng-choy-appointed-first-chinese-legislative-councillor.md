## Background

Following the Convention of Beijing in **1860**, which ceded the Kowloon Peninsula to Britain, the British colony of Hong Kong experienced steady commercial growth and an expanding Chinese population. Governance during this early Victorian era was heavily centralized in the hands of the British Governor and an Executive and Legislative Council composed entirely of European officials and prominent non-official merchants. By the late **1870s**, the growing economic influence and social standing of the Chinese merchant elite created mounting pressure for formal political representation within the colonial administration.

## What happened

In **1880**, **Ng Choy** (伍珅), also known as **Wu Tingfang** (伍廷芳), was appointed to the Legislative Council (立法局) as the first Chinese unofficial member. Born in Singapore and educated in Hong Kong and Britain, **Ng Choy** was the first ethnic Chinese to be called to the English bar. His appointment by Governor **John Pope Hennessy** (弗朗斯) was intended to bridge the growing divide between the colonial government and the local Chinese community, providing a formal channel for Chinese interests within the legislative process.

## Significance

Historians generally view **Ng Choy**'s appointment as a watershed moment for local political representation, establishing a precedent for Chinese participation in the colonial government. However, the exact motivations and long-term impact remain interpreted differently across various perspectives. While it integrated elite Chinese into the colonial structure, it did not extend democratic rights to the broader population, as both European and Chinese members were appointed rather than elected.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Colonial authorities viewed the appointment as a progressive step in administrative efficiency and consultative governance, designed to secure the loyalty and cooperation of the wealthy Chinese merchant class.
- **Local Chinese Elite**: Prominent Chinese merchants saw the appointment as a long-overdue recognition of their growing economic importance and social status within the colony, though representation remained restricted to a narrow, privileged elite.
- **Qing Dynasty Officials**: Qing authorities in Beijing took a cautious interest in the legal status and growing integration of overseas Chinese subjects under British colonial rule, often monitoring how such appointments affected imperial jurisdiction and diaspora loyalties.
