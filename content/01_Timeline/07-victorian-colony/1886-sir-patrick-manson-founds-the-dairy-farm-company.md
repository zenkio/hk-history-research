## Background

During the late **19th century**, Hong Kong faced severe public health challenges. The rapid population growth in the Victorian colony strained sanitation infrastructure, and infectious diseases posed constant threats to residents. Fresh milk was scarce, and what was available often came from unverified sources, carrying risks of contamination and water-borne illnesses. **Sir Patrick Manson** (麥遜), a prominent Scottish physician practicing in the colony, recognized the urgent need for a reliable, hygienic dairy supply to combat infant mortality and general malnutrition.

## What happened

In **July 1886**, **Sir Patrick Manson** partnered with a group of local businessmen to officially found the Dairy Farm Company (牛奶公司) in **Pok Fu Lam** (**薄扶林**). Manson imported **80** dairy cows from the United Kingdom to establish a herd free from local diseases. The company set up its operations in the rural hills of **Pok Fu Lam**, utilizing the open pastures for grazing and establishing a distribution network to deliver fresh, safer milk to urban areas on **Hong Kong Island** (**香港島**).

## Significance

The establishment of the Dairy Farm Company marked a major turning point in Hong Kong's public health history. By introducing pasteurization methods later on and maintaining strict veterinary standards, the venture drastically reduced the incidence of milk-borne diseases. It transformed local dietary habits, making dairy accessible beyond the colonial elite, and laid the institutional groundwork for modern agribusiness and health regulation in Hong Kong.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Viewed the initiative as a triumph of Victorian enterprise and applied science, successfully modernizing the colony's infrastructure and safeguarding European and local health standards.
- **Local Hong Kong communities**: Initially regarded western dairy practices and imported cattle with caution, though urban residents gradually embraced the reliable milk supply as living standards shifted.
