## Background

During the late 19th century, the colonial healthcare system in Hong Kong was largely segregated and limited, leaving the rapidly growing local Chinese population with restricted access to Western medical treatment. Traditional Chinese medicine remained prevalent, but Christian missionary organizations increasingly viewed medical philanthropy as a vital tool for evangelism and humanitarian relief. On **February 16, 1887**, these efforts culminated in a significant institutional development supported by the **London Missionary Society** (倫敦傳道會).

## What happened

The **Alice Memorial Hospital** (雅麗氏紀念醫院) was established on **Hollywood Road** (荷李活道) in **Sheung Wan** (上環). The institution was funded through a generous endowment by **Ho Kai** (何啟), a prominent barrister and Chinese leader in the colony, in loving memory of his late wife, **Alice Ho Mian-soc** (雅麗氏). Operated under the auspices of the **London Missionary Society**, the hospital aimed specifically to offer free or low-cost Western medical and surgical care to the impoverished Chinese residents who could otherwise not afford professional treatment.

Shortly after its opening, the hospital became closely intertwined with medical education. Driven by the vision of **Ho Kai** and medical missionaries such as **Dr. Patrick Manson** (孟生), the facility helped pave the way for the establishment of the **Hong Kong College of Medicine for Chinese** (香港華人西醫書院) later that same year. This educational initiative sought to train local Chinese students in Western medical science, eventually evolving into the Faculty of Medicine at the **University of Hong Kong** (香港大學).

## Significance

The founding of the Alice Memorial Hospital marked a pivotal milestone in the evolution of Hong Kong's public health infrastructure. By bridging Western medical practices with the local Chinese community, it helped shift colonial healthcare paradigms toward broader institutional care for the underprivileged. Furthermore, its role in incubating formal medical education left a lasting legacy on the professionalization of medicine in Hong Kong and the wider region.

From a historiographical standpoint, the hospital exemplifies the complex interplay between British colonial philanthropy, Christian missionary zeal, and the agency of local Chinese elites like **Ho Kai**, who sought to modernize local institutions while navigating Victorian-era society.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Colonial officials viewed the hospital as a welcome supplement to government responsibilities, easing the burden of public health management among the indigent Chinese population through private and missionary charity.
- **Local Chinese Elites and Missionaries**: Figures like Ho Kai and the London Missionary Society regarded the institution as an essential bridge for cultural and religious engagement, using Western medicine to uplift the local community and foster modern professional education.
