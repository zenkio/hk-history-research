## Background

By the late 1880s, Hong Kong had transitioned from a mere entrepôt to a significant centre for light manufacturing and maritime services. With the colony having been ceded the Kowloon Peninsula (九龍半島) in 1860, the British administration sought to consolidate the territory's economic identity. Under the governorship of **Sir George William Des Vœux**, the colonial government, supported by the local mercantile elite, aimed to demonstrate the stability and productivity of the crown colony to the international community and investors.

## What happened

In **1887**, the First Colonial Exhibition was organised to showcase local produce, craftsmanship, and the efficiency of Hong Kong’s maritime infrastructure. Held within the vicinity of the colonial administrative district, the event featured displays of tea, silk, sugar, and various manufactured goods produced by nascent local industries. It served as a precursor to more formal trade fairs, drawing attendees from colonial officials, visiting merchants, and local residents. The event highlighted the integration of traditional Chinese artisanal techniques with modern industrial practices facilitated by British administrative influence. While official records of the specific venue and attendance numbers are subjects of debate among historians, the event was widely reported in contemporary English-language publications as a milestone in the colony’s commercial development.

## Significance

For the British colonial administration, the exhibition was a tool for 'soft power,' used to project the image of Hong Kong as a modern, civilised gateway to East Asia. It was intended to boost the colony's visibility amidst the competition for trade with treaty ports in mainland China. For local Hong Kong merchants, the event provided a platform to legitimise their status and demonstrate their essential role in the colonial economy. However, the exhibition also highlighted the growing economic divide between the modernised colonial port and the traditional agrarian sectors of the New Territories, which would not be leased to Britain until 1898. Later scholarship has frequently debated whether such exhibitions truly reflected the economic realities of the broader population, or if they were carefully curated portrayals of progress designed to impress the British Colonial Office.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: The exhibition was viewed as a testament to the success of colonial governance and free-trade principles. It served to justify the ongoing development of the colony's infrastructure and its status as a vital imperial asset.
- **Local Chinese merchant class**: Many merchants saw the exhibition as an opportunity to secure their standing within the colonial social hierarchy and attract foreign investment. It was viewed as a bridge between the traditional guild-based economy and the modern international trading environment.
- **Qing dynasty officials**: The Qing (清) court generally viewed the rapid economic industrialisation of British-occupied Hong Kong with apprehension. They often perceived such displays as evidence of the colonial erosion of local sovereignty and economic autonomy.
