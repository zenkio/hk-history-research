## Background

During the mid-to-late 19th century following the British colonial acquisition of the Kowloon Peninsula in 1860, the medical infrastructure of Hong Kong sought to expand beyond basic quarantine and military healthcare. British colonial authorities and medical missionaries recognized a pressing need to train local Chinese practitioners in Western medicine to address widespread public health challenges in the growing colony. Prior to **1887**, formal medical training for Chinese students following Western curricula was virtually non-existent in the territory, relying instead on traditional Chinese medical practices.

## What happened

The Hong Kong College of Medicine for Chinese was officially opened in **October 1887**. The institution was founded largely through the efforts of **Patrick Manson**, a prominent physician associated with the London Missionary Society, along with support from colonial officials including Governor **George William Des Vœux**. The college offered a rigorous multi-year curriculum modeled on British medical standards. Among its earliest cohort of students was **Sun Yat-sen** (孫中山), who enrolled at the college and later became its very first graduate, completing his medical studies in **1892**.

## Significance

The opening of the college marked a major milestone in the educational and medical history of the British colony, creating a pathway for local Chinese elites to access professional Western scientific training. While designed primarily to supply qualified medical personnel for Hong Kong and surrounding regions, the institution inadvertently played a pivotal role in modern history by fostering an intellectual environment where **Sun Yat-sen** and his peers developed the reformist and revolutionary ideas that eventually contributed to the overthrow of the Qing dynasty.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Colonial authorities viewed the institution as a successful philanthropic and educational initiative that demonstrated the civilizing benefits of British rule and modern science to the local Chinese population.
- **Qing officialdom**: Imperial Qing officials regarded the influx of Western education and institutions in the British colony with cautious detachment, though some reform-minded officials later acknowledged the practical utility of Western medicine and technical training.
- **Local Hong Kong and later scholarship**: Historians and local commentators often emphasize the college's dual legacy: its foundational contribution to Hong Kong's medical system and its unintended role as a cradle for Chinese republican nationalism through its association with Sun Yat-sen.
