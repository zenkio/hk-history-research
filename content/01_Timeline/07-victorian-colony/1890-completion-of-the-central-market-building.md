## Background
By the late 19th century, the growing urban population of the British colony of Hong Kong placed significant strain on existing sanitation and food distribution networks. The colonial government, operating under the auspices of the sanitary boards and public works departments, sought to replace older, decentralized wet markets with modern, easily regulated facilities. The site of the Central Market, located strategically in the heart of Victoria City (維多利亞城), had long served as a trading hub for local provisions.

## What happened
In **1890**, a newly constructed market building was completed to streamline the sale of meat, fish, and vegetables while enforcing stricter colonial health and hygiene ordinances. The building utilized contemporary Victorian architectural and engineering methods, including iron framing and improved ventilation systems, to manage the tropical climate and large crowds. By consolidating various smaller street hawkers and scattered stalls into a single municipal structure, the administration aimed to curb the spread of disease and improve tax collection and market oversight.

## Significance
The establishment of the **1890** Central Market building reflected broader imperial ambitions to impose order and European sanitary standards upon Asian port cities. It transformed the daily economic rhythms of Victoria City by institutionalizing state oversight over daily sustenance. While initially viewed by colonial authorities as a triumph of public health engineering, the market also represented a profound shift for local vendors, who transitioned from independent trading spaces to heavily regulated municipal stalls.
