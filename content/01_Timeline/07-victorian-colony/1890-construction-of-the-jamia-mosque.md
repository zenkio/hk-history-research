## Background

By the late nineteenth century, the Muslim population in the British colony of Hong Kong had expanded significantly. Originally established in the 1840s or 1850s on Shelley Street (摩羅廟街), the colony's earliest mosque, then known as the Shelley Street Mosque and later as the Jamia Mosque (中環些利街清真寺), could no longer adequately serve the growing number of worshippers. This community was increasingly diverse, comprising South Asian soldiers and policemen in British service, Middle Eastern merchants, and local Chinese converts.

## What happened

In **1890**, construction work was undertaken to rebuild and expand the existing modest structure into a larger place of worship. The project was supported by contributions from the local Muslim community. The expanded building provided improved facilities for daily prayers and community gatherings, reflecting the permanent settlement of Muslim residents in the urban areas of Hong Kong Island.

## Significance

The reconstruction of the Jamia Mosque stands as a physical testament to the multi-ethnic fabric of colonial Hong Kong. While the British administration generally maintained a policy of religious non-interference and permitted various communities to establish their own institutions, the mosque highlights how different diaspora groups integrated into the colony's urban landscape. For later historians, the site illustrates the social stratification and community networks of nineteenth-century Hong Kong, bridging the gap between colonial military/administrative apparatuses and civilian religious life.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Viewed the construction through the lens of legal property rights and religious tolerance, ensuring that diverse ethnic groups within the police and military had spaces to practice their faith without disrupting colonial order.
- **Local Muslim community**: Regarded the expansion as a vital spiritual milestone and a physical anchor for a population bridging South Asia, the Middle East, and southern China.
