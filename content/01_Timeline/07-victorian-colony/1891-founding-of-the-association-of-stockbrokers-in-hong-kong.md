## Background

By the late 19th century, Hong Kong had firmly established itself as a vital entrepôt for trade between China and the West. While informal share trading had taken place in the colony for years—often centered around the **Hong Kong and Shanghai Banking Corporation** and other leading mercantile houses—there was no regulated marketplace for securities. Rapid economic growth and the expansion of local enterprises created an urgent demand for a centralized and reliable mechanism to raise capital and trade shares.

## What happened

On **February 3, 1891**, a group of prominent local brokers officially founded the **Association of Stockbrokers in Hong Kong** (香港股票經紀協會). This institution laid the foundational framework for organized securities trading in the territory. It established formal rules for transactions, standardized broker credentials, and created a unified trading floor, transitioning the colony's financial sector away from informal, ad-hoc gatherings in hotels and offices.

## Significance

The creation of the association marked a watershed moment in the financial history of Hong Kong, transforming it from a purely mercantile port into a sophisticated international financial hub. By providing a structured capital market, the exchange facilitated the funding of major regional infrastructure and commercial ventures. It served as the direct precursor to the modern **Hong Kong Stock Exchange** (香港聯合交易所), cementing the colony's long-term economic trajectory.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Colonial officials viewed the establishment of a formal stock association as a sign of economic maturity and self-regulation, aligning the colony's financial practices more closely with London and bolstering confidence in the British legal and commercial framework.
- **Local merchants and financiers**: Local brokers and business leaders saw the exchange as an essential tool to mobilize capital independently, reducing reliance on foreign agency houses and better serving the specific commercial needs of the China coast trade.
