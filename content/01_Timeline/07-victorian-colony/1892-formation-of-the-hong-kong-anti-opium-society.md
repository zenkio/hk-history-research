## Background
By the late **19th century**, the British colonial administration in Hong Kong derived a significant portion of its total public revenue from the opium monopoly, known as the 'Opium Farm' (鴉片公賣). This fiscal dependency created a direct conflict between the government’s need for income and the growing moral and public health concerns regarding opium addiction. While the colonial authorities often framed the tax as a necessary regulatory tool to control consumption, critics both locally and internationally increasingly viewed the trade as a source of exploitation and societal decay.

## What happened
In **1892**, a group of diverse activists founded the Hong Kong Anti-Opium Society (香港拒毒會). The membership was notable for its collaboration between Chinese elites, such as **Ho Kai** (何啟), and Western figures, including members of the missionary community. The Society aimed to raise public awareness about the physical and moral dangers of opium, while simultaneously lobbying the colonial government to cease its reliance on opium-derived tax income. The activists leveraged growing international anti-opium sentiment, often coordinating their efforts with broader movements in Britain, such as the Society for the Suppression of the Opium Trade. They held public meetings and published pamphlets to challenge the narrative that the opium trade was essential to the economic survival of the colony.

## Significance
The formation of the Society represents one of the earliest instances of a localized, multi-ethnic reform movement in Hong Kong. It highlighted a growing rift between the colonial executive branch and the burgeoning professional class who sought to modernize the colony's social ethics. While the Society did not immediately dismantle the opium tax—which remained a cornerstone of government finance until the early **20th century**—it succeeded in placing the ethics of the trade at the center of public discourse. Later scholars often point to this group as a precursor to subsequent Chinese intellectual and nationalist movements that identified the opium trade as a symbol of colonial subordination and national humiliation.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: The colonial government generally argued that the opium tax was a pragmatic revenue stream that allowed for the funding of essential infrastructure and public services. Officials often contended that a total prohibition would only drive the trade into the hands of unregulated criminal syndicates.
- **Chinese Reformers and Local Elites**: Reformers viewed the trade as a destructive blight that eroded the moral fabric of the Chinese community. They argued that the British reliance on this 'blood money' was evidence of a hypocritical colonial policy that prioritized fiscal gains over the well-being of the local population.
- **Qing Dynasty Government**: Beijing's perspective was complicated; while the Qing government officially condemned the opium trade as a source of national instability, it sometimes struggled to enforce bans within its own borders or viewed the Hong Kong-based protest movements with caution as they represented shifting power dynamics in the treaty ports.
