## Background

In **May 1894**, a severe bubonic plague outbreak struck Hong Kong (香港), originating from southern China. The epidemic caused widespread panic and significant loss of life, particularly straining the densely populated Tai Ping Shan (太平山) district of Victorian-era colonial Hong Kong. British colonial authorities struggled to contain the rapid spread of the disease using contemporary sanitation and quarantine measures, prompting the importation of international medical experts to investigate the pathogen.

## What happened

On **20 June 1894**, **Alexandre Yersin** (耶爾森), a Swiss-French physician and bacteriologist dispatched by the French government and the Pasteur Institute, successfully isolated the causative agent of the plague. Working under primitive conditions inside a small, makeshift straw hut near the epidemic zone, Yersin examined the lymph nodes of plague victims and identified the bacillus (*Yersinia pestis*). Concurrently, Japanese bacteriologist **Kitasato Shibasaburō** (北里柴三郎) was also conducting independent research in Hong Kong, though historical consensus later credited Yersin with the definitive discovery due to the clarity and precision of his bacteriological descriptions.

## Significance

The discovery marked a monumental breakthrough in modern medicine and epidemiology, solving an ancient medical mystery that had plagued humanity for centuries. By identifying the specific microorganism responsible for the disease, Yersin laid the groundwork for future developments in diagnostic testing, effective treatments, and targeted vaccinations, forever altering global public health strategies.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Authorities**: Viewed the scientific breakthrough as a vital tool for eventually restoring public order and trade, though initial sanitary interventions like forced house-to-house searches caused severe civil unrest among the local population.
- **View**: Resented the heavy-handed colonial health policies, mandatory hospitalizations, and whitewashing of neighborhoods, viewing Western medical interventions with deep suspicion while traditional practitioners continued their own care methods.
