## Background
By the late **19th century**, the British colony of Hong Kong had experienced rapid population growth, particularly in crowded Chinese residential areas like Tai Ping Shan (太平山) on Hong Kong Island. Poor sanitation, inadequate ventilation, and substandard housing created ideal conditions for the proliferation of rats and the spread of infectious disease. In **early 1894**, the Third Plague Pandemic, which had originated in Yunnan, China, reached the bustling port city via maritime trade routes.

## What happened
In **May 1894**, cases of bubonic plague surged dramatically in Tai Ping Shan (太平山). The epidemic caused severe symptoms, including high fever and painful swelling of the lymph nodes, leading to a high mortality rate among the affected population. To combat the crisis, colonial authorities implemented draconian measures. Troops and police cordoned off the district, and health inspectors forcibly entered homes to remove the sick and suspected cases. Under controversial sanitary ordinances, the government undertook the sweeping demolition of entire tenement blocks in Tai Ping Shan (太平山) and disinfected or burned personal belongings to eradicate the pathogen.

## Significance
The **1894** outbreak resulted in the deaths of over **2,000** people and led to the exodus of tens of thousands of residents from the colony. It marked a turning point in Hong Kong's urban governance and public health infrastructure. The crisis prompted the establishment of the Bacteriological Institute (細菌學檢定所) and forced colonial authorities to reevaluate housing standards, drainage systems, and medical care for the Chinese population, laying the groundwork for modern sanitary regulations in the territory.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Officials viewed the outbreak as an urgent public health catastrophe requiring swift, aggressive sanitary interventions and sweeping urban renewal to save the colony from ruin. They justified the intrusive inspections and property destruction as necessary measures to control a deadly contagion.
- **Local Chinese residents**: Many inhabitants deeply resented the aggressive colonial tactics, viewing the forced house-to-house searches, removal of patients to makeshift hospitals, and destruction of property as culturally insensitive, heavy-handed, and racially discriminatory. The disruption fueled widespread panic and distrust of Western medical practices.
- **Later medical and historical scholarship**: Modern historians analyze the epidemic as a critical intersection of imperialism, class inequality, and public health, noting that while the measures halted the immediate crisis, they reinforced social divides between the European elite and the local Chinese population.
