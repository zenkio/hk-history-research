## Background

By the early 1890s, the decaying Qing dynasty faced mounting internal rebellions and foreign encroachment, inspiring various reformist and revolutionary movements. **Sun Yat-sen** (**孫中山**), a Western-trained physician who had previously attempted to petition Qing statesman Li Hongzhang for modernization reforms, concluded that the dynasty was beyond salvage and needed to be overthrown. Following the founding of the initial Revive China Society (Xingzhonghui, **興中會**) in Honolulu in November 1894, **Sun Yat-sen** turned his focus toward southern China, recognizing that British-administered Hong Kong (**香港**) offered a secure sanctuary, proximity to the mainland, and relative freedom of movement under British colonial law.

## What happened

On **February 21, 1895**, the Hong Kong branch of the Xingzhonghui was formally established at **Stanton House** (**士丹頓街** or related properties) on **Hollywood Road** (**荷李活道**), operating under the guise of a commercial front named the Kuen Hang Club (乾亨行). Wealthy local supporters and financiers, such as **Yeung Ku-wan** (**楊衢雲**) and **Tse Tsan-tai** (**謝纘泰**), played instrumental roles in organizing the local chapter, which soon merged with Yeung's earlier resistance group, the Furen Literary Society. This newly consolidated organization began actively recruiting members, collecting funds, and secretly plotting an armed uprising against the Qing government in Guangzhou (**廣州**), which would be attempted later that year in October 1895.

## Significance

The establishment of the Hong Kong Xingzhonghui marked a critical turning point by shifting the epicenter of modern Chinese revolutionary planning from overseas diaspora communities directly to the doorstep of the Chinese mainland. By utilizing Hong Kong as a logistical hub, publishers, secret society members, and revolutionaries laid the groundwork for decades of plotting that would eventually culminate in the **1911 Revolution** (**辛亥革命**). The event also highlighted the complex dual nature of British colonial Hong Kong, which simultaneously served as a safe haven for subversives and a territory whose authorities ultimately sought to maintain stability by curtailing overly disruptive anti-Qing activities when diplomatic pressures arose.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial authorities**: Colonial officials viewed the revolutionary activities with caution and pragmatic suspicion, prioritizing public order and diplomatic relations with the Qing empire while generally allowing political exiles to reside within the colony provided they did not violate local laws.
- **Qing government**: Beijing regarded Sun Yat-sen and his followers as dangerous traitors, bandits, and subversives, frequently pressuring British authorities in Hong Kong to suppress revolutionary organizations and extradite or expel key figures.
- **Later historiography**: Modern historians debate the precise date and internal organizational dynamics of the Xingzhonghui's founding, particularly concerning the contentious power-sharing arrangement between Sun Yat-sen and Yeung Ku-wan during the early leadership phase.
