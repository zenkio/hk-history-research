## Background

By the early 20th century, the British colony of Hong Kong had expanded beyond its original island territory to include Kowloon (九龍) and the New Territories (新界). The growing local Chinese elite, along with the Anglican Church, recognized a need for an educational institution that could bridge Western academic structures with traditional Chinese learning. While earlier colonial schools focused predominantly on English instruction to serve the administrative and commercial needs of the British Empire, changing social dynamics prompted a push for a curriculum that maintained cultural roots while offering modern secondary education.

## What happened

In **1903**, **St. Stephen's College** (聖士提反書院) was established under the leadership of Anglican figures and local supporters, with **Bishop Joseph Hoare** (霍約瑟) playing a central role in its inception. The school was designed to offer a dual-medium foundation, instructing students in both English and Chinese classics. Initially operating in urban premises, the institution later relocated to Stanley (赤柱) to secure a larger campus that could accommodate its growing boarding and academic facilities. The early cohort largely comprised sons of wealthy Chinese merchants and prominent families who sought a prestigious Anglo-Chinese education.

## Significance

The founding of St. Stephen's College marked a significant milestone in the evolution of Hong Kong's educational system. It represented a deliberate shift from purely Anglicized schooling toward a bilingual model that validated Chinese language and culture within a British colonial framework. The institution quickly became a cornerstone for educating the colony's future leaders, professionals, and civil servants, mirroring the broader social ambitions of Hong Kong's rising Chinese middle and upper classes during the twilight of the Qing dynasty (清朝).

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Colonial authorities viewed the establishment of bilingual institutions as a pragmatic way to stabilize relations with the local elite, fostering a loyal class of intermediaries who could navigate both British governance and Chinese society.
- **Local Chinese Elite**: Prominent Chinese merchants and community leaders championed the school as a vital mechanism to preserve traditional Confucian values while equipping the younger generation with modern Western knowledge necessary for advancement.
