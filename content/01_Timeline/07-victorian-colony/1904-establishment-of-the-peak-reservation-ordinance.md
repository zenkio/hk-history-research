## Background

By the late 19th century, Victoria Peak (太平山) had emerged as the premier residential enclave for the colonial elite in Hong Kong. Seeking to escape the sweltering heat, humidity, and disease outbreaks of the dense urban areas along the Victoria Harbour (維多利亞港) coastline, Europeans gravitated toward the higher elevations where cooler temperatures prevailed. Prior to formal legislative intervention, informal social pressures, property covenants, and high land values already heavily segregated the area. However, growing numbers of affluent Chinese, Eurasian, and non-European merchants and professionals began acquiring property on the Peak, challenging the de facto European exclusivity of the enclave.

## What happened

In **1904**, during the administration of Governor **Sir Matthew Nathan** (彌敦), the Legislative Council enacted the Peak Reservation Ordinance. This piece of legislation explicitly barred non-Europeans—including wealthy Chinese elites who had previously integrated into upper-crust colonial circles—from residing in the Peak district without the explicit consent of the Governor. The colonial administration framed the policy primarily as a sanitary and public health measure, arguing that European residents required the cooler climate for health preservation due to their biological susceptibility to tropical diseases. The law effectively institutionalized residential segregation, mapping racial hierarchies directly onto the geography of the colony.

## Significance

This ordinance marked a profound turning point in the social and spatial organization of colonial Hong Kong. It formalized a dual-city structure, separating the European ruling class on the hills from the Asian population in the congested urban core and surrounding districts. While later scholarship notes that the ordinance was sometimes bypassed through clever legal maneuvers or exemptions granted to favored individuals, its symbolic and practical impact endured for decades. It set a precedent for racially discriminatory urban planning that persisted until the mid-20th century, profoundly shaping the collective memory of colonial governance and racial inequality in the territory.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Viewed the reservation as a necessary sanitary and health measure to protect European health from tropical climates, ensuring the administrative efficiency and physical well-being of the colonial governing class.
- **Local Chinese Elites and Community**: Condemned the ordinance as a humiliating and discriminatory policy that disregarded the social standing and economic contributions of wealthy non-European residents, effectively treating them as second-class subjects.
- **Modern Historiography**: Analyzes the ordinance as a prime example of institutionalized spatial segregation and racial capitalism, demonstrating how colonial authorities used public health pretexts to maintain social distance and political dominance.
