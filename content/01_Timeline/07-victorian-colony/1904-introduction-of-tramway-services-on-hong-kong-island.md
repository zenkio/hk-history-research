## Background
By the late 19th century, the rapid commercial expansion of the Victorian colony along the northern shore of Hong Kong Island created an urgent demand for a reliable, high-capacity public transport system. Existing modes, such as sedan chairs, rickshaws, and horse-drawn carriages, proved increasingly inadequate for moving a growing urban population between **Kennedy Town** (**堅尼地城**) and **Shau Kei Wan** (**筲箕灣**). Following legislative authorization in 1902, construction commenced on an electric overhead wire tramway system.

## What happened
On **July 20, 1904**, the electric tramway officially commenced operations, introducing a fleet of single-deck cars that would soon become iconic fixtures of the cityscape. Managed by **Electric Traction Company of Hong Kong Limited** (subsequently renamed **Hong Kong Tramways**), the service offered an affordable and efficient means of transit traversing **Victoria City** (**維多利亞城**). Double-deckers were introduced a few years later to cope with heavier passenger volumes, cementing the tram network as the backbone of urban mobility.

## Significance
The tramway drastically reshaped the spatial dynamics of Hong Kong Island by connecting disparate commercial nodes and residential districts. It facilitated labor mobility and supported the ongoing spatial expansion of the colonial urban core. While transportation policies primarily served colonial commercial interests, the system simultaneously became an accessible equalizer for the local working population, embedding itself permanently in the daily rhythms and visual identity of the city.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Colonial authorities viewed the electric tramway as a triumph of British engineering and modern urban management that successfully modernized the colony's infrastructure and commercial efficiency.
- **Local Hong Kong community**: For local residents and merchants, the affordable tram service provided a practical, daily utility that greatly eased movement across the congested northern shore, eventually becoming an affectionate local symbol known as the 'ding ding'.
