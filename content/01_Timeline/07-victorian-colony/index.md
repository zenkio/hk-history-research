## Overview

Between **1860** and **1898**, the British colony of Hong Kong underwent a profound transformation from a fledgling trading post into a bustling Victorian port city. The territorial footprint expanded significantly following the Convention of Peking in **1860**, which ceded the Kowloon Peninsula (九龍半島) and Stonecutters Island (昂船洲) to Great Britain. This expansion was further extended in **1898** with the Second Convention of Peking, which leased the New Territories (新界) for 99 years. Throughout these decades, Hong Kong's physical, economic, and social landscapes were fundamentally reshaped by massive infrastructure projects, public health crises, and shifting racial and governance policies.

## Key themes

Economic development surged during this period, anchored by financial institutions such as the establishment of HSBC (**1865**) and physical transformations like the Launch of the Praya Reclamation Scheme. Infrastructure advanced to meet the demands of a growing population, highlighted by the Completion of the Pok Fu Lam Reservoir (**1877**), the opening of the First Hong Kong City Hall (**1869**), and the inauguration of the Peak Tram (**1888**). Modern scientific and educational foundations were also laid, notably with the founding of the Government Central School (中央書院) in **1862**, the establishment of the Hong Kong Observatory (香港天文台) in **1883**, and the opening of the Hong Kong College of Medicine for Chinese in **1887**.

Socially and politically, the era witnessed both severe strife and institutional philanthropy. Labor tensions erupted during events such as the Hong Kong Coolie Strike, while catastrophic natural disasters tested the colony's resilience, most notably The Great Typhoon of **1874** and the devastating Bubonic Plague Outbreak in Tai Ping Shan (太平山) in **1894**, during which Alexandre Yersin discovered the plague bacillus. In response to local community needs and colonial pressures, charitable and administrative bodies emerged, including the establishment of the Tung Wah Hospital (東華醫院) in **1872** and the founding of the Po Leung Kuk (保良局) in **1878**. Representation in governance slowly evolved, marked by **Ng Choy** (伍才) being appointed as the first Chinese Legislative Councillor. Concurrently, Governor **John Pope Hennessy** introduced reforms to racial policies that favored a more inclusive approach toward the local Chinese population. By the late nineteenth century, Hong Kong also became a crucible for broader Chinese revolutionary movements, exemplified by **Sun Yat-sen** (孫中山) founding the Hong Kong Xingzhonghui (興中會).

## How it shaped Hong Kong

The developments of the late Victorian era established the spatial geography, institutional architecture, and multicultural dynamics that defined modern Hong Kong. The acquisition of Kowloon and the New Territories created the geopolitical boundaries that persisted for over a century. Furthermore, the interplay between British colonial administration, emerging Chinese elite philanthropy, and cross-border revolutionary activities forged a distinct local identity caught between Western imperialism and Chinese nationalism.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Viewed the era as a triumph of Victorian governance, engineering, and free-market capitalism that transformed a barren rock into a modern, sanitary, and prosperous entrepôt of the British Empire.
- **Qing/Beijing perspective**: Regarded the territorial cessions of Kowloon and the lease of the New Territories as products of unequal treaties forced upon a weakened China during a century of national humiliation.
- **Local Hong Kong and New Territories communities**: Experienced the period through a complex lens of adaptation, where local Chinese elites navigated colonial power structures through philanthropy and hospital administration, while indigenous New Territories villagers initially resisted British assumption of control in 1898.
