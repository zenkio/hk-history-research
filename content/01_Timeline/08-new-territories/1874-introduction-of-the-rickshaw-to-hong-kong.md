## Background
Prior to the introduction of the rickshaw, personal transport in Hong Kong relied heavily on sedan chairs carried by coolies, especially for navigating the steep hills of Victoria City (維多利亞城). While sedan chairs remained essential for higher elevations, the expanding flat coastal areas and growing population necessitated a faster, more versatile form of wheeled transport. 

## What happened
Around **1874**, the rickshaw—originally invented in Japan—was introduced to Hong Kong. Records suggest that foreign merchants or returning travelers recognized its potential utility for the colonial port city's congested streets. The vehicle quickly gained traction, offering a cheaper alternative to the sedan chair for middle-class residents and laborers alike. By the late 19th century, thousands of rickshaws operated under a regulated licensing system overseen by colonial authorities, creating a distinct class of rickshaw pullers.

## Significance
The rickshaw revolutionized daily urban mobility in Hong Kong, serving as the backbone of public transport for decades before the introduction of electric tramways (電車) in 1904. It shaped the urban landscape of Victoria City, influenced municipal road planning, and became a prominent symbol of colonial-era street life, while also highlighting the grueling physical labor undertaken by the city's working class.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Colonial authorities viewed the rickshaw as a progressive modernization of urban transport that improved circulation, but they also recognized the need for strict licensing and sanitary regulations to manage the growing industry.
- **Local Hong Kong working-class communities**: For many laborers, working as a rickshaw puller represented a grueling and precarious form of survival amidst high living costs, though it provided a vital entry-level livelihood for newly arrived migrants from Guangdong.
