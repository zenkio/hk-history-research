## Background
In the late **19th century**, the British colonial administration in Hong Kong sought to expand Western medical services and train local practitioners who could bridge the language and cultural gaps with the broader Chinese population. Prior to this, medical training was largely informal or apprenticeship-based. Recognizing this need, the **London Missionary Society** (倫敦傳道會), led by figures such as **Dr. James Cantlie** (康德黎) and **Dr. Ho Kai** (何啟), initiated plans to establish a dedicated institution for higher medical education.

## What happened
On **October 1, 1887** (though classes and formal organization solidified around this period), the **Hong Kong College of Medicine for Chinese** (香港華人西醫書院) officially opened its doors. The curriculum was designed to meet rigorous British professional standards, spanning several years of rigorous study in anatomy, physiology, surgery, and medicine. Instruction was primarily conducted in English. Among its earliest and most prominent students was **Sun Yat-sen** (孫中山), who enrolled at the college and used his time in Hong Kong not only to master Western medicine but also to engage in political discussions that would shape his revolutionary trajectory.

## Significance
The college represents a pivotal milestone in the history of Hong Kong education and healthcare, marking the beginning of institutionalized tertiary education that eventually evolved into the **Li Ka Shing Faculty of Medicine of the University of Hong Kong** (香港大學李嘉誠醫學院). Beyond medicine, the institution is frequently cited by historians as a critical incubator for early anti-Qing revolutionary thought, providing a safe British colonial space where intellectuals could freely debate reform and revolution.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Viewed the college as a successful civilizing mission and a practical solution to public health staffing shortages, demonstrating the benevolent utility of colonial rule through the introduction of advanced Western science and education.
- **Qing/Beijing Perspective**: Historically regarded institutions like the college with suspicion, viewing them as potential breeding grounds for subversion and foreign cultural influence, particularly given their association with radical anti-dynastic figures like Sun Yat-sen.
- **Local Hong Kong Scholarship**: Emphasizes the agency of local visionaries like Ho Kai in partnering with missionaries to secure modern education for Chinese residents, laying the foundational roots for Hong Kong's emergence as an intellectual hub.
