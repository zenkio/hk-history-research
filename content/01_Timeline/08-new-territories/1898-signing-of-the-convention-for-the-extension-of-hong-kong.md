## Background
By the late 19th century, British authorities in Hong Kong argued that the existing colonial boundaries—restricted to Hong Kong Island and the Kowloon Peninsula—were strategically insufficient for the defense of the deep-water harbor. Following the First Sino-Japanese War, foreign powers scrambled for concessions in China, creating an opportune diplomatic environment for London to press the weakened Qing government (大清) for further territorial expansion.

## What happened
On **June 9, 1898**, the **Convention for the Extension of Hong Kong Territory** (香港展拓專條) was signed in Peking. The treaty granted Britain a **99-year lease** over the area north of Kowloon up to the Sham Chun River (深圳河), along with 235 outlying islands and the surrounding waters of Mirs Bay and Deep Bay. This increased the total area of the colony by approximately ten times, incorporating a predominantly rural population and diverse local settlements into the British Empire.

## Significance
The acquisition fundamentally transformed Hong Kong's geography, economy, and administration. While the urban center remained under Crown Colony governance, the **New Territories** (新界) were administered under distinct land laws, customary laws, and policing structures, establishing a dual legal framework that lasted for generations. The 99-year duration of the lease ultimately served as the chronological anchor for the Sino-British Joint Declaration and the eventual handover of all of Hong Kong in 1997.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial**: Viewed the expansion as a vital and pragmatic security measure necessary to protect the economic viability and military defense of the strategic treaty port.
- **Qing Dynasty Official**: Regarded the lease as part of the painful 'scramble for concessions' era, a temporary and humiliating diplomatic accommodation made while the empire faced severe foreign encroachment.
- **Local New Territories Communities**: Initially viewed the British takeover with deep suspicion and armed resistance during the Six-Day War of 1899, before eventually negotiating the protection of traditional land rights and lineage customs.
