## Background

Following the **Convention for the Extension of Hong Kong Territory** signed in **1898**, Britain leased the New Territories and outer islands for 99 years. However, a specific clause stipulated that Chinese military forces stationed within the Kowloon Walled City (九龍寨城) would continue to jurisdictionally occupy the area, provided they did not interfere with British defense operations. Tensions rapidly mounted between the colonial administration and the Qing court as British authorities grew increasingly uneasy about having a sovereign Chinese military and administrative outpost situated directly adjacent to their expanding colony.

## What happened

By the spring of **1899**, following minor armed resistance during the British takeover of the wider New Territories, colonial officials concluded that the continued presence of Qing magistrates within the Walled City posed a security threat. On **December 27, 1899**, **Sir Henry Arthur Blake** (卜力), the Governor of Hong Kong, ordered British troops to march into the Walled City. The British forces encountered virtually no armed resistance, as the remaining Qing officials and soldiers were forced out of the enclave. The administrative yamen and other official buildings were subsequently repurposed or left vacant, effectively ending direct Qing jurisdiction over the walled settlement.

## Significance

The annexation marked a decisive assertion of unilateral British authority in violation of the spirit, if not the precise legal wording, of the 1898 Convention. More profoundly, the expulsion of Chinese officials created a unique legal vacuum. Because the British colonial government chose not to fully integrate the Walled City into its day-to-day municipal administration while Qing sovereignty remained a contested point, the area gradually evolved into an anomalous, densely populated enclave largely left to govern itself, setting the stage for its twentieth-century identity as a lawless 'ungoverned space'.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Viewed the action as a necessary security measure to consolidate the defense and unified administration of the newly acquired New Territories against potential subversion or unrest.
- **Qing Court / Beijing**: Protested the action as a direct violation of the 1898 Convention, which had explicitly reserved Chinese administrative rights within the Walled City.
- **Later Historical Scholarship**: Examines the takeover as a pivotal moment that accidentally created a jurisdictional 'no-man's-land', which shaped the social and architectural evolution of the enclave for the remainder of the colonial era.
