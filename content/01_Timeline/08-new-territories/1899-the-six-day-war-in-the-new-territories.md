## Background

Following the **Convention for the Extension of Hong Kong Territory** signed on **June 9, 1898**, the Qing Empire leased the New Territories to Great Britain for 99 years. As British colonial officials prepared to formally take possession and hoist the Union Jack in **April 1899**, local villagers organized defensive measures. Tensions stemmed from fears regarding land tenure changes, taxation, and interference with traditional ancestral rights and feng shui. The local population, largely consisting of Cantonese and Hakka clans organized under the **Kat Hing Wai (吉慶圍)** and the **Kam Tin (錦田)** alliance, viewed the incoming administration as hostile foreign encroachers.

## What happened

Hostilities broke out ahead of the formal takeover. On **April 14, 1899**, villagers attacked the newly established British police station at **Tai Po (大埔)** and engaged in skirmishes, prompting the colonial government to deploy military force. British troops, equipped with modern artillery and firearms, engaged the poorly armed militia in a series of engagements across the New Territories. The decisive clash occurred at **Lam Tsuen (林村)** and **Shek Kong (石崗)**, where traditional walled villages were breached and subdued by **April 19, 1899**. Despite fierce resistance using matchlocks and traditional weaponry, the local clans were thoroughly defeated, suffering significant casualties.

## Significance

The conflict marked the final major armed resistance against British expansion in Hong Kong. Following the defeat, British authorities consolidated administrative control over the New Territories while adopting a cautious policy of appeasement toward indigenous elites. Land customs, inheritance laws, and local governance structures were largely left intact to prevent future uprisings, laying the foundation for the distinct administrative and legal status of the New Territories that persisted for decades.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial**: Colonial authorities framed the resistance as a law-and-order problem driven by misunderstandings and instigated by recalcitrant local elites, justifying military intervention to establish lawful administration under the lease.
- **Local New Territories communities**: Indigenous clans viewed their actions as a legitimate defense of their homes, ancestral lands, and sovereignty against foreign imperial aggression, honoring the fallen as martyrs who defended local autonomy.
- **Qing/Beijing**: The weakening Qing court maintained a distant stance, having legally ceded administrative jurisdiction under the lease, and offered little practical support to the resisting villagers while seeking to avoid direct conflict with Britain.
- **Later scholarship**: Modern historians analyze the war as a clash of modern state-building against traditional agrarian society, emphasizing how the aftermath shaped colonial governance and land tenure policies in rural Hong Kong.
