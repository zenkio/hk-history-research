## Background

Following the signing of the **Convention for the Extension of Hong Kong Territory** in **1898**, the British administration assumed control over the New Territories. Unlike Hong Kong Island and the Kowloon Peninsula, which were ceded outright, the New Territories were leased for **99 years**. This region comprised complex systems of indigenous land tenure, ancestral trusts, and private holdings governed by centuries of customary Chinese law.

## What happened

To establish fiscal and administrative control, the colonial government enacted the **Land Court Ordinance** in **1900**, establishing the **New Territories Land Court** (新界土地法庭). The court was tasked with investigating indigenous land claims, conducting rigorous land surveys, and issuing formal **Crown leases** (官契) to replace traditional deeds. This process required villagers to present historical title deeds and markers to British surveyors and legal officials.

## Significance

The creation of the Land Court marked the formal imposition of British property law over the rural landscape of the New Territories. While it provided the colonial state with a standardized land registry and taxation base, it fundamentally transformed communal and ancestral land arrangements into individualized or trustee-held leaseholds under the British Crown.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Viewed the Land Court as a necessary and progressive reform to establish legal clarity, secure property rights, and implement a fair land revenue system across the newly acquired territories.
- **New Territories Indigenous Communities**: Often viewed the surveying and registration processes with suspicion and anxiety, fearing that colonial legal interventions would undermine traditional customary rights, increase taxation, and disrupt ancestral land holdings.
- **Qing and Later Scholarship**: Historians analyze the Land Court as a classic mechanism of colonial legal transition, demonstrating how imperial powers co-opted or reconfigured local customary institutions to serve administrative and economic integration.
