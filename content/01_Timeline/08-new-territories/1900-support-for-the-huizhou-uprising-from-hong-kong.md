## Background
By the late 19th and early 20th centuries, the British colony of Hong Kong (香港) had emerged as a safe haven and operational hub for Chinese anti-Qing revolutionaries. The newly leased New Territories (新界) and urban areas provided relative freedom of movement, communication, and press that were unavailable in mainland China under Qing rule. **Sun Yat-sen** (孫中山) and his core network established the Revive China Society (興中會) and subsequently coordinated efforts with local secret societies and reformists to plan armed uprisings in China's southern provinces.

## What happened
On **October 6, 1900** (**光緒二十六年閏八月十三日**), following orders from **Sun Yat-sen**, revolutionary forces launched the Huizhou Uprising (惠州起義) in Guangdong province. Throughout the planning and execution phases, Hong Kong served as the primary base for procuring weapons, gathering intelligence, financial fundraising, and coordinating personnel movements. **Zheng Shiliang** (鄭士良) led the insurgent forces in the field, while key figures operated from within the colony. However, the uprising faced severe logistical disruptions, including the failure of expected Japanese support and weapons shipments, ultimately leading to its suppression by Qing imperial forces later that year.

## Significance
The event underscored Hong Kong's dual and contradictory role in the late Qing period: it functioned simultaneously as a secure rear-guard for revolutionaries seeking to overthrow the imperial government and as a colonial territory monitored by British authorities who sought to maintain neutrality and stability. While the uprising failed militarily, it demonstrated the viability of using Hong Kong's geographical proximity and economic infrastructure for cross-border political agitation, setting a precedent for subsequent revolts that would culminate in the 1911 Xinhai Revolution.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial authorities**: Colonial officials viewed the revolutionaries with growing anxiety, fearing that anti-Qing activities would destabilize public order in Hong Kong and provoke diplomatic retaliation or intervention from the Qing government. Consequently, authorities frequently cracked down on seditious activities and occasionally deported prominent agitators.
- **Qing government**: Beijing officials strongly condemned British authorities for harboring what they considered dangerous rebels and bandits, demanding tighter border controls and the extradition of political dissidents operating out of the colony.
- **Later scholarship and revolutionaries**: Historians and the revolutionaries themselves viewed Hong Kong as an indispensable crucible for the Chinese revolution, arguing that the colony's unique legal and geographic conditions made modern republican organizing possible.
