## Background

During the late 19th and early 20th centuries, Hong Kong under British colonial rule became a vital sanctuary, operational base, and intellectual hub for Chinese revolutionaries seeking to overthrow the Qing dynasty (大清). **Yeung Ku-wan** (楊衢雲), an educator and revolutionary, co-founded the Furen Literary Society (輔仁文社) and later served as the first president of the Hong Kong chapter of the Revive China Society (興中會), alongside **Sun Yat-sen** (孫中山). Following the failed 1895 Guangzhou uprising, Yeung lived in exile in Japan, South Africa, and Southeast Asia before returning secretly to teach English in Hong Kong under an assumed profile. Despite the British colony's status as a safe haven, Qing imperial authorities maintained active intelligence networks and sleeper agents within the territory to monitor, suppress, and eliminate high-profile dissidents.

## What happened

On **January 10, 1901**, a Qing government assassin named **Chen Lin** (陳林) tracked Yeung to his residence and school at No. 52 Gage Street (結志街) in Central, Hong Kong. Chen forced his way into the premises and shot Yeung multiple times in front of his family. Yeung sustained severe wounds and succumbed to them the following day, **January 11, 1901**, at the Alice Memorial Hospital (雅麗氏紀念醫院). The assassin attempted to flee across the border to Chinese territory but was swiftly apprehended by the Hong Kong colonial police force. Chen was subsequently tried, convicted of murder, and executed by the colonial authorities, though historical records remain debated regarding whether higher-level Qing officials directly orchestrated the contract.

## Significance

The murder of **Yeung Ku-wan** sent shockwaves through the local revolutionary networks and the broader Hong Kong community. It starkly illustrated that the British colonial administration could not always guarantee the absolute safety of political exiles residing within its borders against the long arm of the Qing state. For the revolutionary movement, the assassination marked a tragic loss of a key founding intellect, prompting tighter operational security. In historiography, the event is frequently cited as a crucial watershed demonstrating how deeply the violent political power struggles of mainland China spilled over into the British territory of Hong Kong at the turn of the twentieth century.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Viewed the assassination as a serious criminal breach of British jurisdiction and public order, responding by swiftly capturing, trying, and executing the assassin to assert sovereignty and deter foreign political violence within the colony.
- **Qing Imperial Government**: Considered anti-Qing revolutionaries like Yeung Ku-wan to be dangerous rebels and criminals guilty of treason, viewing targeted actions against them as legitimate law enforcement measures to suppress sedition against the empire.
- **Revolutionary Movement**: Regarded Yeung as a martyred pioneer of the republican cause whose murder exposed the ongoing threats posed by imperial tyranny, serving to galvanize further anti-Qing sentiment and resolve among overseas and local Chinese networks.
