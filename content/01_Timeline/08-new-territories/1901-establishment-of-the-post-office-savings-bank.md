## Background
At the turn of the 20th century, Hong Kong experienced rapid economic expansion alongside an influx of working-class laborers. Traditional financial institutions largely catered to corporate trade, wealthy merchants, and expatriates, leaving ordinary workers with few safe options to store modest savings. Informal credit networks, pawnshops, and tontine societies carried significant risks, prompting colonial authorities to consider state intervention to foster financial stability and social order.

## What happened
In **1901**, the colonial administration formally established the **Post Office Savings Bank** (郵政局儲蓄銀行). By leveraging the existing infrastructure of the postal network, the bank allowed residents to deposit and withdraw small sums of money at designated post office branches. The government backed these accounts with its full faith and credit, aiming to attract wage earners, domestic staff, and small traders who previously distrusted or lacked access to Western-style banking institutions.

## Significance
The creation of the savings bank marked an important step in extending formal financial services to the lower strata of Hong Kong society. It reflected a broader colonial administrative shift toward moral governance and social welfare through institutional design. While the long-term impact on working-class wealth accumulation remains a subject of historical debate, the initiative successfully integrated the postal system into the everyday economic lives of ordinary residents.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Viewed the bank as a benevolent institutional reform that promoted thrift, prevented destitution, and reinforced public trust in British financial governance.
- **Local working-class communities**: Approached the government-run bank with a mixture of cautious interest and skepticism, often continuing to rely on traditional, community-based financial networks for better liquidity and cultural familiarity.
