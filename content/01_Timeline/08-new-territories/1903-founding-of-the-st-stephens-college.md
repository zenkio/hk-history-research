## Background
At the turn of the 20th century, following the 1898 Convention for the Extension of Hong Kong Territory, the colonial administration and local religious bodies recognized a growing demand for higher-level education suited for the local Chinese population. While existing mission schools primarily catered to lower-income families or provided basic instruction, there was a distinct lack of institutions offering advanced secondary education to the sons of the emerging Chinese elite. In **1903**, the Anglican Church, spearheaded by prominent figures such as Bishop **Joseph Hoare** (何樂), moved to establish a new boarding school that would address this gap.

## What happened
St. Stephen's College was formally founded in **1903** in Western District on Hong Kong Island before later relocating to Stanley (赤柱). The institution was intentionally designed to blend traditional Chinese classical studies with a rigorous Western curriculum. By offering instruction in both English and Chinese, the school aimed to cultivate bilingual leaders who could navigate both colonial administrative structures and traditional Chinese society. Early supporters and founders included influential Eurasian and Chinese community leaders who saw value in producing graduates capable of serving commerce, government, and the professions.

## Significance
The founding of St. Stephen's College marked a pivotal shift in colonial educational policy, moving away from purely Anglicized assimilation toward a dual-heritage model of elite formation. By institutionalizing bilingualism and the parallel study of Chinese classics and Western sciences, the school set a precedent for elite education in British Hong Kong. It contributed significantly to the formation of a professional Chinese middle and upper class that would play a complex role in the political, social, and economic developments of 20th-century Hong Kong.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Colonial officials viewed the establishment of elite bilingual schools as a pragmatic method to foster loyal intermediaries who could bridge the communication gap between the government and the broader Chinese population.
- **Chinese elite and local community**: Local Chinese leaders and merchants welcomed the institution as a way to ensure their sons received a competitive Western education without losing fluency in their native language and cultural heritage.
- **Later scholarship**: Recent historians often analyze the founding through the lens of cultural imperialism and social stratification, noting how elite mission schools reinforced class divides while simultaneously creating spaces for early anti-colonial or reformist sentiment.
