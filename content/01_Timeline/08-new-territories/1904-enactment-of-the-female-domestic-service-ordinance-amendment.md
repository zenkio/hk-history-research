## Background

By the late **19th century** and early **20th century**, the **Mui Tsai** (妹仔) system—whereby impoverished families transferred young daughters to wealthier households as domestic servants, often in exchange for an advance payment—was widely practiced in **Hong Kong** (香港) and parts of **Guangdong** (廣東). While defenders of the custom viewed it as a form of adoption and social welfare that prevented female infanticide or starvation, critics increasingly condemned the practice as a form of child slavery.

## What happened

In **1904**, the colonial administration passed an amendment concerning female domestic service, aiming to introduce greater oversight into the registration and treatment of these young girls. While stopping well short of outright abolition—a measure the government argued would disrupt local social structures and cause severe economic hardship to the families involved—the **1904** legislation sought to curb the worst abuses associated with the system by establishing firmer legal parameters.

## Significance

This legislative step marked the beginning of decades of intense debate over the regulation, and ultimate abolition, of the **Mui Tsai** system in **Hong Kong** (香港). It set a precedent for government intervention in traditional family customs, though the enforcement of these early regulations remained limited and subject to evasion.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Officials viewed the amendment as a pragmatic compromise that introduced necessary oversight without causing destabilizing friction with local elites and traditional customs.
- **Local Chinese elites and community leaders**: Many traditional households defended the Mui Tsai system as a benevolent institution akin to adoption, arguing that legislative interference undermined parental authority and Chinese social norms.
- **Later scholarship and humanitarian reformers**: Modern historians and contemporary reformers generally argue that the 1904 amendment was excessively timid, reflecting colonial reluctance to challenge entrenched patriarchal and economic interests until sustained public pressure forced bolder action decades later.
