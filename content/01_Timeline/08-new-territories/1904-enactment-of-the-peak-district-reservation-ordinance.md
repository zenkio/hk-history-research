## Background
By the late 19th and early 20th centuries, Victoria Peak (太平山) had become the premier elite residential area in Hong Kong. European residents increasingly sought to distance themselves from the dense, rapidly growing urban districts of the lower levels, driven by concerns over public health, tropical disease, and colonial status.

## What happened
In **1904**, the colonial government passed the Peak District Reservation Ordinance. This legislation formally reserved the upper levels of Victoria Peak for non-Chinese residents, effectively barring wealthy Chinese merchants and residents—despite their economic standing—from purchasing or occupying property in the area. The ordinance established a legal framework for spatial and racial segregation that would influence urban development for decades.

## Significance
This ordinance marked a critical turning point in the institutionalization of racial segregation within the British Empire's colonial administration in Hong Kong. It highlighted the intersection of public health rhetoric, class anxieties, and racial hierarchy in shaping colonial urban planning policies.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Proponents within the colonial government argued that the restriction was necessary to protect the health of European residents from tropical diseases associated with high-density urban environments, while also maintaining the prestige of the colonial administration.
- **Local Chinese elite and later scholarship**: Wealthy Chinese merchants and modern historians viewed the ordinance as a discriminatory measure rooted in racial prejudice, which arbitrarily restricted property rights and reinforced social stratification based on race rather than economic capability.
