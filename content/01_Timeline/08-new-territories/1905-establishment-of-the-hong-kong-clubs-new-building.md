## Background
By the early 20th century, the British colonial presence in Hong Kong had expanded significantly following the 1898 lease of the New Territories (新界). The **Hong Kong Club** (香港會), founded in 1846, had long served as the primary social institution for the British mercantile and administrative elite. As the colony's central district became more developed, the club sought to relocate from its earlier premises to a more prestigious site that reflected the growing wealth and permanence of the British presence in the Far East.

## What happened
In **1897**, the club acquired a prime location on the Praya (海傍), overlooking Statue Square (皇后像廣場). Construction of the new clubhouse began in the early 1900s, and the building was officially opened in **1905**. Designed by the architectural firm **Leigh & Orange**, the structure was a prominent example of Victorian-era architecture in the colony, featuring a grand facade and exclusive interiors. The facility functioned as a private sanctuary for its members, providing dining rooms, reading areas, and recreational spaces away from the public gaze of the bustling central business district.

## Significance
The completion of the clubhouse at Statue Square in **1905** served as a physical manifestation of British colonial social hierarchy. It was situated at the heart of the government and commercial administration, facing the statue of Queen Victoria. Scholars often interpret the club’s development during this period as a consolidation of the "taipan" (大班) culture, where commercial interests and colonial governance were deeply intertwined. By providing an exclusive space for networking, the club facilitated informal decision-making among the city's most powerful men. The building remained an iconic landmark for decades, serving as the social hub for those who held the highest rungs of influence within the colonial apparatus.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Establishment**: For the British elite, the club was seen as a civilizing institution and a home-away-from-home that preserved European social standards. It was viewed as an essential venue for maintaining the cohesion of the administrative and merchant classes during a period of rapid territorial expansion.
- **Local Hong Kong and New Territories Communities**: For the majority Chinese population, the club represented the exclusionary nature of colonial rule, where significant decisions were made in private, segregated spaces. Many locals viewed the building as a symbol of the 'closed shop' mentality of the British ruling class, which largely remained inaccessible to non-Europeans for much of the early 20th century.
