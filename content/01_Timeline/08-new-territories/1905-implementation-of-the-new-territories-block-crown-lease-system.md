## Background
Following the **Convention for the Extension of Hong Kong Territory** signed in **1898**, the British government leased the New Territories and New Kowloon for 99 years. The region was characterized by complex traditional Chinese land tenure systems, including ancestral trusts, clan lands, and private holdings documented through various indigenous deeds and tax receipts issued during the Qing dynasty.

## What happened
Between **1900** and **1905**, the colonial administration conducted a massive cadastral survey of the entire territory. This culminated in the **Block Crown Lease** system of **1905**. Under this system, thousands of individual land parcels were mapped, numbered, and recorded in official Block Crown Lease schedules. Traditional landowners were issued Crown leases, transforming customary tenure into statutory leasehold property under British common law while reserving ultimate land ownership to the British Crown.

## Significance
This monumental administrative undertaking established a modern property registration system in the New Territories, creating legal certainty for land transactions and establishing a reliable basis for colonial taxation and infrastructure development. It bridged traditional rural practices with British jurisprudence, though the imposition of leasehold terms fundamentally altered the conceptual nature of indigenous land rights.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Viewed the survey and lease system as an essential administrative necessity to establish the rule of law, prevent land disputes, secure a reliable tax base, and effectively govern the newly acquired territories.
- **Local New Territories communities**: Exhibited initial anxiety and suspicion regarding foreign interference in ancestral lands and taxation, but ultimately engaged with the process to legally secure their traditional holdings and customary rights against encroaching claims.
