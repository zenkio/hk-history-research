## Background
Following the 1898 Convention for the Extension of Hong Kong Territory (展拓香港界址專條), the British colonial administration sought to integrate the rural economy of the New Territories (新界) into the broader commercial framework of the British Empire. Historically, tea cultivation had been practiced on a small scale by local Hakka (客家) communities residing in the foothills of Tai Mo Shan (大帽山). These communities had long engaged in subsistence farming and small-scale trade, utilizing the mountain’s unique microclimate and high altitude to produce high-quality leaves.

## What happened
Around **1906**, colonial authorities and agricultural reformers began exploring the potential of formalizing and scaling up the tea industry in the area. Official encouragement often manifested through agricultural surveys and the promotion of standardized harvesting techniques designed to improve yield and export viability. While the colonial government sought to treat this as an economic development project, local farmers often viewed these initiatives through the lens of traditional land use. It remains unclear to what extent these colonial interventions directly increased total production or whether they merely provided a regulatory framework for existing traditional practices. The logistical challenges of transporting delicate tea leaves from the rugged terrain of Tai Mo Shan to the ports of Victoria City (維多利亞城) limited the scale of the industry, leaving it largely as a secondary agricultural endeavor rather than a primary industrial pillar.

## Significance
The tea industry on Tai Mo Shan represents a pivotal moment in the early colonial administration's struggle to categorize and control rural land in the New Territories. By attempting to modernize tea cultivation, the government signaled its intent to view the rural landscape not just as a buffer zone for the colony, but as a potential site for economic production. This period reflects the broader tensions of the early 20th century, as traditional agrarian practices encountered the pressures of modernization, global market expectations, and the administrative reorganization mandated by the British lease.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: The administration viewed the promotion of tea cultivation as a civilizing and economic mission intended to bring efficiency to the 'underdeveloped' rural hinterlands. They prioritized the creation of export-ready products to demonstrate the successful economic integration of the New Territories.
- **Local Hakka Communities**: For the local Hakka families, tea cultivation was an ancestral livelihood deeply tied to land tenure and community survival. They often treated colonial administrative efforts with skepticism, prioritizing their own traditional production methods and village autonomy over external economic directives.
- **Modern Scholarly Interpretation**: Contemporary historians often interpret these agricultural efforts as an exercise in colonial state-building. They emphasize the gap between the government's lofty economic goals and the reality of rural life, suggesting that the industry's modest results were due to a fundamental misunderstanding of local social structures.
