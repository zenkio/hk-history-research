## Background
During the late 19th century, the expansion of the British colonial presence in Hong Kong necessitated the reclamation and repurposing of harbor infrastructure. Following the 1898 Convention for the Extension of Hong Kong Territory, which expanded British jurisdiction to the New Territories (新界), the administration sought to consolidate its recreational and logistical footprint. Kellett Island (奇力島), situated off the coast of East Point, was historically used by local fishing communities but became increasingly integrated into the colonial maritime landscape as the harbor developed.

## What happened
The **Royal Hong Kong Yacht Club** (皇家香港遊艇會), which traces its informal origins to the 1840s, sought a more permanent and exclusive base as it gained formal recognition. In **1908**, the club relocated its headquarters to **Kellett Island**, constructing a clubhouse that served as a social and operational hub for the colonial elite. This relocation transformed a small island into a private enclave dedicated to maritime leisure. Over subsequent decades, the island was physically connected to the mainland via land reclamation, fundamentally altering the geography of the Causeway Bay (銅鑼灣) waterfront.

## Significance
The establishment of the clubhouse symbolized the growing prominence of maritime leisure among the British expatriate community. For colonial officials and business leaders, the club served as a space for diplomacy and networking. However, this development also reflected the broader colonial appropriation of harbor space, where the needs of recreational maritime activities were prioritized over the traditional maritime access rights of local boat-dwelling communities. The project illustrates the era's tendency to reshape Hong Kong's topography to reflect the socio-economic status of its ruling class, moving away from the purely utilitarian maritime uses of the mid-19th century toward an era of colonial urban planning.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration and Expatriate Elite**: The administration and club members viewed the move as a modernization effort, providing a necessary facility for the health and social cohesion of the colonial community. They emphasized the club's role in promoting maritime skills and British sporting traditions within the colony.
- **Local Maritime and Fishing Communities**: For local Tanka (蜑家) and fishing populations, the appropriation of Kellett Island represented a further loss of access to traditional harbor waters and anchorage points. The development was seen as a manifestation of colonial exclusion, where public marine space was increasingly fenced off for private, elite use.
