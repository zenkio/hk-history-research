## Background

In **February 1908**, the Japanese steamship *Tatsu Maru* (辰丸) was detained by Qing customs authorities off the coast of Guangdong for allegedly smuggling weapons intended for Chinese revolutionaries. The Japanese government protested vehemently, demanding compensation, an apology, and the release of the ship, which placed the weakened Qing government under immense pressure.

## What happened

When the Qing dynasty (大清) ultimately conceded to Japanese demands and released the vessel, outrage swept across mainland China and the British colony of Hong Kong in **March 1908**. Local Chinese merchants, students, and residents organized a comprehensive boycott of Japanese shipping, trade, and consumer goods. Public meetings were held, and anti-Japanese sentiment intensified rapidly within the urban areas of Hong Kong, testing the colonial government's ability to maintain public order while balancing its diplomatic relations with both Japan and the Qing Empire.

## Significance

The **Tatsu Maru boycott** is widely viewed by historians as a watershed moment for political consciousness in Hong Kong. It demonstrated that local Chinese residents were increasingly responsive to continental nationalist movements and capable of coordinated economic resistance. For the British colonial administration, the events highlighted the delicate challenge of policing politically charged transnational movements within a bustling entrepôt.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Authorities**: Colonial officials viewed the boycott primarily as a threat to public order, trade stability, and neutrality, fearing that rising Chinese nationalism would destabilize governance in Hong Kong.
- **Qing Government**: Beijing officials found themselves caught between aggressive Japanese diplomatic demands and intense domestic fury, struggling to assert sovereignty while preventing revolutionary agitation.
- **Local Chinese Merchants and Nationalists**: Participants saw the boycott as a patriotic duty to defend national sovereignty against foreign aggression, using economic non-cooperation as a modern political weapon.
