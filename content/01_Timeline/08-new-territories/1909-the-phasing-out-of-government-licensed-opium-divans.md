## Background
For decades following the establishment of the colony, the processing, distribution, and consumption of opium generated a massive share of government revenue in Hong Kong. The colonial administration farmed out the opium monopoly to wealthy Chinese merchants who paid substantial fees to operate legal divans and retail shops. By the early **20th century**, however, changing moral standards in Britain and international pressure to curb the opium trade began to mount. The British House of Commons passed resolutions declaring the trade morally indefensible, forcing the Colonial Office to compel its overseas possessions, including Hong Kong, to dismantle their state-sanctioned drug infrastructure.

## What happened
On **March 1, 1909**, the Hong Kong government began phasing out the licensed opium divans (煙館), restricting public consumption venues and tightening regulations on retail sales. This move aimed to comply with imperial mandates while attempting to balance the colony's budget. Because opium farming revenues had previously funded a significant portion of public works and policing, the administration faced the immediate challenge of finding alternative sources of income, ultimately leading to the introduction of new direct taxes and duties.

## Significance
The closure of the licensed divans marked a pivotal moment in the economic and social history of Hong Kong. It signaled the end of an era where the colonial state openly profited from widespread narcotics consumption, though it did not immediately eradicate the drug trade. Smuggling and illicit dens quickly filled the vacuum left by the legal market, setting the stage for decades of challenges regarding narcotics control, law enforcement, and public health in the territory.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Faced with direct moral and political pressure from London and the House of Commons, the colonial government presented the phase-out as a necessary compliance with imperial conscience and international anti-opium treaties, despite the severe fiscal shock it inflicted on the colony's budget.
- **Local Chinese Merchants and Revenue Farmers**: Many elite merchants and holders of the opium monopoly viewed the sudden dismantling of the legal trade with alarm, arguing that heavy-handed suppression would merely drive the lucrative market underground, enriching criminal syndicates without actually reducing consumption.
- **Later Scholarship**: Modern historians often analyze the transition as a complex negotiation between imperial morality and colonial financial survival, noting that the vacuum created by legal suppression inadvertently catalyzed the rise of organized crime and illicit heroin distribution networks in subsequent decades.
