## Background
In the years leading up to **1911**, Hong Kong (香港) served as a vital base for anti-Qing revolutionary movements. Figures such as **Sun Yat-sen** (孫中山) utilized the British colony's relative security and legal separation from the Chinese mainland to organize, raise funds, and publish propaganda. While the colonial government maintained an official stance of neutrality, local Chinese populations and various political factions engaged in intense debates over the future of China.

## What happened
On **October 10, 1911**, the Wuchang Uprising (武昌起義) broke out, triggering the Xinhai Revolution (辛亥革命) that ultimately overthrew the Qing dynasty (清朝). As political upheaval spread across southern China, Hong Kong experienced an immediate and massive influx of mainland refugees, wealthy merchants seeking safety for their capital, and political activists. This sudden demographic and economic shift strained local infrastructure while simultaneously injecting new dynamism into the colonial economy. Newspapers in the colony closely followed the fighting, and republican political enthusiasm surged among the local populace.

## Significance
The events of **1911** cemented Hong Kong's role as a crucial nexus between mainland Chinese politics and international networks. Later scholarship highlights how the revolution transformed the colony into a permanent hub for Chinese nationalism and diaspora politics. At the same time, it forced the British colonial administration to navigate delicate diplomatic relations with the changing regimes in Beijing and Guangzhou.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: The colonial government viewed the revolution with cautious pragmatism, primarily concerned with maintaining public order, protecting British economic interests, and preventing Hong Kong from becoming an uncontrollable base for cross-border anti-government guerrilla warfare.
- **Chinese Revolutionaries**: For republicans and anti-Qing activists, Hong Kong was seen as an indispensable sanctuary and operational headquarters where free speech and British legal protections allowed revolutionary planning to thrive away from imperial agents.
