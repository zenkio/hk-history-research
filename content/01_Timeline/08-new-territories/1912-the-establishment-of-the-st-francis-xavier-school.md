## Background
By the early **1910s**, Hong Kong was experiencing significant demographic shifts. Following the **1898** Convention for the Extension of Hong Kong Territory, the colonial government sought to stabilize its administration of the New Territories while managing a rapidly growing Chinese population in urban areas. During this era, religious orders, particularly the **Marist Brothers (聖母昆仲會)**, sought to expand their pedagogical footprint to meet the demand for Western-style education, which was increasingly viewed as a pathway to professional advancement under British rule.

## What happened
In **1912**, the **Marist Brothers** established the **St. Francis Xavier School (聖芳濟書院)**. Initially located in Wanchai, the institution was founded with the primary objective of providing secondary education to boys. The school followed a curriculum influenced by both French educational traditions and the requirements of the British colonial civil service, emphasizing language acquisition and discipline. While its early years were modest in terms of facilities and enrollment compared to established institutions like Queen’s College, the school quickly positioned itself as a critical provider of education for local Chinese and Eurasian families.

## Significance
The founding of the school serves as a case study for the role of private, religious-led education in early 20th-century Hong Kong. Unlike government schools, which were primarily focused on training clerks for the colonial administration, missionary schools were often viewed as intermediaries between local Chinese communities and the Westernized colonial structure. The establishment of the school underscored a period where the Catholic Church significantly bolstered its presence, effectively utilizing education as a tool for both social service and the integration of Christian values into the local social fabric. Whether this educational model served to empower local Chinese youth or primarily reinforced colonial hierarchical structures remains a subject of active historical debate.

## Perspectives

- **Colonial Administration**: The British government generally welcomed missionary schools as they relieved the burden on the state to provide public education. Authorities viewed these schools as useful institutions for cultivating a loyal, English-speaking professional class.
- **Local Chinese Communities**: For many local Chinese families, schools like St. Francis Xavier offered a rare opportunity for social mobility through English fluency. Education was perceived as a pragmatic necessity for success in a British-dominated economy.
- **Religious Orders**: The Marist Brothers viewed their mission as a spiritual duty to bring Catholic education to the East. They saw themselves as educators filling a vital void in the colonial landscape, emphasizing moral and academic development.
