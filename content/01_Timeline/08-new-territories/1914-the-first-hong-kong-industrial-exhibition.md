## Background
By the early **20th century**, Hong Kong’s economic framework was dominated by its role as a free port and a commercial hub for the entrepôt trade, facilitating the movement of goods between China and the West. However, the period following the **1898** lease of the New Territories (新界) saw an expansion of colonial administrative influence and a steady influx of capital and labor. By **1914**, the onset of World War I disrupted traditional shipping routes and global supply chains, creating an urgent need for local alternatives to imported goods. It was within this context that local entrepreneurs began to explore the potential of light industrial manufacturing.

## What happened
In **1914**, the First Hong Kong Industrial Exhibition was organized to showcase products manufactured within the territory. While records regarding the exact venue and scale remain debated, the event brought together various local businesses to display items ranging from textiles and household goods to processed foods. The exhibition served as an informal platform for business owners to demonstrate that Hong Kong was capable of producing goods that were not merely for transit, but for domestic consumption and export. Although modest compared to later industrial fairs, it highlighted the ingenuity of local workshops and the growing interest in industrial self-reliance during a time of international uncertainty.

## Significance
Historians generally interpret the **1914** exhibition as a symbolic departure from the colonial government's traditional 'laissez-faire' reliance on trade brokerage. While Hong Kong would remain a major port for decades to come, the seeds of industrialization planted during this era would eventually mature into the post-WWII manufacturing boom. The exhibition is frequently cited by scholars as a critical juncture where the narrative of Hong Kong shifted from that of a 'middleman' to an active participant in production. The event successfully signaled to the British colonial administration and local merchant classes that industry could provide a stable economic hedge against the volatility of international trade flows.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: The colonial government viewed early industrial efforts as a secondary concern, largely focusing on maintaining the colony's status as a premier hub for shipping and logistics. Officials often emphasized the stability provided by the British legal framework as the true driver of economic success during this period.
- **Local Hong Kong Merchant Class**: Local entrepreneurs viewed the exhibition as a necessary endeavor to achieve greater economic autonomy and reduce dependency on foreign imports. They often argued that developing local manufacturing was essential for the long-term survival and prosperity of the Chinese community within the colonial system.
