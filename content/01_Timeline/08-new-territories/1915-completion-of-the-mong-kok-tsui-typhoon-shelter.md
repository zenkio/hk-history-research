## Background
In **September 1906**, a devastating typhoon struck Hong Kong with little warning, causing immense loss of life and severe damage to shipping, particularly affecting the vulnerable boat-dwelling communities. In the wake of this disaster, the colonial government recognized an urgent need for secure maritime infrastructure to protect vessels and mariners from extreme weather.

## What happened
Engineering works commenced to construct a dedicated typhoon shelter at Mong Kok Tsui (旺角咀) on the Kowloon peninsula. Completed in **1915**, the Mong Kok Tsui Typhoon Shelter (旺角咀避風塘) provided a designated basin enclosed by breakwaters where fishing junks and cargo vessels could anchor safely during severe storms.

## Significance
The shelter represented a major expansion of colonial public works into the Kowloon peninsula to address the safety of the working-class maritime population. For the marginalized Tanka (漁民/蛋家) boat-dwelling community, the facility offered a critical, albeit limited, physical refuge, highlighting the intersection of colonial urban planning and maritime welfare during the early twentieth century.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Viewed the project as a necessary and progressive humanitarian engineering achievement that secured the colony's maritime trade and reduced the economic and human costs of recurring natural disasters.
- **Tanka and Local Boat-Dwelling Communities**: Experienced the shelter as a vital, life-saving sanctuary against increasingly violent typhoons, though access and regulation were still subject to the overarching control of colonial authorities.
