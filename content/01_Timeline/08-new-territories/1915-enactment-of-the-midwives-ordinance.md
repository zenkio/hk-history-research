## Background
By the early **20th century**, Hong Kong’s rapid urbanization and population growth outpaced the development of formal healthcare infrastructure. Infant and maternal mortality rates remained high, particularly within the impoverished districts of the colony where the majority of births were attended by traditional, untrained midwives. The colonial government began to view these traditional practices—often rooted in customs not aligned with contemporary Western medical standards—as a barrier to improving public health outcomes and maintaining the sanitary standards deemed necessary for a growing port city.

## What happened
In **1915**, the colonial government enacted the **Midwives Ordinance**. This legislation established a framework for the registration and supervision of those practicing midwifery within the colony. The law sought to create a distinction between "trained" practitioners, who would receive certification based on Western medical curricula, and those who continued to operate without formal state recognition. While the ordinance did not immediately criminalize all traditional birthing practices, it effectively initiated a state-led effort to standardize maternal care by encouraging registration and oversight under the supervision of medical officers.

## Significance
The ordinance marked a pivotal shift in the colonial state's approach to the private lives of its subjects. By bringing the birthing room under the purview of public health legislation, the government sought to extend its influence into the domestic sphere, particularly within the Chinese community. The long-term significance of this legislation is debated; some scholars argue it laid the foundation for modern maternal safety, while others contend it served as a mechanism of colonial control that marginalized traditional knowledge systems. The implementation of these standards was slow, and it would take decades for Western-style midwifery to achieve widespread integration among the general population, reflecting the persistence of traditional practices in the face of colonial medical policy.

## Perspectives

- **Colonial Administration**: The administration viewed the ordinance as an essential public health intervention necessary to standardize sanitary practices and lower high infant mortality rates. They believed that formal training and state regulation were the only reliable methods to modernize the colony's maternal healthcare infrastructure.
- **Local Traditional Practitioners**: Many traditional midwives and the communities they served perceived the ordinance with skepticism, viewing the government's intervention as an unnecessary intrusion into long-standing cultural and birthing traditions. There was often resistance to adopting foreign training methods that appeared disconnected from the specific social and practical realities of traditional midwifery in Hong Kong.
