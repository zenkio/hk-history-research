## Background

By the early 20th century, the rapid expansion of Hong Kong's maritime trade led to a significant increase in the boat-dweller and seafaring population residing along the shores of the Kowloon peninsula, particularly around Yaumatei (油麻地). Frequent and violent typhoons historically caused immense loss of life and property among these communities, who lived on traditional junks and sampans with little protection against open-sea gales. Following catastrophic storms that struck the colony around the turn of the century, colonial authorities recognized the urgent need for sheltered anchorage areas to safeguard both vessels and the laborers who sustained the local economy.

## What happened

Construction of the **Yaumatei Typhoon Shelter** progressed through the 1910s, culminating in its formal inauguration around **1915**. The project involved extensive marine engineering works, including the construction of breakwaters to enclose a safe basin where watercraft could ride out severe weather. While the exact timeline of completion and initial capacity figures remain subjects requiring verification from primary public works reports, the facility quickly became a vital haven for the Victoria Harbour junk trade and the local boat-people.

## Significance

The shelter represented a shift in colonial administration policy toward addressing the welfare and infrastructure needs of the non-European maritime populace, though motivations were deeply tied to maintaining commercial stability and port efficiency. For the boat-dwellers, the designated basin provided a physical sanctuary during increasingly unpredictable weather patterns. In the broader context of Hong Kong's urban development, the project facilitated the continuous growth of Kowloon's waterfront and integrated the maritime sector more closely with land-based municipal administration.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Authorities viewed the shelter as a triumph of modern engineering and a necessary humanitarian and economic investment to protect the colony's vital shipping and labor force from recurring natural disasters.
- **Local Boat-Dwellers and Tanka Communities**: For the marginalized maritime population, the shelter offered essential survival infrastructure, though interactions with colonial marine police and harbor regulations also increased government oversight of traditional ways of life.
- **Modern Historiography**: Later scholars often analyze the project through the lens of colonial governance, examining how public works were prioritized to secure economic stability while simultaneously reshaping the maritime geography of Victoria Harbour.
