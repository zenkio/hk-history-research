## Background

Following the outbreak of the First World War in **1914**, the Allied powers faced severe labor shortages on the Western Front. To maintain supply lines, dig trenches, and repair infrastructure, Britain and France turned to recruiting overseas laborers. Although China initially remained neutral, British authorities, with the assistance of colonial officials and commercial networks in southern China and Hong Kong, began organizing the transit of workers. Hong Kong (香港), given its strategic coastal location and colonial infrastructure, was designated as a primary logistical base and transit depot for this unprecedented mobilization.

## What happened

In **1917**, thousands of men from northern and southern China passed through Hong Kong on their way to Europe to join the Chinese Labour Corps (華工兵團). These laborers were housed in temporary camps under strict colonial surveillance before boarding steamships bound for Europe via Canada or the Suez Canal. While the British colonial administration managed the complex logistics, security, and medical quarantines, local communities and intermediaries played crucial roles in recruitment and supply. The movement of such large numbers of transient workers through the territory required extensive coordination between colonial authorities, military officials, and local merchants.

## Significance

The mobilization transformed Hong Kong into an active participant in a global conflict far from East Asian shores. It demonstrated the colony's growing importance as a hub for imperial logistics and transnational labor migration. For the laborers themselves, the journey exposed them to global geopolitics, though their experiences and contributions were long overlooked in mainstream Western histories of the war. In recent decades, historians have reexamined the movement to better understand the global dimensions of the First World War and the specific contributions of Chinese labor to the Allied victory.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Viewed the operation primarily through the lens of imperial efficiency, military necessity, and strict security management, ensuring that the transit of thousands of foreign workers did not disrupt local stability.
- **Local Communities and Intermediaries**: Perceived the recruitment drive through economic opportunities and networks of labor brokers, though local populations also bore the social pressures and health risks associated with massive transient camps.
- **Later Scholarship**: Emphasizes the global nature of the First World War, integrating the Chinese Labour Corps into narratives of transnational labor, imperialism, and the foundational role of non-European peoples in modern history.
