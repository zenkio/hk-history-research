## Background
Queen's College (皇仁書院), originally founded as the Central School (中央書院) in 1862, served as the primary institution for Western-style education in Hong Kong. Under the direction of early educators like **Frederick Stewart**, the school aimed to provide a curriculum that blended English language studies with classical Chinese literature. By the early 20th century, the institution had outgrown its original site on Gough Street, which had been demolished and rebuilt multiple times. The relocation to the **Causeway Bay** site was envisioned as part of the broader colonial administration's effort to standardize and elevate the quality of elite instruction for the local Chinese population.

## What happened
In **1918**, Queen's College officially opened its new campus at the junction of Causeway Road and Leighton Road. The building was designed to accommodate a growing student body and represented a significant capital investment by the colonial government. While the First World War (1914–1918) had caused delays and financial strain, the project was finalized to provide modern classrooms, laboratory space, and facilities that aligned with the British educational standards of the time. The school continued to draw students from prominent local Chinese families, cementing its reputation as an elite gateway to the colonial bureaucracy and professional spheres.

## Significance
The establishment of the new campus is often viewed as the fruition of the colonial government's long-term educational strategy. By providing a stable, purpose-built environment, the administration sought to foster a Westernized administrative class capable of mediating between the colonial government and the Chinese community. While some historians argue this promoted social mobility and modernization for Hong Kong's Chinese subjects, others point out that the curriculum remained strictly controlled to serve the needs of the British Empire, often at the expense of fostering a distinct local or national identity.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: The administration viewed the school as a successful instrument of 'civilizing' influence, intended to produce loyal, Western-educated Chinese professionals who would support the efficient governance of the colony.
- **Local Chinese Elite**: Many local families viewed attendance at the school as a prestigious necessity, providing the linguistic and cultural tools required for success in a British-dominated business and professional environment.
- **Later Historical Scholarship**: Recent academic inquiry often critiques the school's historical role, questioning whether the education provided was genuinely empowering or merely a mechanism for preserving colonial hegemony and social stratification.
