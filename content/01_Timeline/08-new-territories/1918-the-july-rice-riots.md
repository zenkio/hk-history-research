## Background
In **July 1918**, Hong Kong was suffering from the compounding economic pressures of World War I, which included severe global grain shortages, rampant inflation, and currency fluctuations. The price of rice—the dietary staple for the territory's inhabitants—surged beyond the purchasing power of the working class. While the colonial economy generated wealth for merchant elites, lower-income laborers faced acute distress and precarious living standards, creating a tinderbox of social discontent.

## What happened
As rice supplies dwindled and prices skyrocketed, frustration boiled over into widespread public unrest. Crowds gathered and began attacking rice shops, granaries, and grocery stores, looting provisions in broad daylight. The disturbances prompted a rapid response from the colonial authorities, who deployed police and military forces to restore order. Public order was eventually re-established through a combination of crowd dispersal, arrests, and emergency government interventions aimed at stabilizing the grain supply.

## Significance
The **July Rice Riots** laid bare the severe wealth disparity in early 20th-century Hong Kong and demonstrated the fragile food security of the colony under wartime conditions. The disturbances forced the colonial government to re-evaluate its laissez-faire approach to staple food distribution, leading to greater administrative intervention in the local economy in subsequent decades to prevent similar crises.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial authorities**: Viewed the riots primarily as a criminal breakdown of public order driven by panic and opportunistic lawlessness, requiring swift police action and emergency market regulation to restore stability.
- **Local working-class communities**: Perceived the looting as an act of sheer survival against exploitative merchants and an indifferent government that failed to guarantee affordable access to basic sustenance during wartime.
