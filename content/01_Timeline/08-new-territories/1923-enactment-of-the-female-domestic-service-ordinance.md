## Background

The practice of **mui tsai** (妹仔), referring to young Chinese girls sold into households as domestic servants, was widespread in southern China and Hong Kong during the late **Qing dynasty** (清朝) and early colonial period. While often framed by traditional practitioners as an adoption-like arrangement that provided shelter and care for impoverished daughters, critics viewed it as a form of child slavery. By the early **1920s**, intense pressure from humanitarian organizations in Britain and local activists brought the issue to the forefront of colonial governance.

## What happened

In **1923**, following prolonged public debate and inquiries, the colonial administration passed the **Female Domestic Service Ordinance** (女子傭工條例). The legislation aimed to regulate the **mui tsai** system rather than abolish it outright. It prohibited the acquisition of new **mui tsai** and mandated the registration of existing ones to prevent abuse and illegal trafficking. However, enforcement proved challenging, and many households resisted government oversight into private domestic matters.

## Significance

The ordinance represented a pivotal moment in the evolution of social policy and colonial governance in Hong Kong. It highlighted the complex tensions between British humanitarian standards, international pressure, and local Chinese social customs. Modern scholars continue to debate whether the legislation genuinely improved the welfare of these girls or merely drove the practice underground under a veneer of legal compliance.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Viewed the legislation as a progressive step toward eliminating abusive practices while gradually managing local traditions without inciting widespread social unrest.
- **Local Chinese Elites and Traditionalists**: Argued that the arrangement provided a vital social safety net for destitute families and feared that aggressive colonial intervention infringed upon traditional family structures.
- **Humanitarian Activists**: Criticized the **1923** ordinance as overly lenient, arguing that mere registration was insufficient and demanding the total and immediate abolition of child servitude.
