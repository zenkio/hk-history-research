## Overview

The period from **1898** to **1918** fundamentally transformed the geography, economy, and political significance of Hong Kong. Following the signing of the **Convention for the Extension of Hong Kong** (*Beijing Tiao-yue*), Britain secured a 99-year lease over the New Territories and **New Kowloon** in **1898**, drastically expanding the colony's landmass. This territorial enlargement brought hundreds of thousands of rural villagers under British administration, sparking immediate clashes such as the **Six-Day War** of **1899** (*Liu-ri Zhan*). While the colonial government sought to consolidate control—including the **British Annexation of the Kowloon Walled City** (*Jiu-long Cheng*)—the territory simultaneously became a crucial base for mainland revolutionaries, notably supporting the **Huizhou Uprising** (*Hui-zhou Qi-yi*) and witnessing the assassination of revolutionary leader **Yeung Ku-wan** (*Yang Qu-yun*) in **1901**.

The early 20th century also witnessed sweeping modernization and infrastructural milestones. The **Founding of the South China Morning Post** in **1903**, the **Inauguration of Hong Kong Tramways** (*Xiang-gang Dian-che*) in **1904**, and the opening of the **Kowloon-Canton Railway British Section** (*Jiu-Guang Tie-lu Ying-ji Duan*) alongside the grand **Tsim Sha Tsui Railway Terminus** (*Jian-sha-zui Huo-che-zhan*) reshaped urban mobility and commerce. However, the era was punctuated by immense tragedy, notably the catastrophic **Great Typhoon of 1906** (*Bing-wu Da-feng-bao*) and the devastating **Happy Valley Racecourse Fire Disaster** (*Pao-ma-di Ma-chang Da-huo*) of **1918**.

## Key Themes

### Colonial Governance and Social Stratification
British administration expanded deeper into local society, characterized by legal and social segregation. The **Enactment of the Peak District Reservation Ordinance** (*Shan-ding Bao-liu Tiao-li*) of **1904** legally reserved the exclusive Victoria Peak area for non-Chinese residents, formalizing racial hierarchies. Simultaneously, local elites grew more organized, leading to the **Establishment of the Chinese General Chamber of Commerce** (*Xiang-gang Zhong-hua Zong-shang-hui*) in **1900**, which bridged traditional Chinese merchant interests and colonial authorities.

### Geopolitical Tensions and Revolutionary Ferment
Proximity to mainland China made Hong Kong a hotbed for nationalism and anti-imperialism. The **Tatsu Maru Boycott and Anti-Japanese Movement** (*Da-chen-wan Feng-chao*) highlighted rising Chinese national consciousness. The **Outbreak of the 1911 Xinhai Revolution** (*Xin-hai Ge-ming*) caused massive demographic shifts as refugees and political figures flooded the colony. Tensions persisted into the wartime era, manifested in the **Assassination Attempt on Governor Sir Francis May** (*Mei-hui-han*) in **1912**, the **Tramway Boycott Over Foreign Coins** (*Bi-zhi Feng-chao*), and the **Mobilization of the Chinese Labour Corps** during the First World War, as **Hong Kong Enters the First World War** (*Di-yi Ci Shi-jie Da-zhan*) in **1914**.

### Institutional and Cultural Development
Education and higher learning saw monumental progress with the **Inauguration of the University of Hong Kong** (*Xiang-gang Da-xue*) in **1911**, which aimed to train professionals for the region under British academic frameworks.

## How it Shaped Hong Kong

The events of this twenty-year span defined the modern boundaries and geopolitical identity of Hong Kong. The acquisition of the New Territories ensured the colony's agricultural and spatial survival, setting the stage for future suburban and industrial expansion. Meanwhile, the influx of mainland revolutionaries and merchants cemented Hong Kong's dual identity as a British-administered port city intimately tied to Chinese political and cultural affairs.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Viewed the New Territories lease as an essential strategic necessity to protect the defensibility and economic viability of the original colony, while administering the new regions through a mix of indirect rule and gradual legal integration.
- **Qing Court and Nationalist Revolutionaries**: The weakening Qing dynasty viewed the expansion with helpless alarm, whereas Chinese revolutionaries treated Hong Kong as a vital, relatively safe sanctuary for plotting the overthrow of the imperial system.
- **New Territories Indigenous Communities**: Local villagers initially resisted the British takeover out of fears for their land rights, lineage traditions, and taxation, eventually negotiating a complex accommodation with colonial authorities that preserved customary laws like the Qing code regarding land tenure.
