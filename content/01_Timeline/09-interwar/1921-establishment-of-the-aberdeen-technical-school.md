## Background

During the early decades of the twentieth century, interwar Hong Kong experienced significant economic shifts, rapid population growth, and social challenges stemming from poverty and the displacement of young people. As the British colonial economy expanded, there was an increasing demand for skilled artisans, mechanics, and technicians to support dockyards, construction, and nascent manufacturing industries. Concurrently, Catholic religious orders, particularly the **Salesians of Don Bosco** (慈幼會), expanded their missionary and educational footprint in Southern China and Hong Kong, focusing heavily on the moral formation and vocational uplift of marginalized boys.

## What happened

In **1921**, the Salesian Fathers officially took over the administration of the existing trade school in Aberdeen, which had previously operated under different management. Located in the southern district of Hong Kong Island, the institution was reorganized as the **Aberdeen Trade School** (香港仔工業學校, later known as the Aberdeen Technical School). The curriculum was restructured to combine rigorous academic instruction with practical, hands-on training in trades such as carpentry, shoemaking, tailoring, and metalwork. This approach aimed to equip impoverished and orphaned Chinese youth with marketable skills that would ensure their self-reliance and integration into the workforce.

## Significance

The establishment and subsequent development of the school under Salesian administration marked a crucial step in the evolution of vocational education in colonial Hong Kong. Rather than relying solely on traditional apprenticeship systems, the institution provided a formalized framework for industrial training that blended Western pedagogical methods with practical labor needs. Over the interwar years, the school grew in reputation, supplying skilled workers to local industries and establishing a model for charitable and religious involvement in social welfare that persisted long after the Second World War.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Perspective**: Colonial administrators generally welcomed religious and charitable initiatives that addressed juvenile poverty and supplied trained labor without requiring direct government expenditure, viewing vocational schools as stabilizing influences during an era marked by labor unrest and strikes.
- **Salesian and Catholic Perspective**: The religious order viewed the school as a vital mission field dedicated to saving souls through preventive systems of education, combining Christian morality with practical trades to rescue destitute boys from the perils of the streets.
- **Local Chinese and Historiographical Perspective**: Later scholars and local historians often emphasize the school's role in providing upward mobility for working-class families, noting how industrial training institutions inadvertently laid the groundwork for Hong Kong's later manufacturing workforce.
