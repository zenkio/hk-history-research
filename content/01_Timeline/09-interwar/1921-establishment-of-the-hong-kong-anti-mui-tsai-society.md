## Background
During the early 20th century, the *mui tsai* (妹仔) system—a practice involving the transfer of young girls into domestic servitude—was widely prevalent in Hong Kong and Southern China. While often framed by practitioners as a form of adoption or charitable upbringing for girls from impoverished families, the system lacked formal legal protections and frequently resulted in the exploitation of the girls involved. By the late 1910s, pressure from international humanitarian groups and local activists began to mount against the British colonial government, which had previously maintained a policy of non-interference in what was categorized as a traditional Chinese social custom.

## What happened
In **1921**, a group of prominent local Chinese elites, intellectuals, and reformers founded the Hong Kong Anti-Mui Tsai Society (香港反對蓄婢會). This organization aimed to publicly challenge the legitimacy of the system and lobby the colonial administration to implement legislative bans. The founding of the society followed a period of intense public discourse in newspapers and local assemblies. The society sought not only to provide a platform for anti-slavery advocacy but also to offer a structured way to manage the transition of existing *mui tsai* out of servitude. Their efforts necessitated a complex negotiation between Chinese customary law, colonial administrative policy, and emerging humanitarian standards.

## Significance
The establishment of the society is widely regarded by historians as a milestone in the development of organized civic activism in Hong Kong. It represented a shift where local Chinese figures began to actively define and redefine social morality within the colonial context, moving away from a reliance on traditional patriarchal structures. The movement forced the colonial government to reconcile its commitment to colonial neutrality with international obligations, eventually leading to the Female Domestic Service Ordinance of **1923**. The long-term impact remains a subject of study, particularly regarding how the society navigated the tension between modernizing reforms and the preservation of family integrity in a transitional colonial society.

## Perspectives

- **Colonial Administration (Early 20th Century)**: The British colonial government initially viewed the *mui tsai* practice as a 'domestic institution' protected by the Convention of Peking, fearing that direct intervention would destabilize the local social order and cause friction with the Chinese population. Their eventual policy shift was driven as much by pressure from the British Parliament and international anti-slavery lobbies as by the activities of the local society.
- **Traditional Chinese Community Leaders**: Many contemporary local elites and family heads defended the system as an act of charity that prevented young girls from starvation during times of extreme poverty. They argued that the Anti-Mui Tsai Society’s efforts were an imposition of Western cultural values that misunderstood the complexity and potential benevolence of traditional Chinese households.
- **Modern Historiography**: Later scholarship often views the society as a nexus of class and power, suggesting that the reformist movement was led by an emerging Chinese elite who used humanitarian advocacy to align themselves with colonial sensibilities and assert social control over traditional practices. Researchers debate the extent to which the lives of the girls involved actually improved following the society's legislative victories.
