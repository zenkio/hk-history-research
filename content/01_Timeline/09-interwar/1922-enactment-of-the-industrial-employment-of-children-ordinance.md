## Background
During the early 1920s, Hong Kong's manufacturing sector experienced rapid expansion, leading to an increased reliance on cheap labor, including young children. Working conditions in local factories, glassworks, and match-making workshops were frequently hazardous and unregulated. These conditions drew severe criticism from visiting British social reformers, Christian missionaries, and emerging local labor organizers who viewed the exploitation of minors as a pressing humanitarian crisis.

## What happened
Prompted by mounting pressure from both domestic social campaigns and scrutiny from the Colonial Office in London, the Legislative Council passed the **Industrial Employment of Children Ordinance** in **1922**. The legislation represented the first formal attempt by the colonial administration to legally regulate child labor within the colony's burgeoning industrial sector, setting baseline restrictions on the age of employment and working hours for minors.

## Significance
Historians view the **1922** ordinance as a pivotal, albeit modest, turning point in Hong Kong's labor history. While enforcement remained notoriously weak due to a shortage of factory inspectors and widespread evasion by factory owners, the legislation established the crucial precedent of state intervention in colonial labor markets and laid the groundwork for future twentieth-century labor reforms.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Colonial officials often framed the ordinance as a progressive step toward aligning Hong Kong's standards with international labor norms, though they simultaneously worried that overly stringent regulations would harm the colony's competitive economic edge.
- **Local industrialists and factory owners**: Many local business operators resisted state interference, arguing that child labor was vital for the survival of struggling family enterprises and that strict British-style regulations ignored local economic realities.
- **Labor organizers and reformers**: Activists viewed the initial legislation as dangerously weak and poorly enforced, serving primarily as a superficial concession to appease critics in London rather than offering genuine protection for vulnerable workers.
