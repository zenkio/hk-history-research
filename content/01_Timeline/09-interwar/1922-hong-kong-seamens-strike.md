## Background

In the early 1920s, Hong Kong experienced severe inflation following the First World War, while the wages of local Chinese workers remained largely stagnant. British shipping companies and colonial authorities maintained hierarchical labor practices that favored foreign staff and offered little recourse for local grievances. In late 1921, the Chinese Seamen's Union (中華海員工會) formally demanded a wage increase of 30 to 40 percent to counter the rising cost of living. When employers and colonial officials repeatedly rebuffed these demands, the union began organizing industrial action.

## What happened

On **January 12, 1922**, the Hong Kong Seamen's Strike officially commenced. Led by union figures such as **Su Pak-lam** (蘇伯林) and **Chan Lun** (陳倫), the movement rapidly gained momentum. Within weeks, the strike expanded far beyond maritime workers to encompass dockers, cooks, hotel staff, and ricemill workers, bringing the total number of striking participants to over 120,000. 

As the strike crippled the colony's economy, the colonial administration attempted to suppress the movement by deregistering the union and arresting leaders. In response, workers began a mass exodus from Hong Kong back to Guangdong province. The crisis reached a bloody turning point on **March 4, 1922**, during the **Sha Tin incident** (沙田慘案), when British military forces fired on unarmed striking workers marching along the railway, resulting in several deaths. Public outrage over the violence forced colonial authorities back to the negotiating table. The strike concluded when employers agreed to wage increases ranging from 15 to 30 percent, alongside the official recognition of the union.

## Significance

Modern scholarship frequently views the 1922 strike as a watershed moment in the history of the Chinese labor movement. It demonstrated the immense economic and political power of organized Chinese labor within a British colony. The strike also fostered closer ties between local trade unions and the Nationalist government (Kuomintang) in Guangzhou, laying the groundwork for the even larger Canton-Hong Kong Strike of 1925–1926.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Authorities**: Colonial officials initially viewed the strike as a dangerous political subversion orchestrated by external agitators from Guangzhou rather than a genuine economic dispute. They were concerned with maintaining public order, preserving British commercial dominance, and preventing the total paralysis of the port.
- **Chinese Labor and Nationalists**: For Chinese workers and the emerging anti-imperialist movement, the strike was a triumphant assertion of national dignity and labor rights against colonial oppression. Nationalist leaders in Guangdong saw the solidarity of Hong Kong workers as a vital component of the broader anti-colonial struggle.
