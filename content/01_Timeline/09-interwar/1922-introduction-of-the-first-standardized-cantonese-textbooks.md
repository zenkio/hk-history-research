## Background
During the early 20th century, the educational landscape of Hong Kong (香港) was undergoing significant shifts. While the colonial government initially favored traditional classical Chinese and English-medium instruction, the influence of the New Culture Movement in China began to filter into the territory. By the **1920s**, discussions regarding the medium of instruction grew more urgent as educators grappled with the gap between the formal written language and the spoken vernacular of the local population. The **1919** May Fourth Movement in China pushed for the adoption of *Baihua* (白話), or vernacular Chinese, but Hong Kong's unique position under British rule led to a distinct trajectory.

## What happened
In **1922**, a series of standardized textbooks utilizing Cantonese-influenced grammatical structures and local vernacular expressions were introduced in select schools. These textbooks, sometimes referred to as the 'Cantonese readers' (粵語讀本), aimed to make literacy more accessible to the urban working class and student population. Unlike the mainland vernacular movement, which sought a unified national language (國語), the Hong Kong initiative was grounded in the practical utility of the local tongue, which was the primary language of daily life in the territory's bustling ports and markets.

## Significance
The introduction of these texts is often cited as a pivotal moment in the codification of Hong Kong's linguistic identity. By creating standardized materials, the educational system formalized the use of Cantonese in a professional and academic setting, a practice that set the region apart from the Beijing-centered linguistic standardization efforts. While some sources suggest this was a strategic move by colonial authorities to create a distinct cultural identity, others argue it was a grassroots effort by local teachers to improve pedagogical efficiency for students who did not speak Mandarin at home. Regardless of intent, this period began a long-standing tension between the usage of local Cantonese and the push for standardized written Chinese, a dialogue that has defined the territory's educational development for the past century.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: The colonial government viewed the standardization of textbooks as a pragmatic tool for basic literacy. Officials often prioritized social stability and economic utility over nationalist linguistic agendas originating from the mainland.
- **Republican China/Mainland Intellectuals**: Mainland educators associated with the vernacular movement often perceived the focus on Cantonese as a parochial regression. They viewed the unification of the Chinese language as essential to national modernization and considered regional variants an obstacle to national cohesion.
- **Local Hong Kong Educators**: Many local teachers and community leaders argued that Cantonese-based education was a pedagogical necessity. They maintained that teaching children in their mother tongue provided a more stable and effective foundation for learning than enforcing an unfamiliar regional dialect.
