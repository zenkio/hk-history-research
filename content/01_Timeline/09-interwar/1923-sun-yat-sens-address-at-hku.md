## Background
On **February 20, 1923**, **Sun Yat-sen** (**孫中山**) returned to the University of Hong Kong (**香港大學**), where he had previously studied medicine at the Hong Kong College of Medicine for Chinese. By 1923, Sun was a pivotal figure in modern Chinese history, having led the **1911 Revolution** (**辛亥革命**) that overthrew the Qing dynasty and established the Republic of China. His return to the British colony occurred amidst a complex political landscape defined by the rise of Chinese nationalism, the aftermath of the **1922 seamen's strike** (**香港海員大罷工**), and shifting British colonial policies.

## What happened
Visiting the Loke Yew Hall at the University of Hong Kong, **Sun Yat-sen** addressed the faculty and students. In his speech, Sun famously confessed that his revolutionary ideas were conceived in Hong Kong. He contrasted the security, good government, and rule of law he experienced in the British colony with the corruption, disorder, and misrule he perceived under the Qing government in mainland China. Sun used the occasion to encourage students to actively participate in the transformation and modernization of China, viewing the colony's educational institutions as crucial incubators for future leaders.

## Significance
The address remains a frequently cited text in the historiography of modern Hong Kong and China, illustrating the paradoxical relationship between British colonial administration and Chinese nationalism. While the speech highlighted Hong Kong's role as a safe haven and intellectual seedbed for anti-Qing revolutionaries, historians continue to debate the extent to which Sun's praise of colonial governance was a tactical rhetorical device or a genuine endorsement of British administration over traditional Chinese political structures.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Perspective**: Colonial authorities and British observers often viewed Sun's remarks as a vindication of the benefits of British law, order, and Western education, demonstrating the colony's civilizing influence on Chinese intellectuals.
- **Chinese Nationalist Perspective**: Nationalist historiography emphasizes Sun's agency in utilizing a foreign-administered territory as a strategic base to plan the overthrow of autocratic rule and modernize the Chinese nation.
- **Later Scholarship**: Modern historians analyze the speech as a complex rhetorical moment, noting that Sun's praise of colonial order must be understood within the context of his ongoing pragmatic negotiations with various foreign powers to gain support for a unified China.
