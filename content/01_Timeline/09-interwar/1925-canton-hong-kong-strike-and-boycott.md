## Background

In the wake of the May Thirtieth Incident in Shanghai in **1925**, where British-led police fired on Chinese protesters, anti-foreign and anti-imperialist sentiment surged across China. Tensions had already been simmering in Hong Kong and Guangdong due to labor exploitation, racial discrimination, and growing nationalist fervor promoted by both the Kuomintang and the Chinese Communist Party. These conditions culminated in the organization of a massive general strike directed against British colonial rule in Hong Kong.

## What happened

Beginning in **June 1925**, Chinese workers in Hong Kong staged a general strike, eventually involving hundreds of thousands of workers across various industries, including transport, dockyards, and public utilities. Strikers engaged in a mass exodus from the colony, relocating to Guangzhou (廣州) where the Nationalist government supported them. Concurrently, a strict economic boycott was imposed on Hong Kong, cutting off food supplies, shipping, and trade. The blockade crippled the colony's commercial viability for over a year, paralyzing the port and forcing the colonial administration to implement emergency financial measures and issue paper currency to prevent total collapse. The strike eventually wound down and was called off in **October 1926** as political priorities in Guangzhou shifted.

## Significance

Scholars debate the primary drivers and long-term outcomes of the strike, but it fundamentally demonstrated the potency of organized Chinese labor and nationalist mobilization against a Western colonial power. While it failed to overthrow British administration in Hong Kong, it permanently altered labor relations, demonstrated the economic interdependence between Hong Kong and the Pearl River Delta, and marked a crucial chapter in the early history of the Chinese Communist Party and the Kuomintang united front.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Viewed the strike as an external subversion orchestrated by Bolshevik agitators and the Nationalist government in Guangzhou rather than legitimate local labor grievances, utilizing emergency regulations and police powers to maintain order.
- **Chinese Nationalist and Leftist Accounts**: Framed the event as a patriotic, anti-imperialist struggle by oppressed Chinese workers against British colonial exploitation and imperialist violence following the May Thirtieth Incident.
- **Local Hong Kong Business Community**: Experienced the blockade as an existential economic crisis that forced pragmatic negotiations, revealing the vulnerability of the colony's entrepôt economy to political instability in mainland China.
