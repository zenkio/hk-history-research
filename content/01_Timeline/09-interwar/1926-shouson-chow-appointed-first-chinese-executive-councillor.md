## Background

During the 1920s, Hong Kong experienced severe social and political upheaval, notably the Canton-Hong Kong strike from 1925 to 1926. This massive labor dispute paralyzed the colony's economy and demonstrated to British colonial authorities that maintaining stability required closer cooperation with moderate, influential Chinese leaders. Prior to this period, governance was heavily dominated by British officials and European merchants, with limited formal representation for the local Chinese population.

## What happened

On **July 9, 1926**, **Sir Shouson Chow** (周壽臣) was officially appointed to the Executive Council (ExCo), the highest policymaking body in the colony under the Governor. Having previously served on the Legislative Council (LegCo) and established a distinguished career bridging Chinese and Western spheres, Chow's appointment represented a calculated effort by the administration to co-opt the local elite and secure a reliable channel of communication with the broader Chinese community.

## Significance

This appointment is widely viewed as a symbolic turning point in colonial governance, offering the first institutional opening for a Chinese representative at the highest level of executive power. However, historians debate the extent of influence Chow actually wielded. While some contemporary and colonial accounts emphasized the progressive nature of his inclusion, later critical scholarship suggests that such appointments were largely tokenistic, designed to appease elite interests without substantially devolving political power from British hands.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Viewed the appointment as a progressive measure to integrate trusted local leaders into governance, thereby fostering civic stability and loyalty during a period of intense regional turmoil.
- **Later critical scholarship**: Argues that the inclusion of figures like Shouson Chow was a form of elite co-optation designed to preserve colonial hierarchy and appease wealthy merchants while excluding genuine democratic reform.
