## Background

By the late **1920s**, wireless telegraphy and radio broadcasting were rapidly transforming mass communication globally. In **Hong Kong**, commercial and amateur radio experiments had taken place earlier in the decade, but the colonial administration recognized the potential value of a dedicated, state-regulated broadcasting service. The initiative was designed to disseminate official news, provide entertainment, and strengthen communication ties within the British colony.

## What happened

On **June 30, 1928**, regular public radio transmissions officially commenced under the station callsign **GOW** (later changed to **ZBW**). Operated directly by the colonial government, the station broadcasted a mix of musical performances, speeches, and news bulletins. This broadcast marked the formal establishment of what would eventually evolve into **Radio Television Hong Kong (RTHK)**, making it the oldest broadcasting station in the territory.

## Significance

The launch of public radio represented a major milestone in Hong Kong's media history, shifting the territory toward an era of mass electronic communication. While initially serving a relatively small segment of the population that could afford radio receivers—primarily expatriates and wealthier local residents—broadcasting gradually expanded its linguistic reach and cultural programming over the subsequent decade, deeply integrating into daily urban life before the disruptions of the Second World War.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Viewed the establishment of GOW as a progressive step in imperial communication, modernization, and the dissemination of official information to foster stability and civic connection across the colony.
- **Local Residents and Scholars**: Note that while the station laid technical foundations for public media, its early programming and governance heavily reflected British colonial priorities, only gradually shifting to more inclusive representation of the broader local populace in later decades.
