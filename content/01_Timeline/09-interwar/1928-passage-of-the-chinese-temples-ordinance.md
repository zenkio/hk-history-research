## Background
During the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, traditional Chinese temples in Hong Kong and the New Territories operated largely autonomously, managed by local lineages, village communities, or self-appointed temple keepers. Revenues generated from donations, incense sales, and festival fees were generally retained locally and used for community welfare or religious maintenance. Following the turbulence of the 1925–1926 Canton-Hong Kong strike and boycott, the British colonial administration grew increasingly concerned about the potential for anti-colonial mobilization and the unchecked financial flows within indigenous religious institutions.

## What happened
In **1928**, the Legislative Council passed the Chinese Temples Ordinance (華人廟宇條例). The legislation mandated that all Chinese temples in the colony must be formally registered with the government. Furthermore, it established a central Chinese Temples Committee (華人廟宇委員會) to oversee temple administration and manage surplus revenues. Rather than managing these sites directly, the colonial state co-opted prominent local Chinese leaders to sit on the committee, utilizing these elites as intermediaries between the government and grassroots religious communities.

## Significance
The ordinance marked a significant expansion of colonial state reach into the social and cultural fabric of the Chinese population, transforming traditional religious spaces into regulated entities. It institutionalized a system of governance that relied heavily on elite Chinese mediation, a cornerstone of British indirect rule in Hong Kong. While intended to curb financial corruption and political subversion, the law also fundamentally altered the autonomy of local ancestral and religious practices, embedding them within a legal and bureaucratic framework managed jointly by the colonial state and conservative community leaders.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Viewed the ordinance as a necessary administrative reform to prevent the misuse of public funds, ensure public order, and suppress potential anti-colonial agitation following the 1925-1926 strike.
- **Local Communities and Lineages**: Often regarded the legislation as an unwelcome colonial intrusion into customary religious affairs and an appropriation of local resources by urban-based elites who had little connection to rural traditions.
- **Later Scholarship**: Interprets the ordinance as a key mechanism of institutionalized co-optation, demonstrating how the colonial state successfully incorporated Chinese elites to legitimize state control over indigenous civil society.
