## Background

The practice of keeping *mui tsai* (妹仔), young girls sold into domestic servitude by impoverished families, had long existed in traditional Chinese society and persisted in colonial Hong Kong. By the early **1920s**, humanitarian groups in Britain and anti-slavery activists in Hong Kong, notably the Anti-Mui Tsai Society, launched rigorous campaigns demanding the complete abolition of the system. While the colonial administration initially defended local customs to avoid destabilizing Chinese social structures, mounting parliamentary pressure from London eventually forced a policy shift.

## What happened

Following earlier ordinances and investigations, the Hong Kong government formally enacted regulations requiring the mandatory registration of all *mui tsai*. On **November 30, 1929**, the deadline for registration took effect, compelling masters to declare every girl under their servitude so authorities could monitor their treatment, wages, and eventual emancipation. The legislation aimed to prevent the introduction of new girls into servitude while gradually phasing out the existing population through oversight and government-managed rescue homes, such as the Po Leung Kuk (保良局).

## Significance

The **1929** registration marked a critical legal turning point in shifting the colonial government's stance from tacit acceptance to active regulation of domestic labor. However, historians note that enforcement proved challenging, as many households concealed the girls or disguised their status as adopted daughters (*simpzai* or 媳抱仔). The reform highlighted the complex tensions between British humanitarian ideals, colonial governance, and traditional Chinese family customs in interwar Hong Kong.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Colonial officials viewed the registration system as a pragmatic and progressive step that balanced humanitarian concerns with the need to avoid provoking widespread social unrest among the Chinese elite.
- **Local Reformers and Anti-Slavery Activists**: Activists argued that mandatory registration was merely a half-measure, contending that only immediate and total abolition could genuinely protect vulnerable children from exploitation.
- **Traditional Chinese Community**: Many conservative community leaders and households perceived the legislation as an unwelcome interference in traditional family structures, domestic arrangements, and customary practices.
