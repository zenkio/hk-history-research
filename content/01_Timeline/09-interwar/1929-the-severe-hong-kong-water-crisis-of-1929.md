## Background
By the late **1920s**, Hong Kong's rapid population growth had continually outpaced its municipal water supply. Prior to the crisis, the colony relied heavily on rainfall runoff captured by existing reservoirs, such as the Tai Tam system (大潭水塘) and the recently completed Shing Mun Valley (城門水塘) preliminary works. However, the region's monsoonal climate made it historically susceptible to seasonal dry spells, and infrastructure struggled to keep pace with the demands of an expanding urban center.

## What happened
In **1929**, an acute drought severely depleted reservoir storage across the territory, forcing the colonial government to implement extreme water rationing measures. Access to municipal water was restricted to limited hours daily, causing widespread disruption to households and commerce. To avert a humanitarian and economic disaster, authorities organized emergency water imports by sea, deploying vessels to transport fresh water from mainland sources.

## Significance
The crisis laid bare the fragility of Hong Kong's water security infrastructure. It prompted urgent reassessments of engineering capabilities and accelerated major reservoir construction projects, shaping water management policies for decades to come.

Historians note that while the immediate crisis was managed through emergency logistics, it permanently altered how the colonial administration and local communities viewed natural resource vulnerability.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Viewed the crisis primarily as an engineering and logistical challenge, emphasizing swift emergency procurement, maritime water transport, and the expansion of centralized reservoir infrastructure to secure the colony's economic stability.
- **Local Hong Kong Residents**: Experienced the severe daily hardships of strict rationing, heightened public health concerns, and disruptions to domestic life and small-scale manufacturing reliant on a stable water supply.
