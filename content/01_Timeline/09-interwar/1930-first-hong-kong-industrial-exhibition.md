## Background
During the **1920s**, Hong Kong faced significant economic turbulence, largely driven by the **Canton-Hong Kong Strike (省港大罷工)** of 1925–1926 and persistent boycotts of British goods. These events, combined with the instability of the late Qing and early Republican periods in China, forced colonial administrators and local businessmen to reconsider the territory's singular reliance on its status as a free-port entrepôt. As traditional trade routes were disrupted, there was a growing realization that diversifying into light manufacturing could insulate the local economy from external geopolitical shocks.

## What happened
In **1930**, the **Chinese Manufacturers' Association of Hong Kong (香港中華廠商聯合會)**—then in its nascent form—partnered with colonial authorities to organize the first public exhibition dedicated to local goods. Held at the **City Hall (大會堂)**, the event showcased a range of consumer products manufactured within the territory, including textiles, preserved foods, and light industrial items. The exhibition was designed to foster a sense of 'local' quality, encouraging both the expatriate community and the local Chinese population to purchase goods produced within the colony rather than relying exclusively on imported European or mainland Chinese commodities.

## Significance
Historians generally regard this exhibition as the foundational step in Hong Kong’s industrial development, pre-dating the massive post-war manufacturing boom of the 1950s. By placing 'made in Hong Kong' products in the public eye, the event successfully promoted the idea of local industrial self-sufficiency. While the **British colonial** administration initially viewed local manufacturing as a minor supplement to trade, the success of the exhibition suggested a shift in policy, eventually leading to closer cooperation between the government and local industrial elites. The exhibition is credited by later scholars with establishing a template for the recurring trade fairs that would define Hong Kong’s economic identity for decades to come, marking a departure from the purely mercantile interests of the 19th century.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Government**: The colonial administration viewed the exhibition as a pragmatic response to trade instability, largely concerned with maintaining civil order and economic revenue. They supported the initiative primarily to minimize the economic impact of labor unrest and to ensure the colony remained a stable profit-center within the Empire.
- **Local Chinese Business Elites**: For the emerging class of local manufacturers, the exhibition represented an opportunity to assert their autonomy and influence in the face of both British colonial indifference and the political chaos of the Chinese mainland. They championed local manufacturing as a path to community self-reliance and economic modernization.
