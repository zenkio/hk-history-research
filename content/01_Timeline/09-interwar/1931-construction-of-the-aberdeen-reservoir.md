## Background

During the interwar period, Hong Kong experienced significant population growth driven by migration from mainland China, placing immense strain on the colony's municipal services. Water supply was a persistent crisis, as existing reservoirs struggled to meet the demands of urban expansion. To secure a reliable water source for the southern side of Hong Kong Island, the colonial administration initiated plans for a new water storage scheme in the Aberdeen (香港仔) area.

## What happened

Construction of the **Aberdeen Reservoir** began in the late 1920s and was officially completed in **1931**. The engineering project involved the construction of two dams—the upper and lower Aberdeen dams—along with a network of catchwaters and tunnels designed to capture and channel runoff from the surrounding hills. Labour was primarily sourced locally, executed under the supervision of the Public Works Department (工務司署). The completion of the reservoir successfully augmented the island's water storage capacity during a challenging economic period marked by the Great Depression.

## Significance

The project represented a critical step in the infrastructural modernization of interwar Hong Kong. By expanding municipal water supplies, the reservoir supported continued urban development and public health improvements in the expanding districts. However, the expansion of colonial waterworks often involved land appropriation and altered traditional rural livelihoods, themes that remain central to the study of modern Hong Kong's spatial transformation.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Viewed the reservoir as a triumph of modern engineering and a vital humanitarian and administrative necessity to sustain urban growth and public health in the colony.
- **Local and New Territories communities**: Often experienced these large-scale infrastructure projects through the lens of displacement, resource competition, and government intervention in customary land use.
