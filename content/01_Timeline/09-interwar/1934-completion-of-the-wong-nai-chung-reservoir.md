## Background
During the interwar era, Hong Kong experienced significant demographic expansion, placing unprecedented strain on municipal infrastructure. The growing urban population, coupled with industrial development and sanitation needs, frequently outstripped the capacity of existing water supply systems such as the Tai Tam and Pok Fu Lam reservoirs. British colonial authorities recognized that securing an expanded and reliable freshwater source was critical for public health and economic stability.

## What happened
Construction engineering efforts culminated in **1934** with the official completion of the **Wong Nai Chung Reservoir (黃泥涌水塘)**, located in the valley area of Hong Kong Island. The project involved damming the local stream network to create a storage basin designed to capture seasonal rainfall. Engineering works proceeded through the early 1930s, utilizing both Western hydrological techniques and local labor forces typical of public works projects in the colony at the time.

## Significance
The reservoir marked a notable milestone in municipal engineering and public utilities management in interwar Hong Kong. While it did not permanently solve the colony's chronic water scarcity—which persisted due to unpredictable weather patterns and continued population influxes—it temporarily stabilized water supplies for the expanding residential and commercial districts surrounding Victoria Harbour.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Viewed the reservoir's completion as a technical triumph and fulfillment of administrative duty to ensure public health, sanitation, and industrial growth in a rapidly expanding port city.
- **Local Working-Class Population**: Experienced the infrastructure development through the lens of ongoing socioeconomic pressures, where improved municipal services often contrasted with high living costs and inadequate housing.
