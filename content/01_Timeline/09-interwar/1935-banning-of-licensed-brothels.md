## Background
For decades, commercial sex in colonial Hong Kong operated under a government-regulated licensing system designed to control public health and generate municipal revenue. By the early **1930s**, however, growing moral reform movements in Britain and increasing international pressure from the League of Nations regarding human trafficking challenged the legitimacy of state-sanctioned prostitution in Crown colonies. 

## What happened
On **June 30, 1935**, following mandates from the British imperial government, Hong Kong authorities officially closed all remaining licensed brothels. The legislation sought to eradicate the legal framework of regulated prostitution, forcing the commercial sex trade underground and transforming the dynamics of entertainment hubs such as Shek Tong Tsui (石塘咀) and Sai营盘 (Sai Ying Pun).

## Significance
The sudden abolition marked a major turning point in colonial social policy, shifting the nature of vice from a publicly administered system to illicit operations. While intended to curb exploitation and align colonial administration with metropolitan ethical standards, the ban created new enforcement challenges for the Hong Kong Police Force and altered the livelihoods of thousands of women and workers connected to the industry.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Viewed the ban as a necessary moral alignment with imperial anti-trafficking commitments and international standards set by the League of Nations.
- **Local Communities and Business Owners**: Expressed concern over the economic fallout in districts reliant on nightlife commerce and worried that prohibition would merely drive the trade underground beyond municipal oversight.
