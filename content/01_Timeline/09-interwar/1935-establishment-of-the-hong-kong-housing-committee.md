## Background
During the 1920s and early 1930s, Hong Kong experienced a rapid population surge as migrants fled political instability and economic hardship in mainland China. The existing housing stock, primarily consisting of cramped tenement buildings or 'tong lau' (唐樓), became severely overcrowded. Poor ventilation, lack of sewage infrastructure, and extreme density facilitated the spread of diseases such as tuberculosis and bubonic plague. While private developers sought profit through high-density construction, the colonial administration was increasingly pressured by public health concerns and the potential for social unrest among the working class.

## What happened
In **1935**, the colonial government formally established the **Hong Kong Housing Committee** (香港房屋委員會) to address these systemic issues. The committee was tasked with surveying the living conditions of the working population and assessing the feasibility of state-led intervention in housing provision. For the first time, the government moved beyond purely regulatory roles—such as setting building height limits—toward an acknowledgment that housing was an administrative responsibility. The committee’s deliberations highlighted the clash between laissez-faire economic principles, favored by many in the merchant class, and the growing necessity for social welfare and public health measures.

## Significance
The creation of this committee marks a landmark shift in the philosophy of colonial governance. It represented an admission that the private market alone could not resolve the housing shortages that plagued the working class. Although the committee did not immediately launch large-scale public housing construction—largely due to impending war and budgetary constraints—its existence set a precedent for later policies. It served as a precursor to post-World War II housing initiatives, formalizing the discourse around sanitary standards and urban planning that would eventually reshape the city's landscape after 1945. The findings of the committee underscored the chronic nature of the housing crisis, framing it not just as a matter of construction, but as a critical element of colonial stability and public health strategy.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Perspective**: The administration viewed the committee as a necessary step to maintain public order and health standards, emphasizing cautious intervention to preserve the colony's reputation as a modern, stable port.
- **Local Working Class Perspective**: For the working-class families living in squalor, the committee was often seen as a belated and insufficient response to their desperate daily realities, with many hoping for direct government subsidies or rent controls that were slow to materialize.
- **Later Historical Scholarship**: Contemporary historians often interpret the committee's formation as a reactive measure to global trends in social reform, noting that the government's reluctance to spend public funds severely limited the committee's impact prior to the Pacific War.
