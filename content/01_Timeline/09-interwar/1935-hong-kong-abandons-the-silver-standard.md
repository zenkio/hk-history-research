## Background

For decades, Hong Kong's monetary system was anchored to silver, aligning the colony with international trade patterns and the economy of neighboring Guangdong. However, global economic turbulence during the Great Depression, fluctuating silver prices, and aggressive American silver-buying policies severely strained this arrangement. In **November 1935**, the Nationalist government in Nanjing instituted sweeping currency reforms, abandoning the silver standard entirely in favor of a fiat currency known as the *fabi* (法幣).

## What happened

Faced with the destabilizing effects of mainland China's departure from silver and the risk of massive capital flight, the Hong Kong government took decisive legislative action. On **December 5, 1935**, the Legislative Council rushed through the Currency Ordinance, officially ending the colony's long-standing reliance on silver. The legislation established a foreign exchange fund and pegged the Hong Kong dollar (香港元) to the British pound sterling (英鎊) at a rate initially set at approximately one shilling and three pence per dollar. Commercial banks, notably the Hongkong and Shanghai Banking Corporation (香港上海滙豐銀行), were authorized to issue the newly regulated banknotes.

## Significance

This monetary pivot fundamentally integrated Hong Kong's financial infrastructure with the British Empire, providing much-needed exchange rate stability for international trade and investment. It marked a crucial step in the modernization and centralization of the colony's banking system. At the same time, severing the direct monetary tie with silver distanced Hong Kong from the domestic economic shocks of mainland China, foreshadowing its emergence as a distinct financial hub.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Colonial authorities viewed the currency reform as a vital and successful defensive measure that protected the colony's economy from external shocks and integrated its financial systems with the stability of sterling.
- **Local Merchants and Trade Communities**: Many merchants trading across the border experienced initial anxiety regarding how the shift away from silver and the new peg to sterling would affect commercial transactions with mainland China and regional markets.
- **Later Economic Historians**: Scholars often analyze the 1935 ordinance as a pivotal moment of divergence, wherein Hong Kong's monetary policy was decisively uncoupled from Chinese economic fluctuations and formally anchored to British imperial interests.
