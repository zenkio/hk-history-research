## Background
By the early 1930s, the **Hongkong and Shanghai Banking Corporation (HSBC)** (香港上海滙豐銀行) needed a larger and more modern headquarters to reflect its dominant role in the financial architecture of East Asia. The bank's previous second-generation building, completed in the late 19th century, had become insufficient for its expanding operations amid Hong Kong's growing status as a major entrepôt and financial hub. Planning began for a state-of-the-art skyscraper that would project both corporate strength and architectural modernity.

## What happened
On **October 10, 1935**—deliberately chosen to coincide with Double Ten Day—the third HSBC building officially opened in Central, Hong Kong. Designed by the architectural firm Palmer and Turner and built by Gammon, the structure stood at **70 meters** with **13 storeys**, making it the tallest building in the British Empire outside of London and the tallest building in Asia upon its completion. It featured cutting-edge technological advancements for the era, including the first fully air-conditioned office building in Hong Kong, high-speed elevators, and an advanced steel-frame construction designed to withstand typhoons. The interior was lavishly decorated with marble, bronze, and mosaic artwork depicting the bank's global reach.

## Significance
The opening of the third HSBC building was widely celebrated as a testament to Hong Kong's resilience and growing prominence in global finance, even during the broader economic challenges of the Great Depression. The skyscraper fundamentally altered the Victoria Harbour skyline, shifting the visual dominance from classical colonial administrative structures to corporate financial power. It served as a potent symbol of British imperial and financial confidence in the region, just a few years before the turbulence of the Second World War drastically reshaped the colony.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration and Corporate View**: Viewed the building's completion as a crowning achievement of British engineering, financial stability, and free-market capitalism in the Far East, projecting long-term confidence in the colony's strategic and economic future.
- **Local Workers and Community Perspective**: For many local residents and construction laborers, the project represented vital employment during difficult economic times, though it also starkly contrasted the modern luxury of European corporate power with the impoverished living conditions in surrounding tenement districts.
- **Later Architectural and Historical Scholarship**: Modern scholars analyze the building as a pivotal monument of Art Deco and early modernist architecture in Asia, highlighting how Western corporate entities shaped the urban topography of colonial port cities.
