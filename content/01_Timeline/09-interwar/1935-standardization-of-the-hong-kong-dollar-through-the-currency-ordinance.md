## Background
During the early 20th century, Hong Kong's monetary system was primarily based on silver, mirroring the trade practices of the Qing Dynasty (清朝) and early Republican China. While the Hong Kong dollar was technically the local currency, it functioned alongside various silver coins and bullion. This system left the colony highly vulnerable to global fluctuations in the price of silver. During the **1930s**, the United States' Silver Purchase Act led to a dramatic spike in global silver prices, which effectively drained silver from the Chinese and Hong Kong economies, causing severe deflation and trade instability.

## What happened
In **1935**, the colonial government, under the guidance of the British administration, responded to the escalating crisis by passing the **Currency Ordinance (貨幣條例)**. This landmark legislation abandoned the silver standard that had defined the region for decades. The government nationalized the silver holdings of the note-issuing banks, primarily the **Hongkong and Shanghai Banking Corporation (香港上海匯豐銀行)**, the **Chartered Bank of India, Australia and China (印度、澳洲及中國渣打銀行)**, and the **Mercantile Bank of India (有利銀行)**. In its place, the ordinance established a managed currency system that pegged the Hong Kong dollar to the British pound sterling at a rate of approximately 16 pence per dollar. To facilitate this, the Exchange Fund (外匯基金) was created to manage the currency's value and reserves.

## Significance
This reform is widely considered the birth of the modern, independent Hong Kong monetary system. By decoupling the local currency from silver, the government gained a greater degree of control over money supply and exchange rates. While the shift initially caused concerns regarding the loss of autonomy to British monetary policy, it provided the necessary stability for Hong Kong to weather the global economic pressures of the late 1930s. This transition laid the groundwork for the structural financial management that would define Hong Kong's role as an international trade hub in the post-war era.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: The administration viewed the ordinance as a pragmatic necessity to ensure the economic survival of the colony amidst global volatility. Officials emphasized the need for alignment with the British Empire's financial architecture to maintain trade reliability.
- **Chinese Mercantile Community**: Local merchants and business leaders were initially divided; while many welcomed the end of erratic silver prices, others feared that the direct link to the British pound would make Hong Kong commerce overly dependent on London's fiscal policy and distant interests.
