## Background
During the interwar period, Hong Kong experienced rapid population growth, driven by migration from mainland China amidst political instability. This demographic expansion placed immense pressure on the colony's municipal infrastructure, particularly the water supply system. Prior to the 1930s, existing reservoirs such as Tai Tam and Aberdeen struggled to meet the rising demand during dry seasons, leading to frequent water shortages.

## What happened
Construction of the Shing Mun Reservoir (城門水塘), later named the Jubilee Reservoir to commemorate the silver jubilee of King George V, began in the 1930s. It was a massive civil engineering undertaking that involved constructing a large dam and flooding the Shing Mun valley. The project was officially completed on **January 30, 1936**, with the ceremonial opening officiated by Governor **Sir Andrew Caldecott**. The reservoir vastly increased the colony's water storage capacity and integrated several catchment areas in the New Territories.

## Significance
The completion of the Shing Mun Reservoir marked a major milestone in Hong Kong's infrastructural development. It temporarily alleviated the chronic water shortages that had plagued the urban areas. However, as the population continued to surge in the late 1930s, further water supply challenges soon emerged, demonstrating the persistent difficulties in matching colonial infrastructure with rapid demographic shifts.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Viewed the project as a triumph of modern engineering and efficient governance, demonstrating the administration's commitment to public health and urban infrastructure for the growing colony.
- **New Territories local communities**: Experienced significant disruption, as the construction and subsequent flooding of the valley displaced local villagers, permanently altering traditional land use and rural livelihoods in the area.
