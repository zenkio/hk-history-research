## Background

During the interwar period, Hong Kong experienced rapid demographic and economic shifts alongside rising social activism influenced by regional and global movements. By the mid-1930s, local reformers and expatriates increasingly turned their attention to pressing social welfare issues, particularly the conditions affecting women and children. Traditional practices such as the mui tsai system—the practice of keeping young girls as indentured domestic servants—drew intense scrutiny and debate among humanitarian groups, colonial authorities, and local elites.

## What happened

In **1937**, the Hong Kong Council of Women (香港婦女委員會) was formally established. Founded by a coalition of prominent women activists and social reformers, the organization aimed to coordinate advocacy efforts for women's rights, improve labor and social welfare standards, and provide a structured platform for female civic engagement. The council sought to address systemic inequalities embedded in both traditional customs and colonial legal frameworks, engaging in public campaigns and liaising with government officials to push for legislative and social reforms.

## Significance

The creation of the council marked a significant milestone in the history of local feminist activism and civil society organization in Hong Kong. It represented a transition from informal philanthropic efforts to structured, organized advocacy led by women. Although its early initiatives operated within the constraints of a colonial administration and a heavily stratified society, the council laid important organizational groundwork for future social welfare and women's rights movements in the territory.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Perspective**: Colonial officials often viewed early women's advocacy groups through the lens of administrative order and gradual social reform, sometimes welcoming assistance in welfare provision while carefully managing political agitation.
- **Local Reformer Perspective**: For local activists and progressive figures, the council was seen as an essential vehicle for modernizing society, empowering women, and dismantling oppressive customary practices like the mui tsai system independently of traditional patriarchal structures.
