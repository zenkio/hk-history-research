## Background
By the early 20th century, rapid population growth and the expanding needs of the University of Hong Kong's Faculty of Medicine outgrew the colony's existing healthcare facilities, principally the Civil Hospital and the Government Civil Hospital. Plans were initiated to construct a large-scale, state-of-the-art public teaching hospital that could meet modern clinical standards and serve as the hub for medical training in British Hong Kong.

## What happened
On **April 13, 1937**, **Queen Mary Hospital** (瑪麗醫院) was officially opened in Pok Fu Lam. Named after **Queen Mary**, the wife of King George V, the institution was equipped with advanced medical technology and designed to accommodate hundreds of beds. It immediately integrated with the University of Hong Kong to provide clinical instruction for medical students, taking over the primary teaching duties from older facilities.

## Significance
The establishment of the hospital significantly elevated the standard of modern healthcare and professional medical training in the colony. While colonial authorities emphasized the project as a humanitarian achievement and a testament to imperial efficiency, later scholarship and local perspectives often note that advanced medical services during this period were disproportionately accessed by urban elites, while rural and working-class communities continued to rely on traditional remedies or faced barriers to institutional care.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Viewed the hospital as a crowning achievement of imperial benevolence and modern urban planning, demonstrating Britain's commitment to public health and scientific progress in the territory.
- **Later scholarship and local communities**: Examine the hospital through the lens of colonial hierarchy, noting that while it advanced clinical medicine, access to its highest-tier facilities often reflected existing social and economic inequalities within the population.
