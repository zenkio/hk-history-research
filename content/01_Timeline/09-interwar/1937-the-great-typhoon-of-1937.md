## Background
In the interwar period, Hong Kong's meteorological infrastructure relied heavily on the Royal Observatory, led by figures like **T. F. Claxton** and later **C. W. Jeffreys**, to track severe weather in the South China Sea. However, pre-war forecasting technology lacked modern radar, satellite imaging, and instantaneous regional communication networks, leaving the colony vulnerable to sudden, rapidly intensifying tropical cyclones.

## What happened
On **September 2, 1937**, an unheralded and exceptionally fierce typhoon struck the Pearl River Delta. Unlike the famous 1906 typhoon, this storm brought a devastating storm surge that rapidly inundated low-lying coastal areas including **Taipo (大埔)**, **Shau Kei Wan (筲箕灣)**, and parts of **Kowloon (九龍)**. Winds of hurricane force battered the harbor, smashing hundreds of junks, sampans, and larger steamships against the shore or sending them to the bottom of the sea. Entire fishing villages were flattened, and countless homes were destroyed by wind and floodwaters.

## Significance
The disaster remains one of the deadliest natural calamities in Hong Kong's history. While contemporary colonial authorities officially recorded around 11,000 fatalities, many historians and local researchers suggest the actual death toll could be significantly higher due to unregistered transient workers, fishermen, and mainland refugees whose deaths went unrecorded. The catastrophe prompted urgent reviews of maritime safety, storm warning systems, and disaster relief protocols within the British administration, leaving a profound mark on the collective memory of the local populace.
