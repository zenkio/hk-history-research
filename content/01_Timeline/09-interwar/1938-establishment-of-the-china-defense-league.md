## Background

Following the outbreak of the Second Sino-Japanese War in **July 1937**, Hong Kong became a vital logistical and political hub for the Chinese mainland. As Japanese forces advanced, controlling major ports and rail lines, the British colony remained relatively neutral territory under British administration. This unique status allowed international sympathizers, overseas Chinese communities, and political activists to use the colony as a safe base for fundraising and organizing relief efforts. Amidst this backdrop of intensifying refugee crises and critical shortages of medical supplies on the front lines, prominent figures sought to establish a dedicated organization to channel aid directly to areas controlled by various Chinese resistance forces.

## What happened

On **June 14, 1938**, **Soong Ching-ling** (宋慶齡)—widow of Sun Yat-sen—officially established the China Defense League (保衛中國同盟) in Hong Kong. The organization was designed to transcend domestic political divisions and focus strictly on humanitarian aid, gathering funds, medical equipment, and pharmaceutical supplies from international donors in countries such as the United States, Great Britain, and various European nations. The League worked closely with international medical missions and local volunteers to ensure these vital resources reached hospitals and frontline units across war-torn China, often navigating complex bureaucratic hurdles imposed by both colonial authorities and wartime conditions.

## Significance

The establishment of the League marked a major milestone in mobilizing global solidarity for China's wartime resistance. By leveraging **Soong Ching-ling**'s immense international prestige and diplomatic network, the organization successfully raised substantial foreign contributions and established a reputation for transparency and efficiency in aid distribution. For Hong Kong, the League's operations demonstrated the colony's capacity to serve as a pivotal node in global anti-fascist networks, though its high-profile activities also tested the delicate neutrality maintained by the British colonial administration until the eventual fall of Hong Kong in **December 1941**.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Colonial authorities viewed the burgeoning political and humanitarian mobilization with caution, balancing the desire to maintain strict neutrality and appease Japanese diplomatic protests with the pragmatic need to manage local stability and international public opinion.
- **Chinese patriotic and leftist networks**: Organizers and supporters regarded the League as an essential lifeline for the resistance, successfully bypassing domestic political schisms to unite international humanitarian efforts behind the broader cause of national defense.
