## Background
Throughout the 1920s and 1930s, tuberculosis (TB) emerged as the leading cause of mortality in Hong Kong. The rapid urbanization of the territory, coupled with an influx of refugees fleeing conflict in mainland China, resulted in extreme overcrowding within the city's tenement buildings (tong lau). Poor ventilation, lack of sanitation, and severe domestic overcrowding created an environment where infectious diseases could thrive. While the colonial medical authorities were aware of the rising death rates, the government's role in public welfare remained limited by a philosophy of 'positive non-interventionism,' which often prioritized fiscal conservatism over large-scale social health infrastructure.

## What happened
In **1938**, the Hong Kong Anti-Tuberculosis Association (**香港防癆會**) was founded. The initiative was driven by a collaboration between colonial health officials and local social elites. The association aimed to consolidate efforts that had previously been fragmented, focusing on public health education, the establishment of sanatoriums, and clinical treatment for the infected population. By creating a formalized non-governmental body, the association sought to bridge the gap between private philanthropy and official medical oversight. The effort reflected an increasing recognition that the TB crisis was a systemic issue rather than a matter of individual hygiene.

## Significance
The foundation of the association is often viewed as a turning point in the professionalization of public health in Hong Kong. It signaled a move away from purely reactive medical measures toward a more structured, long-term preventative strategy. However, the efficacy of the association during its early years is debated. Some scholars argue that the association provided a crucial framework for post-war health expansion, while others contend that its early impact was hampered by limited funding and the impending outbreak of the Pacific War, which disrupted medical services in **1941**. The organization demonstrated the potential for local Chinese elites to play a significant role in colonial administration through charitable governance, marking a transition toward the welfare-oriented policies that would characterize the later colonial period.

## Perspectives

- **Colonial Administration**: The administration viewed the association as a success in civic partnership, demonstrating the government's ability to facilitate community-led health solutions without assuming the full financial burden of healthcare provision.
- **Local Chinese Community**: Many in the Chinese community saw the association as a necessary intervention by local philanthropists to protect the population from neglect, asserting a degree of agency in managing urban life amidst a crisis that the colonial government had been slow to address.
