## Background
For nearly a century, the financial model of British Hong Kong relied almost entirely on indirect taxation, primarily derived from land sales, stamp duties, and monopolies such as opium and alcohol, alongside port and harbor dues. Direct taxation on income or business profits had long been resisted by the local mercantile elite and the colonial administration alike, who argued that a low-tax environment was essential for attracting international trade and maintaining the entrepôt economy.

However, by **1940**, the outbreak of the Second World War in Europe and the escalating conflict in mainland China radically altered the fiscal landscape. The colony was inundated with hundreds of thousands of refugees fleeing the Japanese advance, straining public infrastructure and social services. Furthermore, direct communications and trade routes with the United Kingdom were disrupted, forcing the local administration to seek new and reliable revenue streams to contribute to imperial defense expenditures and local wartime administration.

## What happened
In **1940**, the colonial legislature passed emergency legislation to introduce the War Revenue Ordinance, marking the historic introduction of direct taxation in the form of a war revenue and income tax. This new fiscal measure levied duties on salaries, corporate profits, and interest earnings for the first time in Hong Kong's history. 

The policy met with immediate resistance from powerful business associations and wealthy merchants, who feared it would permanently undermine Hong Kong's competitive advantage as a free port. Despite these protests, the urgent financial demands of wartime governance ensured the measure was enacted.

## Significance
Historians generally view the introduction of the 1940 income tax as a watershed moment in the fiscal history of Hong Kong. While initially framed as a temporary wartime measure—the 'War Revenue'—it laid the structural and administrative groundwork for the formal introduction of permanent direct taxation after the Pacific War. It signaled a permanent shift away from the pure laissez-faire fiscal model of the nineteenth century toward a more interventionist and modern tax apparatus capable of supporting a growing urban population.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Viewed the tax as an unavoidable and patriotic necessity to shoulder the financial burdens of imperial defense and manage the massive humanitarian costs associated with wartime refugees.
- **Local Mercantile Elite**: Strongly opposed the direct tax as a dangerous departure from the traditional free-market principles that made Hong Kong a thriving entrepôt, fearing it would drive capital and commerce away to rival ports.
