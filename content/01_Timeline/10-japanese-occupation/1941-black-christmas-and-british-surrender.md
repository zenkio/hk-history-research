## Background

Following the outbreak of the Pacific War on **December 8, 1941**, Imperial Japanese forces launched an invasion of Hong Kong just hours after attacking Pearl Harbor. Despite British, Canadian, Indian, and local volunteer forces making preparations, the garrison was heavily outnumbered and lacked adequate air and naval support. Japanese forces quickly overran the Gin Drinkers Line in the **New Territories (新界)** within days, forcing the defenders back onto Hong Kong Island.

## What happened

As water supplies dwindled and military positions collapsed under intense artillery bombardment and aerial attacks, the defense of the island became untenable. On **December 25, 1941**—a day that would become locally known as "Black Christmas"—Governor **Sir Mark Young (楊慕琦)** and General **Christopher Maltby** decided to surrender. They walked across the harbor from **Hong Kong Island (香港島)** to the Japanese headquarters at the **Peninsula Hotel (半島酒店)** in **Kowloon (九龍)** to formally sign the unconditional surrender document, ending the 18-day Battle of Hong Kong.

## Significance

The surrender marked the end of British colonial rule and the beginning of the period known locally as the "three years and eight months" (三年零八個月) of Japanese occupation. This military defeat shattered the myth of European invincibility in Asia, profoundly altering the geopolitical landscape of the region. The subsequent occupation brought immense hardship, severe food shortages, hyperinflation, and violence to the local population, leaving a lasting scar on the collective memory of Hong Kong.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Perspective**: Viewed the defense as a tragic but inevitable defeat against overwhelming odds, emphasizing the bravery of the Commonwealth garrison and the grim necessity of surrender to prevent further civilian slaughter.
- **Local Hong Kong Perspective**: Remembered primarily as 'Black Christmas,' marking the abrupt collapse of daily life, the onset of severe deprivation, and the beginning of a harsh occupation that tested the resilience of the indigenous and urban populations alike.
