## Background
On **1941-12-25**, the British colonial administration surrendered Hong Kong to the Japanese military, beginning the period known locally as the "Three Years and Eight Months" (三年零八個月). To consolidate control, the Japanese occupation authorities recognized that limiting access to outside information was crucial. Radio broadcasts from the BBC and other Allied networks posed a direct threat to their authority and military security.

## What happened
On **1941-12-28**, the Japanese military administration issued stringent orders requiring all residents to surrender their private radio receivers. Possessing a radio or listening to unauthorized foreign broadcasts became a capital offense. Military police, known as the **Kempeitai** (憲兵隊), conducted house-to-house searches to confiscate hidden devices. Despite the severe penalties, many residents attempted to conceal crystal sets or listen in secret, relying on whispered intelligence networks to learn about the progress of the Pacific War.

## Significance
The radio ban marked the beginning of total information isolation for Hong Kong residents. By severing ties with external news sources, the occupation government sought to enforce compliance and spread official propaganda through controlled media channels like the **Hong Kong News** (香港新聞). Later scholarship emphasizes that this policy fundamentally altered daily life, turning ordinary information-gathering into a dangerous act of resistance.
