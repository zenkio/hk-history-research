## Background

By **December 1941**, tensions between the British Empire and the Empire of Japan had reached a critical threshold. Following the outbreak of the Second Sino-Japanese War in 1937, Hong Kong had functioned as a vital conduit for international aid and supplies into China. While British military strategists recognized that the colony was strategically indefensible in the long term against a concerted Japanese onslaught, a political decision was made to reinforce the garrison to deter aggression and honor imperial commitments.

## What happened

On the morning of **December 8, 1941** (local time, mirroring the date of the attack on Pearl Harbor across the International Date Line), the **Imperial Japanese Army** launched an invasion across the Sham Chun River (深圳河) from occupied Guangdong into the New Territories. Simultaneously, Japanese naval aviation bombed Kai Tak Airport (啟德機場), destroying most of the aircraft belonging to the Royal Air Force. The defenders, comprising British, Canadian, Indian, and local volunteer units, executed pre-planned demolitions and fell back to the Gin Drinkers Line (醉酒灣防線) to mount a protracted delaying action.

## Significance

The commencement of hostilities on **December 8, 1941** marked the end of Hong Kong's relative isolation from the Pacific War and began a brutal campaign that would culminate in the British surrender on Christmas Day, initiating three and a half years of Japanese occupation. The battle shattered the perceived invulnerability of Western colonial powers in Asia and fundamentally altered the social and political trajectory of the territory.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration and military command**: Viewed the defense of Hong Kong as an unavoidable imperial duty intended to delay the Japanese advance, despite knowing the garrison was heavily outnumbered and isolated without naval or air support.
- **Local Hong Kong and New Territories communities**: Experienced the sudden onset of total war, aerial bombardment, and the immediate breakdown of civil order, plunging civilians into severe hardship and uncertainty regarding their survival and future allegiances.
- **Mainland Chinese and Nationalist perspectives**: Regarded the defense as part of the broader, protracted war of resistance against Japanese aggression, though coordination between British and Chinese forces during the brief campaign remained limited and fraught with mutual distrust.
