## Background

During the Battle of Hong Kong in **December 1941**, St. Stephen's College in Stanley (赤柱) was designated as an emergency military hospital to treat the mounting casualties of the British and Allied forces. As Imperial Japanese troops advanced toward the Stanley peninsula in the final hours of the battle, communications broke down, and the facility was overrun. 

## What happened

On **December 25, 1941**—a date remembered in Hong Kong history as "Black Christmas"—Japanese soldiers entered the college buildings. Despite the premises clearly functioning as a medical facility with wounded personnel and medical staff, soldiers bayoneted and killed dozens of wounded British, Canadian, and Indian soldiers, as well as medical staff and nurses. The precise number of victims remains difficult to establish definitively due to the chaos of the defeat and subsequent burials, but contemporary estimates and postwar investigations confirm a major loss of life. Surviving witnesses and medical personnel later documented the harrowing hours of the attack, which stands as one of the darkest incidents of the Japanese occupation.

## Significance

The massacre at St. Stephen's College became a powerful symbol of the brutality of the Japanese occupation of Hong Kong (香港日佔時期), which lasted for three years and eight months. For the British colonial administration and Allied governments, the event underscored the harsh treatment of prisoners of war and non-combatants in violation of international conventions. In local memory, it remains a poignant trauma associated with the fall of the colony, influencing subsequent historiography regarding wartime atrocities in the Pacific theater.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial and Allied Perspective**: Wartime reports and postwar investigations categorized the event as a deliberate and unjustified war crime, highlighting the slaughter of defenseless wounded soldiers and medical personnel in violation of the Geneva Convention.
- **Japanese Military and Postwar Accounts**: While post-war tribunals and historical records acknowledged the killings, Japanese accounts often struggled to reconcile the chaos of urban combat, poor command control, and the desperate conditions of the final assault, sometimes attributing the violence to frontline units acting in the heat of battle without direct higher-level orders.
