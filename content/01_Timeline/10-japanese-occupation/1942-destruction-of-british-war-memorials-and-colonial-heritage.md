## Background
Following the surrender of the British garrison on **December 25, 1941**, the Japanese military administration sought to thoroughly eradicate Western influences from the urban landscape of Hong Kong. Public symbols, war memorials, and statues dedicated to British monarchs, governors, and military figures stood as prominent reminders of European hegemony. Japanese authorities viewed the removal of these monuments as essential for establishing their New Order in East Asia and promoting the Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere.

## What happened
Between **1942 and 1943**, numerous British colonial monuments, bronze statues, and war memorials were systematically pulled down. Notable casualties of this campaign included the statue of **Queen Victoria** located in Statue Square (皇后像廣場), which was dismantled alongside other European civic figures. Many of these bronze and metal structures were shipped to Japan or utilized locally to meet wartime metal shortages, being melted down for industrial and military manufacturing. Street names and placenames carrying overt British or Western colonial connotations were also progressively altered to reflect Japanese terminology.

## Significance
The systematic destruction of colonial heritage marked a profound physical rupture in the urban identity of Hong Kong. By erasing these visible markers of British sovereignty, the occupation authorities attempted to psychological re-engineer the territory's populace, replacing British imperial supremacy with Japanese imperial dominance. Surviving artifacts and the eventual postwar restoration of some recovered monuments became focal points for later historical memory regarding the occupation era.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Viewed the destruction as an act of calculated cultural vandalism and desecration of war memorials, demonstrating the ruthless disregard of the occupying forces for established civic heritage and historical memory.
- **Japanese military administration**: Framed the removal of colonial symbols as a necessary liberation of Asian territory from Western imperialism, mobilizing valuable metal resources to support the wartime economy and the Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere.
