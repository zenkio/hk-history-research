## Background

Following the fall of Hong Kong to the Empire of Japan in **December 1941**, the new colonial administration faced severe shortages of raw materials, particularly metals required for wartime manufacturing. To sustain the Japanese war effort, the military government initiated comprehensive scrap metal collection campaigns across the territory. Prominent British colonial symbols and public monuments, erected during decades of British rule, were identified as prime sources of high-grade bronze.

## What happened

Beginning around **August 1942**, authorities systematically removed several iconic bronze sculptures from public spaces. Among the most notable removals were the statue of **Queen Victoria** situated in Statue Square (遮打花園/皇后像廣場) and the famous bronze lions guarding the headquarters of The Hongkong and Shanghai Banking Corporation (香港上海滙豐銀行). These artifacts were dismantled, loaded onto ships, and transported to Japan to be smelted into munitions and military equipment. Fortunately, not all statues shipped to Japan were actually melted down before the end of the war; certain pieces survived and were later recovered and repatriated to Hong Kong.

## Significance

The removal of the royal and commercial monuments represented a deliberate attempt by the Japanese administration to erase British imperial prestige and reshape the urban landscape of Hong Kong to reflect Japanese imperial hegemony. For the local population, the disappearance of familiar landmarks underscored the total disruption of pre-war colonial life under an oppressive military regime. In the post-war period, the eventual recovery and restoration of surviving statues became symbolic of the colony's return to British administration, though interpretations of these symbols have continued to evolve in contemporary times.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Perspective**: Viewed the destruction and removal of royal monuments as an act of cultural vandalism and an illegitimate assault on British imperial heritage during an illegal occupation.
- **Japanese Wartime Administration**: Justified the confiscation strictly as a military necessity to secure essential metal resources for the empire's war production needs, aligning with total mobilization policies.
- **Local Hong Kong Community**: Experienced the loss of familiar city landmarks as a profound psychological marker of defeat and foreign subjugation, though survival of items like the HSBC lions later provided a sense of post-war continuity.
