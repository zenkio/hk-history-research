## Background
Following the British surrender on **December 25, 1941**, Hong Kong entered the period of Japanese occupation, commonly remembered as the "Three Years and Eight Months" (三年零八個月). The immediate aftermath left the territory facing severe food shortages, widespread displacement, and economic collapse. To maintain public order and manage the destitute population without deploying excessive military resources, the Japanese military administration sought to co-opt prominent local Chinese leaders who had previously operated under the British colonial system.

## What happened
In **March 1942**, the occupation authorities compelled local elites to establish the Hok Aik Hui Welfare Association (協益會). This organization was designed to function as an intermediary body between the Japanese administration and the local Chinese populace. Its primary responsibilities included distributing meager food rations, organizing relief efforts, and executing community-level directives issued by the military government. While nominally a welfare organization, membership and participation placed local leaders in a deeply compromised position, requiring them to balance civilian survival against the oppressive demands of the occupation authorities.

## Significance
The establishment of the Hok Aik Hui marked a crucial step in the Japanese strategy of indirect rule through local proxies, known as "using Chinese to rule Chinese" (以華制華). It highlighted the precarious predicament of Hong Kong's elite class during wartime, as they navigated coercion, survival, and collaboration. The administrative structures developed during this period also laid groundwork that later influenced postwar social welfare and urban administration in the colony.

## Perspectives

- **Local Hong Kong and New Territories communities**: Many residents viewed the organization with ambivalence, recognizing that while it provided vital, scarce relief supplies, it was ultimately an instrument of Japanese wartime mobilization and surveillance.
- **Later scholarship**: Modern historians analyze the Hok Aik Hui as a prime example of the complex grey zones of wartime collaboration, emphasizing that survival often required local elites to negotiate and cooperate with an aggressive occupying power.
