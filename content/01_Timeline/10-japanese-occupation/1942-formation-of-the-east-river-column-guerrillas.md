## Background

Following the surrender of the British garrison to the Japanese on **December 25, 1941**, known locally as the "Three Years and Eight Months" (三年零八個月) period of occupation, the rural areas of the New Territories and neighboring Guangdong province fell into a security vacuum. Remnants of Chinese regular forces, local villagers, and Communist-affiliated fighters began organizing armed resistance against the Imperial Japanese Army. 

## What happened

In early **February 1942**, various Communist-led anti-Japanese guerrilla units operating in the region underwent reorganization to form a more cohesive military structure. This ultimately coalesced into the Guangdong People's Anti-Japanese Guerrilla Force, widely known as the **East River Column** (東江縱隊). Operating from bases in the rugged terrain of the New Territories and southern Guangdong, the guerrillas ambushed Japanese patrols, punished collaborators, and established crucial underground escape routes.

One of the most notable operations associated with the column was the rescue of trapped British, Chinese, and other Allied personnel, including airmen downed during the Doolittle Raid and escaping prisoners of war from Hong Kong camps. Figures such as **Zeng Sheng** (會生) played pivotal leadership roles in coordinating these underground networks alongside local villagers.

## Significance

The East River Column emerged as one of the primary indigenous armed resistance groups against Japanese occupation in the region. While operating under severe constraints, their intelligence-sharing operations linked local resistance efforts with the broader Allied war strategy in the Pacific theater, though the exact extent and military impact of their engagements remain subjects of varying historical interpretation.

## Perspectives

- **Later scholarship and local communities**: Modern historians and oral history projects emphasize the vital role played by indigenous villagers and Hakka communities in the New Territories, who provided food, shelter, and intelligence to the guerrillas despite facing brutal Japanese reprisals.
- **Official historiography (Beijing and British colonial)**: Mainland Chinese historiography heavily highlights the Communist Party's leadership in spearheading the anti-Japanese national salvation movement, whereas traditional British colonial accounts often downplayed Communist contributions, focusing instead on clandestine British-run operations like the British Army Aid Group.
