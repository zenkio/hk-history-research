## Background
Following the British surrender of Hong Kong on **December 25, 1941**, the Japanese military administration sought to integrate the territory into the Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere. To secure ideological control and sever British colonial ties, the administration under Governor **Rensuke Isogai** (磯谷廉介) planned systemic measures to reshape the social and cultural fabric of the local population.

## What happened
The Cultural Japanization Campaign manifested across multiple facets of daily life. Japanese language education was made compulsory in remaining schools, while the study of English was heavily restricted or banned. Major streets and landmarks throughout Victoria City and Kowloon were renamed with Japanese designations; for example, Queen's Road was renamed Meiji-dori (明治通). Public holidays were altered to align with the Japanese imperial calendar, replacing British celebrations with festivals such as Tencho-setsu (Emperor's Birthday). Additionally, currency was forcibly converted to military yen, and public media were subjected to strict censorship to promote Japanese culture and wartime propaganda.

## Significance
The campaign fundamentally disrupted the pre-war social order of Hong Kong, though its long-term effectiveness remains a subject of historical debate. While it aimed to create loyal imperial subjects, severe food shortages, economic collapse, and coercive enforcement largely alienated the local populace. The rapid reversal of these measures following the Japanese surrender in **August 1945** highlighted the shallow penetration of forced assimilation under wartime duress.

## Perspectives

- **Japanese Military Administration**: Viewed the campaign as a benevolent and necessary civilizing mission to liberate Asians from Western imperialism and incorporate them into a harmonious cultural sphere.
- **Local Hong Kong Residents**: Experienced the campaign as oppressive coercion that disrupted daily life, imposed foreign rituals, and sought to erase local identity amid severe wartime suffering.
