## Background
Following the surrender of the British garrison in **December 1941**, the Japanese military administration sought to integrate Hong Kong's monetary system into the Japanese Empire's Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere. Initially, both the Hong Kong Dollar and Japanese military yen (軍票) circulated concurrently, but the colonial currency posed a challenge to Japanese economic control and resource extraction.

## What happened
On **July 24, 1942**, the Japanese military administration officially banned the use and possession of the Hong Kong Dollar. Residents were ordered to exchange their Hong Kong dollars for military yen at an artificially fixed, highly unfavorable rate. Because these military yen were entirely unbacked by gold or economic reserves and could be printed at will by the occupation authorities, the measure effectively wiped out the life savings of ordinary citizens, merchants, and institutions. As the war progressed and Japanese military fortunes declined, hyperinflation set in, rendering the military yen virtually worthless by the end of the occupation in **August 1945**.

## Significance
The forced currency conversion remains one of the most economically devastating episodes of the Japanese occupation, often referred to locally as the period of the "three-year-and-eight-month" hardship. Post-war compensation for holders of defunct military yen became a complex legal and diplomatic issue that persisted for decades between the British government, Japan, and local claimants, with most receiving little to no redress.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial perspective**: Viewed the forced currency conversion and subsequent hyperinflation as an illegal expropriation of wealth and a violation of international norms regarding occupied territories.
- **Local Hong Kong residents' perspective**: Experienced the policy as a catastrophic humanitarian and financial disaster that caused widespread starvation, poverty, and the total loss of generational savings.
