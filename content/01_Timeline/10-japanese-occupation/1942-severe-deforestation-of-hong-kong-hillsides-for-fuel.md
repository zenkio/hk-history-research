## Background

Following the surrender of the British garrison in **December 1941** (香港保衛戰), Hong Kong fell under the administration of the Japanese military government. As the Pacific War progressed, Allied blockades and shipping disruptions severely cut off normal supplies of imported fuel, particularly coal and oil, which were desperately needed for both military logistics and civilian survival.

## What happened

To combat the extreme energy crisis, the occupying authorities and desperate local residents turned to the territory's forested hillsides for alternative fuel. Between **1942** and **1945**, large-scale and often indiscriminate tree-felling took place across Hong Kong Island and the New Territories (新界). Mature woodlands, secondary forests, and plantation trees were systematically chopped down to supply firewood for domestic cooking, bakeries, and industrial use. Hills that had been greened by decades of colonial afforestation efforts were rapidly denuded.

The large-scale removal of vegetation destroyed root systems that held the topsoil in place. Consequently, the heavy tropical monsoon rains of **1942–1945** triggered severe soil erosion, landslides, and widespread degradation of the local ecosystem, permanently altering the natural topography of many areas.

## Significance

The environmental devastation of the occupation years created a legacy that took decades to reverse. Post-war British administrations faced a barren landscape prone to erosion and flooding, prompting major reforestation and water conservation initiatives in the late **1940s** and **1950s**. The denudation of the hills remains one of the most visible ecological scars of the wartime period.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration (Post-War)**: Post-war British officials and forestry officers viewed the widespread deforestation as a tragic ecological disaster caused by reckless wartime exploitation, necessitating urgent and costly afforestation programs to restore the colony's water catchment areas.
- **Local Hong Kong Residents**: For the local population struggling to survive under harsh occupation conditions, felling trees was not an act of vandalism but an absolute necessity for daily survival, cooking, and warmth amid severe rationing and economic collapse.
