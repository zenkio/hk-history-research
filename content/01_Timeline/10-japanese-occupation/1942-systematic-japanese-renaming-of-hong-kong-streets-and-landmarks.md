## Background
Following the surrender of the British garrison in **December 1941**, Hong Kong entered the period of Japanese occupation. The new military administration, led by governors such as **Rensuke Isogai** (**磯谷廉介**), sought to fundamentally transform the territory into a thoroughly Japanese-administered zone. Erasing British colonial markers was deemed a crucial step in achieving the Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere's ideological goals, signaling the permanent end of British sovereignty.

## What happened
In **April 1942**, the administration officially announced the systematic renaming of major streets, buildings, and geographical landmarks across Hong Kong Island and Kowloon. Western-derived names commemorating British royalty, governors, and naval figures were supplanted by Japanese names, often reflecting Japanese geography, historical figures, or auspicious themes. Prominent examples included renaming Queen's Road to **Meiji-dori** (**明治通**), Des Voeux Road to **Showa-dori** (**昭和通**), and Victoria Peak to **Kasugayama** (**春日山**). Public notices, maps, and official documents were updated to reflect these changes, and residents were expected to use the new nomenclature in daily life and postal correspondence.

## Significance
The renaming campaign served as a powerful tool of psychological warfare and cultural assimilation. By altering the physical and linguistic landscape, the occupiers aimed to sever the psychological ties between the local population and the former British administration. While the changes were strictly enforced during the occupation years, they proved temporary; following the Japanese surrender in **August 1945**, the restored British colonial government promptly reinstated the original English street names and landmarks, leaving the wartime Japanese designations as a brief, disruptive episode in the territory's urban history.

## Perspectives

- **Japanese Military Administration**: Viewed the renaming as a necessary administrative and ideological measure to purge British imperial influence, incorporate Hong Kong into the Japanese Empire, and reorient the territory culturally and geographically.
- **Local Hong Kong Residents**: Generally experienced the renaming as an unwelcome imposition, often continuing to use traditional or colloquial Chinese names in private while reluctantly complying with official Japanese designations out of survival.
- **Post-War British Colonial Government**: Interpreted the wartime renaming as an illegitimate attempt to alter colonial geography, moving swiftly to restore all original British place names immediately upon the resumption of control in 1945.
