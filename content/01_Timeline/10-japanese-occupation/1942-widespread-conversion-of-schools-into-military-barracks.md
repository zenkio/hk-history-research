## Background
Following the fall of Hong Kong to the Japanese forces in **December 1941**, the new military administration faced the immediate need to house troops, store munitions, and establish command centers across the territory. Major educational institutions, possessing substantial infrastructure, large compounds, and central locations, were quickly identified by the Japanese military as prime candidates for requisitioning. Before the war, schools such as **King's College** (英皇書院) and **Diocesan Boys' School** (拔萃男書院) served as pillars of the British colonial education system, catering primarily to the elite and middle classes.

## What happened
Throughout **1942**, the Japanese military authorities systematically seized dozens of schools across Hong Kong Island, Kowloon, and the New Territories. Prominent campuses were converted into military barracks, hospitals, supply depots, and stables. Classrooms, libraries, and assembly halls were gutted or modified to accommodate soldiers, horses, and military equipment. Educational activities were officially suspended or severely restricted, halting the formal schooling of an entire generation of children. Many teachers and students were forced to flee, seek alternative employment, or participate in clandestine, informal tutoring groups often referred to as refugee schools.

## Significance
The conversion of schools into military installations marked a devastating blow to Hong Kong's social fabric and educational continuity. The physical infrastructure of learning was severely degraded, with many buildings suffering extensive damage, looting, and structural neglect by the time the occupation ended in **1945**. This disruption created a massive educational vacuum, leaving thousands of youths without formal instruction for nearly four years and presenting immense challenges for post-war reconstruction and the eventual revival of the school system.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Perspective**: British authorities and subsequent colonial historiography viewed the requisitioning of schools as a blatant act of cultural vandalism and a violation of the welfare of local youth, highlighting the destructive nature of the Japanese military administration.
- **Local Hong Kong and New Territories Communities**: For local families and displaced residents, the closure of schools meant immediate hardship, forcing children into labor or leaving them idle while struggling for daily survival under severe wartime food shortages and rationing.
