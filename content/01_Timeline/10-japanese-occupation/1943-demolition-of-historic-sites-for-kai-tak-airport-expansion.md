## Background

Following the British surrender of Hong Kong on **December 25, 1941**, the Japanese military administration sought to upgrade local infrastructure to support its wartime operations. Kai Tak Airport (啟德機場), originally established as a civilian and military airfield in the 1920s, was deemed inadequate for heavy bomber operations and military transport. To accommodate larger aircraft and expand runways, the occupying forces targeted adjacent areas, which unfortunately encompassed several centuries-old heritage sites rich in local and imperial history.

## What happened

Beginning around **January 1943**, forced Chinese labor crews, operating under harsh conditions and military coercion, were mobilized to dismantle structural and geographical landmarks. The granite blocks from the historic fortifications of the Kowloon Walled City (九龍城寨) were systematically dismantled and repurposed as paving materials for the airport's expanded runway network. Concurrently, engineers targeted the revered Sung Wong Toi (宋王台) rock, an ancient memorial boulder associated with the final boy-emperors of the Song dynasty. Large portions of the rock were blasted and quarried to clear landing paths and source aggregate for construction.

## Significance

The destruction of these sites represented a profound loss of tangible heritage for the local population. While wartime military necessity drove the Imperial Japanese forces to prioritize operational infrastructure over historical preservation, the destruction severely impacted local cultural memory. The episode remains a stark illustration of the collateral damage inflicted on Hong Kong's historical fabric during the **1941-1945** occupation period, permanently altering the landscape of Kowloon and erasing physical links to both Qing dynasty administration and medieval Song history.

## Perspectives

- **Japanese Military Administration**: Viewed the expansion of Kai Tak Airport as an urgent military necessity for the defense and logistical support of the empire in the Pacific War, superseding local heritage preservation.
- **Local Hong Kong Residents**: Regarded the demolition of landmarks like Sung Wong Toi and the Walled City as a culturally devastating act of vandalism that erased sacred historical touchstones during an already oppressive occupation.
