## Background
During the Japanese occupation of Hong Kong (**1941–1945**), the territory faced acute shortages of imported rice, which had traditionally formed the foundation of the local diet. As maritime trade routes were disrupted by Allied actions and military demands, the colonial administration sought alternative calorie sources to sustain the urban population and reduce reliance on scarce grain supplies. Authorities turned to promoting alternative staples such as soybeans (大豆) and sweet potatoes (番薯) to replace rice.

## What happened
In **May 1943**, the administration intensified efforts to alter local dietary habits by introducing Japanese ingredients and cooking methods to residents. Public cooking classes and demonstrations were organized in various districts, including urban community centers, to teach homemakers how to prepare unfamiliar foods. Instructors demonstrated recipes utilizing soybeans, sweet potatoes, and other available substitutes, aiming to make these nutritional alternatives more palatable to the Cantonese palate. Rationing systems and propaganda outlets were simultaneously used to encourage acceptance of the dietary shift.

## Significance
The promotion of Japanese cuisine and cooking techniques highlights the intersection of wartime resource scarcity and cultural policy during the occupation. While framed by authorities as a practical measure to combat starvation and ensure food security, the initiative was also perceived by residents as part of a broader, coercive push toward Japanization. The long-term impact on local culinary traditions was limited, as post-war resumption of traditional food supplies led most residents to quickly return to rice-based diets.

## Perspectives

- **Japanese Occupation Authorities**: Viewed the promotion of alternative staples and cooking classes as a benevolent and necessary public welfare measure to address severe food shortages and prevent widespread starvation.
- **Local Hong Kong Residents**: Generally regarded the forced dietary changes and unfamiliar ingredients with suspicion and hardship, viewing them as unwelcome impositions by the occupation regime.
