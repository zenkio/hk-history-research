## Background
Following the surrender of British forces on December 25, **1941**, Hong Kong entered a period of Japanese military administration. The Japanese occupation authorities viewed the erasure of British colonial influence as a necessary step for integrating Hong Kong into the **Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere** (大東亞共榮圈). By **1943**, the military government intensified efforts to restructure local society, moving beyond initial stabilization to active cultural and linguistic indoctrination.

## What happened
In **1943**, the occupation administration took formal steps to restrict the use of English in public life. The military government ordered the removal of English-language signage and mandated that government offices, public utilities, and private businesses conduct all official correspondence and record-keeping in either Japanese or Chinese. Use of the English language in public areas, including shops and transport hubs, was discouraged or prohibited, with severe penalties for those who defied the directives. Simultaneously, the curriculum in remaining schools was overhauled to prioritize the Japanese language, with the aim of replacing English as the primary medium of instruction and commerce.

## Significance
The linguistic shift was intended to facilitate complete administrative control and ensure the efficacy of the new regime's mandates. However, the success of these measures remains a subject of historical debate. While documentation exists concerning the issuance of these orders, the extent to which they were strictly enforced across all socioeconomic strata remains uncertain. Scholars often note that while formal colonial signage was successfully removed, the daily linguistic habits of the general populace were difficult to suppress entirely, leading to a complex dynamic between official mandates and private practice.

## Perspectives

- **Japanese Occupation Authorities**: The administration viewed the suppression of English as an essential act of decolonization, necessary to integrate Hong Kong into a new regional order centered on Tokyo. They believed that removing the linguistic markers of British rule would accelerate the local acceptance of Japanese cultural and political hegemony.
- **Hong Kong Residents**: For the local population, the ban created significant disruption to trade, education, and daily navigation. Many residents viewed these linguistic restrictions as an existential threat to their established professional and social lives, often employing subtle, non-cooperative resistance to maintain existing communication norms.
