## Background
During the Japanese occupation of Hong Kong (**1941**–**1945**), the urban population faced catastrophic shortages of food, fuel, and imported medicine. The colonial health infrastructure was dismantled or repurposed to serve the Japanese military, leaving the remaining local residents with virtually no institutional medical support. Severe rice shortages and the distribution of poor-quality or adulterated food substitutes severely compromised public health.

## What happened
By **March 1944**, malnutrition was ubiquitous, leading to widespread epidemics of beriberi (腳氣病), a disease caused by Vitamin B1 deficiency that severely affected the nervous and cardiovascular systems. Simultaneously, the breakdown of clean water supplies, sewage disposal, and urban sanitation created ideal conditions for a major cholera (霍亂) outbreak. Hospitals overflowed, and with a critical lack of vaccines, antibiotics, and trained medical personnel, mortality rates soared. Thousands of residents succumbed to these preventable diseases, and mass burials became a daily necessity.

## Significance
The health crisis of **1944** underscored the devastating human cost of the occupation and the total failure of the military administration to sustain civilian life. The epidemics permanently scarred the collective memory of the local population, reinforcing the darkest perceptions of the wartime period, known locally as the 'Three Years and Eight Months' (三年零八個月).

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial / Post-War Perspective**: Post-war British authorities and historians emphasized the health catastrophe as a direct consequence of Japanese military mismanagement, forced food exports, and total neglect of civilian welfare obligations under international law.
- **Local Hong Kong Community Perspective**: For the local residents who survived the period, the epidemics represented a desperate daily struggle against starvation and disease, where traditional herbal remedies and community solidarity often provided the only defense against institutional collapse.
