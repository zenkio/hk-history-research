## Background
By **July 1944**, the Japanese military administration in Hong Kong faced a critical shortage of food, fuel, and essential supplies. This crisis was largely driven by Allied naval blockades, the disruption of traditional trade routes, and the prioritization of military logistics. To reduce the civilian population consuming scarce resources in the urban core, the Japanese authorities revived and expanded earlier schemes to drive residents out of the city and back to the Chinese mainland.

## What happened
Thousands of urban residents were systematically rounded up or coerced into leaving the urban areas of Hong Kong and Kowloon. These individuals were loaded onto crowded coastal vessels operating routes to various ports in Guangdong (廣東) province. Conditions aboard these repatriation vessels were notoriously poor, characterized by severe overcrowding, lack of sanitation, and inadequate food and water. Many deportees suffered from disease, malnutrition, and abuse during the transit process. Upon arrival in mainland territory controlled either by the Japanese or contested by various military factions, the returnees faced immense hardship, often having to make their own way inland with virtually no personal belongings or financial resources.

## Significance
This operation starkly illustrates the brutal realities of the three years and eight months of Japanese occupation. It reflects the colonial administration's complete disregard for civilian welfare in the pursuit of military and logistical sustainability. The forced displacement fractured families and permanently altered the demographic makeup of Hong Kong, leaving a legacy of trauma that persisted long after the war ended in **1945**.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial and postwar perspective**: Postwar British and Western accounts framed the forced repatriation as a cruel and desperate measure that demonstrated the callousness of the Japanese military administration and its failure to uphold basic responsibilities toward the occupied civilian population.
- **Japanese military administration perspective**: Wartime administrative records and rationales presented the population reduction measures as a necessary logistical step to prevent widespread starvation in a besieged urban center where food imports had become virtually impossible to maintain.
- **Mainland and local historical perspective**: Chinese historical narratives and local oral histories emphasize the immense suffering endured by ordinary citizens who were treated as disposable surplus population, while highlighting the resilience of those who survived the perilous journeys to Guangdong.
