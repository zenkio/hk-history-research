## Background

By **1944**, the Pacific War was turning decisively against the Japanese Empire. Allied blockades, successful submarine warfare, and the disruption of maritime shipping routes severely choked the supply of imported coal, oil, and other conventional fuels to occupied Hong Kong (香港). As conventional energy sources dwindled and disappeared entirely, the urban population faced immense difficulties in cooking food and heating water, forcing both civilian residents and the occupation authorities to look toward alternative energy supplies.

## What happened

With municipal supplies exhausted, the hillsides of Hong Kong Island and the New Territories (新界) became the primary source of accessible fuel. Widespread, unregulated illegal logging and wood-cutting stripped the landscape bare. Trees were systematically felled, roots were dug up, and vegetation was stripped across country parks, water catchment areas, and urban peripheries alike. The Japanese military administration (香港佔領地政府) instituted various rationing and control measures, but the desperate need for survival among the local populace—compounded by institutional looting of natural resources—rendered conservation efforts ineffective. By the time the British resumed control in **August 1945**, the once-lush green hills characteristic of the territory had been transformed into barren, eroded slopes.

## Significance

The catastrophic loss of forest cover triggered severe secondary environmental crises, most notably massive soil erosion, landslides during the heavy monsoon rains, and the degradation of watersheds critical for local water reservoirs. Post-war colonial administrations faced a monumental task in ecological restoration, initiating large-scale reforestation programs in the late **1940s** and **1950s** that eventually replanted the denuded hills with hardy species such as pine and acacia. Culturally and historically, the barren landscape became a defining visual memory of the deprivation and suffering experienced by residents during the period known locally as the "Three Years and Eight Months" (三年零八個月).

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration and post-war conservationists**: Viewed the widespread deforestation as an ecological catastrophe and a direct consequence of the breakdown of orderly governance and resource management under enemy occupation, necessitating decades of arduous replanting.
- **Local Hong Kong and New Territories residents**: Experienced the cutting of firewood not merely as environmental destruction, but as a desperate, daily struggle for basic survival and sustenance amidst extreme wartime starvation and cold.
