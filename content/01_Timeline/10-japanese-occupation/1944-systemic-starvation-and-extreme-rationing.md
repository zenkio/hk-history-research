## Background
By **1944**, the Pacific War had turned decisively against the Japanese Empire. Allied submarine blockades and intense bombing campaigns severely disrupted maritime shipping routes connecting Hong Kong to Southeast Asian rice-producing regions. Consequently, the colonial infrastructure and the wartime administration under the Japanese military governor were entirely unable to sustain the territory's pre-war population levels, which had already been heavily altered by forced deportations of residents to mainland China (清鄉).

## What happened
The administration implemented an increasingly strict rationing system for essential commodities such as rice, flour, and oil. However, the official rations provided through designated distribution shops quickly became inadequate to sustain human life, often consisting of adulterated flour or meager quantities of substitute grains like sweet potatoes and tapioca. As official supplies dwindled, rampant inflation and currency devaluation—accelerated by the circulation of military yen (軍票)—rendered legal markets largely dysfunctional. This drove the population into the black market, where exorbitant prices prevailed.

Famine conditions intensified throughout **1944** and peaked in **1945**. Widespread malnutrition, edema, and deficiency diseases such as beriberi decimated the urban populace. Contemporary observations and later historical accounts note that corpses were regularly collected from the streets of Victoria City and Kowloon each morning by charitable organizations and municipal workers. To cope with the mortality crisis, mass graves were utilized, and many destitute residents resorted to consuming roots, bark, and even sawdust.

## Significance
The period of systemic starvation remains one of the darkest chapters in Hong Kong's modern history. It fundamentally transformed the social fabric of the territory, decimating families and leaving deep psychological scars on the surviving population. The failure of the occupation authorities to feed the populace severely delegitimized the Japanese military administration, paving the way for the relatively smooth, albeit chaotic, resumption of British colonial rule in August **1945**.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Historiography**: Traditional British accounts emphasize the cruelty and incompetence of the Japanese military administration, framing the famine as a direct result of callous enemy occupation and the inevitable consequence of totalitarian resource exploitation.
- **Local Hong Kong and New Territories Oral History**: Local recollections focus heavily on grassroots survival strategies, mutual aid networks within urban neighborhoods and rural villages, and the heavy reliance on relatives in the New Territories or Guangdong to smuggle minimal amounts of food.
- **Later Socio-Economic Scholarship**: Modern historians analyze the catastrophe through the lens of global wartime logistics, maritime blockades, and the systemic vulnerability of import-dependent colonial economies during total war.
