## Background
During the Japanese occupation of Hong Kong (香港) from **1941** to **1945**, hundreds of thousands of residents fled to mainland China or were forcibly repatriated to escape severe food shortages, forced labor, and political oppression. Families were frequently torn apart amidst the chaos of conflict, evacuation orders, and the immediate aftermath of the Japanese surrender in **September 1945**. Upon the re-establishment of civil and military administration, the restoration of family networks became an urgent humanitarian priority.

## What happened
In **September 1945**, the provisional authorities and voluntary organizations set up registration centers and inquiry offices across urban areas, including Victoria (維多利亞城) and Kowloon (九龍). These centers compiled lists of survivors, missing persons, and returning refugees. Radio broadcasts, public notice boards, and newspaper registries were utilized to help displaced individuals locate scattered relatives. Many rural communities in the New Territories (新界) also absorbed returning kin, though formal administrative reach into outlying areas varied.

## Significance
The post-liberation reunification efforts represented a critical step in the demographic and social recovery of Hong Kong after the Pacific War. While traditional historiography often emphasizes the high-level political and military transition from Japanese military rule to British colonial restoration, local social history highlights the grassroots resilience of families rebuilding their lives amidst severe infrastructural devastation. These emergency measures laid the groundwork for the more permanent social welfare systems that emerged in the subsequent decades.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Emphasized the rapid establishment of orderly bureaucratic infrastructure, registration systems, and humanitarian relief as proof of efficient governance and the successful restoration of public order.
- **Local Hong Kong residents**: Focused heavily on personal trauma, the immense difficulties of physical survival, and the vital role of grassroots mutual aid networks in locating lost family members when official resources were stretched thin.
