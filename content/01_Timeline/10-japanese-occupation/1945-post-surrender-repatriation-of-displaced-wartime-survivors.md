## Background
During the Japanese occupation (**1941**–**1945**), Hong Kong’s population plummeted from over 1.6 million before the war to approximately 600,000 by **1945**, driven by forced deportations, starvation, and disease. As the British Pacific Fleet accepted the Japanese surrender in **September 1945**, hundreds of thousands of former residents who had fled to mainland China or Guangdong (廣東) sought to return, alongside displaced Allied prisoners of war, forced laborers, and refugees.

## What happened
The newly established British Military Administration (**BMA**), headed by **Sir Cecil Harcourt** (夏愨), confronted severe shortages of food, medicine, and housing. Emergency relief operations were rapidly organized to distribute rations and establish makeshift shelters. Concurrently, a massive repatriation initiative began to send hundreds of thousands of destitute individuals back to their ancestral homes in mainland China or to reintegrate them into local society. Logistical bottlenecks, rampant inflation, and disease outbreaks complicated these efforts, resulting in high mortality rates among the most vulnerable returnees.

## Significance
The repatriation process marked the chaotic first phase of Hong Kong's postwar reconstruction. While the successful return of hundreds of thousands of people re-established the colony's demographic base, the immense strain of the refugee crisis laid bare the administrative challenges facing the returning British authorities and shaped the socioeconomic landscape of the early postwar era.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Viewed the repatriation and relief efforts as a triumphant, orderly humanitarian mission managed under extreme resource constraints by the British Military Administration to restore civic order.
- **Local Hong Kong Residents**: Emphasized the severe hardships, bureaucratic delays, and acute shortages of food and shelter that returning survivors faced in a devastated city during the immediate postwar months.
