## Background

By **March 1945**, the Japanese occupation of Hong Kong (香港) had plunged the territory into an acute humanitarian and economic crisis. Allied blockades, combined with the collapse of maritime trade, led to severe shortages of basic necessities such as rice, fuel, and medicine. The official rationing system administered by the Japanese military administration became largely dysfunctional, leaving the general population facing starvation and disease. To survive, residents increasingly turned to illicit trade, circumventing official controls to secure vital supplies.

## What happened

An extensive underground economy thrived in hidden locations across the territory, most notably around typhoon shelters and narrow alleyways. Networks of local boat people, small-scale merchants, and corrupt officials facilitated the smuggling of goods from neighboring regions into urban centers. Transactions often relied on hidden British currency or barter systems, as the occupation-issued military yen suffered from hyperinflation and widespread distrust. Essential items including scarce medicines, quinine, and smuggled foodstuffs changed hands under the constant threat of Kempeitai (憲兵隊) raids, arrests, and severe punishments. These clandestine markets operated through word-of-mouth and intricate community trust networks, forming a crucial lifeline for urban survival during the final months of the Pacific War.

## Significance

The wartime black markets demonstrated the remarkable resilience and adaptability of local communities under extreme duress. While colonial authorities and occupation forces viewed these activities as illegal disruption of order, local populations regarded them as essential survival mechanisms. The reliance on alternative currencies and informal distribution networks during this period is frequently cited by historians as a foundational factor in shaping Hong Kong's postwar entrepreneurial spirit and informal economic resilience.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial / Postwar Perspective**: Colonial authorities and subsequent official histories often viewed wartime black markets through the lens of lawlessness and wartime profiteering, emphasizing the disruption of legal economic order.
- **Local Hong Kong Community Perspective**: Local residents and oral histories remember the underground economy as an indispensable survival mechanism that bridged the gap between official starvation and endurance during brutal military rule.
- **New Territories Communities**: Rural inhabitants in areas like Sai Kung (西貢) and rural districts participated in and facilitated cross-border smuggling networks, leveraging traditional kinship and maritime ties to navigate the wartime crisis.
