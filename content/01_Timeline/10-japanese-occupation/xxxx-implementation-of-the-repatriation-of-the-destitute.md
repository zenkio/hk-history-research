## Background
Following the surrender of the British colonial government on **December 25, 1941**, Hong Kong fell under the administration of the **Imperial Japanese Army**. By **1942**, the city faced a severe humanitarian crisis. Supply lines from the mainland and overseas had been severed, leading to hyperinflation, a collapse in trade, and widespread starvation. The Japanese military administration sought to stabilize the city's remaining resources by reducing the population, which had been artificially inflated by refugees fleeing the Second Sino-Japanese War in the late 1930s.

## What happened
The occupation government launched the 'Repatriation of the Destitute' (遣返貧民政策) initiative. The policy primarily targeted the unemployed, those unable to purchase food at official prices, and recent arrivals who did not possess local residential status. Using force, military police and their collaborators rounded up civilians from tenements and makeshift shelters. Deportees were transported to the border at **Lo Wu (羅湖)** or shipped across the **Pearl River Delta (珠江三角洲)** to areas such as **Guangdong (廣東)**. It is estimated by many historians that the population of Hong Kong was reduced from approximately 1.6 million in early 1941 to roughly 600,000 by the end of 1945, though exact figures remain difficult to verify due to the destruction of administrative records.

## Significance
The mass expulsion serves as a defining event of the 'Three Years and Eight Months' (三年零八個月) occupation. It highlights the brutal pragmatism of the occupiers, who viewed the city's inhabitants as mouth-to-feed rather than citizens. The demographic shift fundamentally altered the social fabric of Hong Kong, dismantling community networks and leaving a lasting psychological impact on the survivors. The event is often cited as a turning point in the city's modern history, marking the extreme end of the colonial-transition period before the post-war recovery began.

## Perspectives

- **Post-war British Colonial Perspective**: The British administration often framed the policy as evidence of Japanese criminality and their total disregard for the welfare of the civilian population. This perspective emphasizes the cruelty of the expulsions as a violation of international humanitarian norms.
- **Local Hong Kong Survivor Perspective**: Many survivors emphasize the trauma of the separation and the life-threatening conditions faced by those deported to war-torn areas of the mainland. For them, the policy is remembered as an act of cold-blooded survivalism that prioritized the Japanese garrison at the direct expense of human life.
- **Historical/Revisionist Perspective**: Some contemporary historians analyze the policy within the broader context of total war in the Pacific, noting that the Japanese administration was itself suffering from severe logistical failures. They argue the policy reflects the catastrophic breakdown of regional supply chains rather than a singular ideology of expulsion.
