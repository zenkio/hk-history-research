## Background
Following the fall of Hong Kong on **December 25, 1941**, the Japanese military administration imposed severe restrictions on the press and public communication. The **Hong Kong News** (香港新聞) became the sole authorized newspaper, acting as a propaganda outlet for the occupying forces. Wireless radio receivers were confiscated or required official seals, and the dissemination of non-sanctioned information was strictly forbidden under penalty of imprisonment or execution. Despite these draconian measures, various underground networks emerged to bridge the information gap.

## What happened
Between **1943** and **1945**, clandestine groups—including remnants of the **British Army Aid Group** (英軍服務團) and the **Dongjiang Column** (東江縱隊)—organized the collection and distribution of news from Allied sources. These networks relied on smuggled radios hidden in domestic homes or remote rural areas in the **New Territories** (新界). Volunteers transcribed reports from overseas broadcasts, such as the BBC, and reproduced these snippets onto small, hand-copied leaflets often referred to as "flysheets" or "mosquito papers" (蚊報). These materials were distributed through trusted social circles, market contacts, and clandestine drop-off points to avoid detection by the **Kempeitai** (憲兵隊), the Japanese military police. The operation was inherently perilous, as the discovery of an illegal radio or illicit literature usually resulted in immediate detention.

## Significance
The existence of these underground networks served as a crucial psychological lifeline for residents suffering under food shortages and strict martial rule. By providing accounts of Allied progress in the Pacific Theater, these news networks countered Japanese claims of regional dominance. Historians debate the efficacy of these broadcasts; some emphasize their role in fostering anti-Japanese sentiment and preparing the population for liberation, while others suggest the reach was limited to an educated urban minority and had a negligible impact on the desperate struggle for daily survival in rural areas.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial perspective**: Often highlights these networks as evidence of the loyalty of the population to the British Empire and the bravery of intelligence officers coordinating with local resistance. The efforts are frequently framed as a prelude to the eventual return of colonial administration.
- **Chinese nationalist and local historian perspective**: Emphasizes the role of the Dongjiang Column (東江縱隊) and local guerrilla forces in resisting occupation, portraying these networks as a grassroots movement for national survival and resistance against foreign aggression.
