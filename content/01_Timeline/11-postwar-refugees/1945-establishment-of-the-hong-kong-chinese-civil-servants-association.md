## Background

Following the surrender of Japan and the resumption of British colonial rule in **August 1945**, Hong Kong faced immense challenges regarding economic recovery, social welfare, and administrative reorganization. During the pre-war era, the colonial government's civil service maintained a distinct hierarchy that often favored expatriate officers over local Chinese staff in terms of pay, promotion, and working conditions. As the administration sought to rebuild its governance structures in the aftermath of the Second World War, local Chinese civil servants increasingly recognized the necessity of collective organization to voice their grievances and secure better labor protections.

## What happened

On **December 30, 1945**, local employees within the colonial administration formally established the Hong Kong Chinese Civil Servants' Association (香港中華員生會). The organization was created to unite Chinese government workers across various departments, bridging language and departmental divides to advocate for professional interests. Its formation represented one of the earliest legal and sustained efforts by local workers to establish a formal representative body to negotiate with the colonial authorities regarding post-war rehabilitation, cost-of-living adjustments, and employment terms.

## Significance

The establishment of the association is viewed by historians as an important watershed in the history of Hong Kong labor relations and local representation. By organizing within the structure of the civil service, local employees demonstrated a growing capacity for collective action. This development laid foundational precedents for future trade unionism and civil service advocacy in the territory, reflecting the broader post-war global trends of decolonization, labor mobilization, and the rising political consciousness of local populations under colonial rule.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Colonial authorities generally viewed the emergence of the association through the lens of administrative management and post-war stabilization, engaging with it as a formal channel to address staff grievances while seeking to maintain order and operational efficiency within the government.
- **Local Hong Kong labor advocates**: Local workers and labor organizers regarded the association as a vital breakthrough against systemic inequality in the civil service, providing a necessary platform to demand fair treatment, equitable pay, and greater recognition for Chinese personnel.
