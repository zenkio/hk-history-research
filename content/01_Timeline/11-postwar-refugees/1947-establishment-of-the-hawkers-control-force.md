## Background
Following the end of the Japanese Occupation in **1945**, Hong Kong experienced a demographic explosion. The influx of refugees fleeing political instability and civil war in mainland China significantly expanded the urban population. With formal employment opportunities limited, many displaced persons turned to the informal economy, specifically street hawking, to sustain their livelihoods. By **1947**, these vendors occupied major thoroughfares and alleyways, creating significant challenges for urban sanitation, pedestrian traffic, and the maintenance of public order.

## What happened
In **1947**, the British colonial government sought to formalise the regulation of this burgeoning sector. The authorities established the Hawkers Control Force (小販管理隊), a specialised unit tasked with overseeing street activities. This agency was empowered to issue licenses, designate specific zones for trading, and clear vendors from areas deemed critical for traffic flow or public health. The creation of this force marked a transition from a laissez-faire approach toward a more structured, state-monitored model of urban management. Enforcement efforts often led to confrontations between the officers and the hawkers, who frequently relied on these mobile stalls for their survival.

## Significance
The establishment of the Hawkers Control Force serves as a microcosm of Hong Kong’s postwar transformation. It highlights the government's dual challenge of maintaining order in a densely populated city while acknowledging the economic necessity of the informal sector. Historians often debate the effectiveness of these early regulations. Some scholars interpret the policy as a necessary step for modernising urban infrastructure and public health standards. Others suggest the move reflected a broader colonial desire to exercise stricter control over a politically volatile, refugee-heavy population. This development set the precedent for subsequent decades, during which the regulation of hawkers became a recurring point of contention between the administration and the local grassroots community.

## Perspectives

- **Colonial Administration**: The administration viewed the regulation as an essential public service to ensure urban hygiene and traffic safety. They argued that formalising hawking was necessary to prevent the city's infrastructure from collapsing under the weight of the refugee crisis.
- **Refugee/Hawker Community**: For the newly arrived refugees, hawking was often the only available means of income and self-reliance. Many viewed the control force as an intrusive mechanism that threatened their subsistence and hampered their integration into the city.
