## Background

Following the end of the Second World War in **1945**, Hong Kong faced staggering challenges, including massive influxes of refugees, widespread destitution, and the breakdown of basic infrastructure. Initially, relief efforts relied heavily on ad-hoc charities, international aid organizations such as the United Nations Relief and Rehabilitation Administration (UNRRA), and traditional clan or native-place associations. As the refugee population swelled due to the Chinese Civil War, these voluntary measures proved insufficient to address the scale of urban poverty and social dislocation.

## What happened

In **1948**, the British colonial administration formally established the Social Welfare Office (社會福利辦事處) under the Secretariat for Chinese Affairs. This new governmental body was tasked with coordinating relief efforts, managing social welfare services, and addressing the needs of vulnerable populations, particularly women, children, and destitute refugees. By institutionalizing these functions, the administration began to absorb responsibilities previously handled by informal community networks and religious charities.

## Significance

The creation of the Social Welfare Office is widely viewed by historians as a foundational moment in the evolution of Hong Kong’s public administration. It signaled the colonial government's growing recognition that economic recovery required active state intervention in social welfare, laying the groundwork for the more extensive public housing and social service expansions that followed the **1953** Shek Kip Mei fire.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Viewed the establishment of the office as a progressive and pragmatic response to unprecedented humanitarian crises, demonstrating administrative competence and a growing sense of paternalistic duty toward the territory's residents.
- **Local Communities and Later Scholarship**: Often emphasize that the office was a belated and minimal intervention driven largely by fears of social unrest and political instability, arguing that grassroots mutual aid networks and voluntary associations actually bore the brunt of postwar relief work.
