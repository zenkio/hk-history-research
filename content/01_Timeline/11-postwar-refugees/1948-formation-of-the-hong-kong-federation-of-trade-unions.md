## Background

In the aftermath of the Second World War, Hong Kong faced severe economic dislocation, high unemployment, and rapid population growth driven by an influx of refugees from mainland China. Working-class residents endured harsh conditions, low wages, and limited labor protections under the British colonial administration. Against the backdrop of the ongoing Chinese Civil War between the Kuomintang (Nationalists) and the Chinese Communist Party (CCP), labor organizations in Hong Kong increasingly aligned with broader national politics. Left-leaning and pro-communist organizers sought to mobilize the burgeoning industrial workforce, laying the groundwork for a unified labor movement.

## What happened

On **April 20, 1948**, the Hong Kong Federation of Trade Unions (香港工會聯合會), commonly known as the FTU, was formally established. Bringing together numerous leftist trade unions representing various industries—such as transport, shipbuilding, and printing—the FTU created a centralized umbrella organization to coordinate labor activities, welfare services, and political campaigns. From its inception, the organization maintained close ties with the Chinese Communist Party and the mainland labor movement, positioning itself as a primary advocate for grassroots workers while also advancing broader patriotic and anti-colonial sentiments.

## Significance

The formation of the FTU marked a turning point in Hong Kong's social and political history. It transformed the fragmented local labor scene into a disciplined, highly organized mass movement capable of staging major industrial actions, such as the 1949 Kowloon dockyard strike. Over the following decades, the FTU evolved into one of the most influential sociopolitical institutions in the territory, operating schools, clinics, and cooperative stores alongside its union activities. Its establishment institutionalized a persistent left-right political division within Hong Kong society that would heavily influence local events through the mid-20th century and beyond.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Colonial authorities viewed the FTU with deep suspicion, regarding it as a subversive political instrument directed by a foreign government rather than a genuine economic labor union. Officials closely monitored its activities and restricted its operations to prevent communist subversion of public order.
- **Pro-Communist and Beijing Perspective**: From this viewpoint, the FTU was hailed as a patriotic vanguard that defended the legitimate rights and interests of Hong Kong workers against capitalist exploitation and colonial oppression. It was seen as an integral part of the broader Chinese national liberation and patriotic movement.
