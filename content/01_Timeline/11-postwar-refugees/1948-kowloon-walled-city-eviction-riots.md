## Background
The Kowloon Walled City (九龍城寨) occupied a unique legal and administrative gray area following the 1898 Convention for the Extension of Hong Kong Territory (《展拓香港界址專條》). While the British leased the surrounding New Territories, the 1898 treaty included a specific clause allowing Chinese officials to retain jurisdiction within the walled enclave, provided their actions did not interfere with British military defense. Over subsequent decades, the exact enforcement of this jurisdiction remained ambiguous, leading to fluctuating de facto control by Hong Kong authorities and local residents.

Following the conclusion of the Second World War, the population of the Walled City swelled dramatically as thousands of refugees and squatters arrived from mainland China. Citing sanitation concerns, rising crime, and urban disorder, the **Hong Kong Government** determined that clearing the densely packed enclave was necessary for public health and colonial administration.

## What happened
In **January 1948**, the Hong Kong colonial administration issued eviction orders to residents of the Walled City, announcing plans to demolish the structures and relocate the inhabitants. The announcement met with immediate and fierce resistance from the local population. Residents, joined by nationalist sympathizers and local organizations, organized protests and refused to vacate their homes.

Tensions escalated rapidly into violent riots. Protesters clashed with colonial police forces, and the unrest spilled over into the broader urban area. In Guangzhou (廣州) and Nanjing (南京), the protests struck a powerful nationalist nerve. Anti-British demonstrations erupted across major mainland cities, notably resulting in the torching of the British Consulate in Guangzhou. Facing intense diplomatic pressure from the Republic of China (中華民國) government and widespread public outrage, the British colonial administration ultimately backed down from its clearance scheme, abandoning the immediate demolition plans.

## Significance
The 1948 eviction crisis cemented the Kowloon Walled City's status as a near-autonomous enclave beyond effective colonial policing for the decades that followed. The inability of the Hong Kong Government to enforce its writ created a regulatory vacuum, allowing the area to develop its famous labyrinthine, high-density architecture and become largely controlled by triad syndicates until the Sino-British Joint Declaration initiated plans for its eventual demolition in the late 20th century.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Government**: Viewed the Walled City as a dangerous lawless slum, a breeding ground for disease and crime, and asserted that administrative control was necessary for public safety and order within the colony's borders.
- **Chinese Nationalist Government and Nationalists**: Regarded the eviction attempts as a violation of reserved Qing-era sovereign rights guaranteed under the 1898 treaty, utilizing the protests to stoke anti-colonial sentiment and assert Chinese territorial jurisdiction.
- **Local Residents and Squatters**: Resisted displacement to protect their homes, livelihoods, and communities, relying on a combination of legal ambiguity, popular mobilization, and defiance against colonial authority.
