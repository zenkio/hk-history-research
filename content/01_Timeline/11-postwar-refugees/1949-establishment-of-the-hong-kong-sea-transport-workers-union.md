## Background

Following the end of the Second World War in **1945**, Hong Kong experienced rapid postwar recovery, accompanied by a massive wave of refugees fleeing the Chinese Civil War. By **1949**, the triumph of the Chinese Communist Party on the mainland transformed Hong Kong's political landscape. The colony's maritime and transport sectors, vital to its entrepôt economy, housed a large, mobile, and politically conscious proletariat. Labor unions emerged as powerful organizations, deeply divided between pro-Nationalist (Kuomintang or KMT) and pro-Communist factions.

## What happened

In **1949**, the Hong Kong Sea Transport Workers Union (香港海運工會) was established amid this charged atmosphere. Maritime workers, ranging from dockers to seamen, faced difficult working conditions, low wages, and precarious employment. The newly formed union quickly became embroiled in the broader ideological struggle playing out across the colony. Rather than focusing solely on economic grievances regarding pay and hours, union leadership and membership found themselves pulled into the fierce rivalry between Beijing and Taipei sympathizers, leading to competing mobilizations, strikes, and internal power struggles.

## Significance

The establishment of the union highlighted the complex intersection of local labor conditions and external Chinese politics in early postwar Hong Kong. The intense polarization within maritime labor set a precedent for industrial relations in the 1950s, characterized by ideological factionalism. For the British colonial government, politically active and polarized unions posed a significant security and administrative challenge, prompting careful surveillance and occasional intervention to maintain public order without triggering massive anti-colonial uprisings.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Viewed militant labor activism and political polarization primarily as a threat to internal security and public order, necessitating careful surveillance and strict enforcement of emergency regulations to prevent mainland political conflicts from destabilizing the colony.
- **Pro-Communist and pro-Nationalist factions**: Regarded Hong Kong as a crucial strategic base for their respective political struggles, utilizing labor unions not just for workplace representation, but as instruments for ideological mobilization and political legitimacy among the overseas Chinese working class.
