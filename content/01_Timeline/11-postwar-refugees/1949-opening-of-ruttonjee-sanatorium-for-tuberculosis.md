## Background
In the aftermath of the Second World War, Hong Kong faced immense demographic and humanitarian challenges. A massive influx of refugees fleeing the Chinese Civil War caused the population to surge rapidly, leading to severe overcrowding, poor sanitation, and inadequate housing. Amid these desperate conditions, tuberculosis (TB) emerged as the leading cause of death in the territory, disproportionately affecting the impoverished working class and newly arrived refugees living in squatter settlements.

## What happened
On **February 23, 1949**, the Ruttonjee Sanatorium (律敦治結核療養院) was officially opened in Wan Chai (灣仔). The institution was established largely through the philanthropic efforts and endowment of **Jehangir Ruttonjee** (J. H. Ruttonjee / 律敦治), a prominent Parsi businessman in Hong Kong who tragically lost his daughter to the disease. Originally functioning as the French Hospital, the building was converted into a specialized medical facility dedicated entirely to the diagnosis, treatment, and study of tuberculosis. Collaborating with medical authorities and religious orders, the sanatorium gradually introduced modern treatments and institutional care that helped stem the epidemic.

## Significance
The opening of the sanatorium marked a critical turning point in Hong Kong's public health infrastructure. By transforming a general facility into a specialized tuberculosis center, it set a precedent for targeted disease management in the colonial territory. While the colonial government initially relied heavily on private philanthropy and voluntary organizations to shoulder the burden of healthcare for refugees, the success of institutions like Ruttonjee eventually prompted a more systematic, government-led public health and housing strategy in the 1950s and 1960s.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Colonial officials often emphasized their eventual partnership with local philanthropists as evidence of progressive social welfare development, though critics note that the government was slow to fund comprehensive public healthcare for refugees independently in the immediate postwar years.
- **Local Hong Kong community**: For the local population and prominent merchant elites like the Ruttonjee family, the sanatorium represented an essential community-led humanitarian response to an overwhelming crisis that the formal administration was initially unequipped to handle alone.
