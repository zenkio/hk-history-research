## Background

Following the conclusion of the Chinese Civil War and the founding of the People's Republic of China in **1949**, hundreds of thousands of mainland refugees fled southward into the British colony of Hong Kong. Among them were defeated Kuomintang (KMT) soldiers, former government officials, and their dependents who remained loyal to the Nationalist government now based in Taiwan. Initially, many of these refugees congregated in urban squatter areas, creating severe administrative, housing, and security challenges for the Hong Kong government.

## What happened

In **1950**, the colonial authorities decided to segregate and relocate these pro-Nationalist refugees to a remote, isolated coastal area in the southeastern New Territories known as Rennie's Mill, or Tiu Keng Leng (調景嶺). The site was named after **Alfred Herbert Rennie**, a Canadian businessman whose flour mill venture there had ended in failure decades earlier. The government, along with relief organizations such as the Haking Wong-sponsored groups and later international aid agencies, established a designated camp to house the population. Over the subsequent years, Tiu Keng Leng developed into a tightly knit community characterized by its fervent adherence to the Republic of China (Taiwan) flag, political symbols, and ideology, effectively functioning as a 'little Taiwan' within the British colony.

## Significance

Tiu Keng Leng became a unique geopolitical anomaly during the Cold War, representing a pocket of fierce anti-communist sentiment on the border of socialist China. The camp highlighted the complex humanitarian and security dilemmas faced by the British colonial administration as it managed a massive influx of politically polarized refugees. For decades, the enclave served as a crucial symbolic and logistical bridge between Taiwan and the anti-communist diaspora in Hong Kong until its eventual clearance and redevelopment by the government in the 1990s.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Viewed the relocation primarily as a pragmatic security and humanitarian measure to clear volatile urban squatter settlements and maintain public order amidst heavy political tensions.
- **Kuomintang loyalists and refugees**: Perceived the camp as a temporary base of resistance and a sanctuary where they could preserve their political identity and allegiance to the Republic of China.
- **Beijing and mainland authorities**: Scrutinized the enclave with suspicion, viewing it as a potential base for espionage, anti-communist subversion, and Kuomintang-backed operations against the newly established socialist state.
