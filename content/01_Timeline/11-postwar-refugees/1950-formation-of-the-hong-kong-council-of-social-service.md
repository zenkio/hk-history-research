## Background
Following the end of World War II in **1945**, Hong Kong faced a severe humanitarian crisis. The transition of power from Japanese occupation back to British administration coincided with a massive wave of refugees fleeing the Chinese Civil War. By **1950**, the population had swelled, placing immense pressure on the city's limited infrastructure and resources. The colonial government, while maintaining a policy of 'positive non-interventionism' in the economy, struggled to address the systemic poverty, malnutrition, and lack of housing that defined the era.

## What happened
In **1950**, representatives from various religious and secular voluntary agencies recognized that the scale of human need exceeded the capacity of fragmented, individual charity efforts. These organizations established the Hong Kong Council of Social Service (HKCSS, 香港社會服務聯會) as a coordinating body. The council was designed to bridge the gap between private charitable initiative and the nascent social welfare responsibilities of the colonial administration. By creating a unified network, these agencies sought to streamline the distribution of aid, standardize service quality, and present a collective voice when negotiating with the government for support and resources.

## Significance
The establishment of the HKCSS marked a turning point in the professionalization of social work in Hong Kong. It transitioned the territory from a landscape of sporadic, isolated relief efforts to a more structured social welfare system. Furthermore, the council acted as a crucial intermediary between the grassroots organizations working in refugee settlements and the colonial authorities. This model of 'subvented' social services—where the government provides funding to NGOs to carry out public welfare tasks—became the cornerstone of Hong Kong's social service delivery system for decades to come. The council's formation also reflected the increasing societal necessity for state-led intervention in public health and welfare, even as the government remained committed to its broader laissez-faire economic ideology.

## Perspectives

- **Colonial Administration**: The administration often viewed the council as a pragmatic partner that allowed for the provision of essential social services without requiring a massive expansion of the civil service. They favored the council's ability to mobilize private resources efficiently to maintain social stability.
- **Local Social Workers and Advocates**: Many local social workers and voluntary groups viewed the council as a necessary corrective to colonial neglect. They argue that the government's initial reliance on the council was a strategic attempt to deflect responsibility for the welfare of the refugee population onto charitable bodies.
