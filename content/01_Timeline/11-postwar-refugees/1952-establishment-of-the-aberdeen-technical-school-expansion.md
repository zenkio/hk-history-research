## Background

Following the end of the Second World War in **1945**, Hong Kong experienced an unprecedented demographic surge. Hundreds of thousands of refugees fled the Chinese mainland, creating a population boom that strained the territory's housing, social services, and educational infrastructure. At the same time, the imposition of a United Nations embargo on trade with China during the Korean War forced Hong Kong to pivot away from its traditional entrepôt trade and rapidly industrialize.

To sustain this nascent manufacturing economy, there was an urgent demand for skilled labor. Technical and vocational education was seen by both colonial authorities and local organizations as a vital tool to equip working-class youth with practical trades.

## What happened

In **1952**, the **Aberdeen Technical School** (香港仔工業學校), originally established by the Salesians of Don Bosco, underwent significant expansions to enhance its capacity for vocational training. The development aimed to modernize its facilities, allowing the institution to accommodate more students and provide up-to-date instruction in mechanics, carpentry, and other industrial arts.

While precise enrollment figures and capital expenditure details remain subjects for deeper archival verification, the expansion reflected a broader collaborative effort between religious orders, private philanthropy, and the colonial government to address the skills gap in the industrial sector.

## Significance

The expansion of technical institutions like the Aberdeen Technical School played a foundational role in supplying the skilled technicians and factory workers who drove Hong Kong's economic miracle. By offering structured vocational training, these schools not only met the immediate labor demands of the expanding factories but also provided upward mobility and social stability for working-class and refugee youths during a turbulent era.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Viewed vocational education expansions as a pragmatic and necessary measure to stabilize the economy, curb juvenile delinquency among refugee youth, and supply the burgeoning manufacturing sector with disciplined labor.
- **Later Scholarship**: Historians often analyze the institutionalization of technical training as part of a wider colonial strategy to manage the postwar refugee crisis through utilitarian education, shaping a compliant and productive working class.
