## Background

Following the end of the Second World War and the establishment of the People's Republic of China in 1949, hundreds of thousands of refugees fled across the border into the British Crown Colony of Hong Kong. This massive influx overwhelmed local infrastructure and created severe housing shortages. Unable to find affordable accommodation, many refugees erected dense, highly flammable squatter settlements on the hillsides and marginal lands of Kowloon and Hong Kong Island, including the area of Shek Kip Mei (石硤尾).

## What happened

On **25 December 1953**, a catastrophic fire broke out in the Shek Kip Mei squatter area. Fueled by high winds, flimsy wooden construction, and the extreme density of the dwellings, the blaze spread with terrifying speed. It raged for hours, destroying acres of makeshift homes and leaving an estimated **53,000 to 50,000** people destitute overnight on Christmas Day. The disaster exposed the extreme vulnerability of the colony's rapidly growing population to natural and man-made hazards.

## Significance

The Shek Kip Mei fire marks a watershed moment in Hong Kong's history. Prior to the disaster, the colonial government largely viewed squatter settlements as temporary and maintained a laissez-faire approach to housing the refugee population. Compelled by the sheer scale of the humanitarian crisis and the risk of social unrest, then-Governor **Alexander Grantham** (葛量洪) and the colonial administration abandoned their previous reluctance regarding direct state intervention. The government rapidly cleared the burnt site and initiated a massive public housing construction program, beginning with the erection of multi-story concrete resettlement blocks. This policy laid the physical and administrative foundation for Hong Kong's modern public housing estate system, which eventually housed a major portion of the territory's population.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Government**: Officials viewed the fire as an unprecedented emergency that forced a pragmatic, rapid shift from temporary relief to direct state responsibility for permanent housing and social welfare to maintain public order.
- **Local Residents and Later Scholarship**: Historians and local communities emphasize that while the fire catalyzed public housing, the early resettlement blocks were austere and served primarily to clear valuable land for industrial development while tightly controlling the refugee population.
