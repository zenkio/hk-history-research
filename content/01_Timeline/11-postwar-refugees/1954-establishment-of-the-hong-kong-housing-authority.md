## Background

Following the end of the Second World War in **1945**, Hong Kong experienced an immense influx of refugees fleeing the Chinese Civil War and the subsequent establishment of the People's Republic of China in **1949**. The population surged from roughly 600,000 at war's end to over two million by the early **1950s**. This demographic explosion far outpaced available housing stock, leading to widespread, densely packed squatter settlements constructed of flammable materials across the urban fringes and hillsides. 

## What happened

On Christmas Day in **1953**, a catastrophic fire broke out in the Shek Kip Mei (石硤尾) squatter area, leaving over 50,000 people homeless overnight. This disaster forced the colonial administration, led by Governor **Alexander Grantham**, to abandon its previous laissez-faire approach to housing and intervene directly. In **1954**, the government established the Hong Kong Housing Authority (香港房屋委員會) as a statutory body tasked with designing, building, and managing permanent multi-storey domestic housing for middle- and lower-income residents. Initially operating alongside the newly formed Resettlement Department, the Authority began constructing modern estates equipped with basic amenities.

## Significance

The creation of the Housing Authority laid the permanent foundation for Hong Kong's extensive public housing programme, which eventually housed a significant majority of the territory's population. It transformed urban geography, giving rise to high-density planned communities and reshaping the relationship between the colonial state and its populace through formalized social welfare provision.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Officials viewed the initiative as a pragmatic, humanitarian response to an unprecedented emergency, aimed at maintaining social stability, public health, and order against the backdrop of Cold War tensions.
- **Later critical scholarship**: Some contemporary and later historians argue that the rapid push for resettlement was also driven by economic imperatives, clearing valuable urban land for industrialization and commercial development while exercising greater social control over a politically volatile refugee population.
