## Background
In the aftermath of the Second World War, Hong Kong experienced an immense influx of refugees fleeing the Chinese Civil War and the establishment of the People's Republic of China. This demographic pressure led to the rapid proliferation of sprawling squatter settlements constructed of flammable materials across the urban fringe and hillsides. These densely packed communities lacked basic sanitation, running water, and fire safety infrastructure, making them highly vulnerable to disasters.

On **25 December 1953**, a massive fire broke out in the Shek Kip Mei squatter area, destroying the homes of over **50,000** people in a single night. This catastrophe overwhelmed existing charitable and government relief efforts, forcing the colonial administration to reconsider its laissez-faire approach to housing.

## What happened
In **1954**, the Hong Kong government formally established the Urban Council Resettlement Department (市政局徙置部) to take charge of emergency shelter administration and large-scale resettlement operations. Operating initially under the Urban Council, the department was tasked with clearing squatter areas and constructing multi-storey, utilitarian concrete blocks on-site to house displaced fire victims and other squatters.

This initiative marked the beginning of government-built, high-rise resettlement estates. These early blocks—often referred to as Mark I and Mark II blocks—featured communal washrooms and rudimentary living spaces designed to house maximum numbers of people efficiently and economically.

## Significance
The creation of the Resettlement Department is widely viewed as a watershed moment in Hong Kong history. It represented the government's transition from temporary disaster relief to a permanent, structural commitment to public housing. Later scholarship and contemporary observers note that this intervention laid the foundation for Hong Kong's massive public housing program, which eventually housed a significant portion of the territory's population and fundamentally reshaped its urban landscape.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Viewed the establishment of the department as a pragmatic and humanitarian necessity to maintain social stability, prevent disease, and clear valuable land for economic development.
- **Local residents and refugees**: Experienced the resettlement blocks as a vital safety net that provided physical security against recurrent fires, though many initially found the living conditions cramped and regimented.
- **Later historians**: Interpret the shift as a foundational moment in the evolution of the modern interventionist colonial state, transforming Hong Kong's governance model.
