## Background

In the post-World War II era, British Hong Kong experienced a massive influx of refugees, prompting the colonial government to launch rapid public housing construction projects. On **August 9, 1955**, during leveling work for the Lei Cheng Uk Village (李鄭屋村) resettlement estate in Sham Shui Po (深水埗), construction workers struck structural brickwork embedded in the hillside.

## What happened

Upon investigation by government officials and historians, the structure was identified as an ancient tomb constructed from hollow carved bricks bearing geometric patterns and inscriptions, characteristic of the Eastern Han dynasty (25–220 CE). The tomb featured a multi-chamber layout consisting of an anteroom and three side chambers. Archaeologists, led by officials from the University of Hong Kong and the colonial administration, recovered over fifty grave goods, including bronze bowls, mirrors, and various pottery vessels. Following documentation, the site was preserved and later opened to the public as a branch of the Hong Kong Museum of History.

## Significance

Before this discovery, knowledge of Hong Kong's early history relied heavily on sparse textual references rather than physical artifacts from within the territory itself. The tomb provided direct material evidence linking the region to the broader administrative and cultural sphere of the Han dynasty empire. While the physical existence of the tomb is undisputed, its exact historical context—specifically how a high-status Han tomb came to be located in what was then a remote southern frontier—remains a subject of scholarly discussion.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Colonial authorities viewed the discovery through an administrative and preservationist lens, quickly organizing academic documentation while ensuring that urban development for postwar housing could proceed safely around the historical site.
- **Local Hong Kong and Chinese Historians**: Local scholars and mainland Chinese historians emphasized the find as vital physical proof of Chinese cultural continuity and ancient administrative presence in the Pearl River Delta region, reinforcing deep historical ties to the mainland.
