## Background

Following the Chinese Civil War and the establishment of the People's Republic of China in **1949**, Hong Kong became a sanctuary for refugees and a proxy battleground for competing political ideologies. The population was deeply polarized between supporters of the Chinese Communist Party (CCP) and loyalists of the Kuomintang (KMT). Tensions frequently simmered in the densely populated resettlement areas, such as Tsuen Wan (荃灣) and Sham Shui Po (深水埗), where rival factions competed for influence among the working-class populace.

## What happened

On **October 10, 1956**, clashes erupted after a low-level colonial government official removed a Nationalist Taiwan flag (the Blue Sky, White Sun, and a Wholly Red Earth flag) from the Li Uk Yew resettlement estate (李屋村) in Kowloon. This act infuriated pro-KMT residents and workers. What began as localized protests quickly escalated into widespread rioting, looting, and arson targeting leftist organizations, trade unions, and properties. 

For several days, mobs attacked individuals perceived to be pro-Communist, and retaliatory violence occurred. The Hong Kong Police Force struggled to contain the chaos, eventually deploying the British military to restore order. By the time the violence subsided, official reports recorded scores of deaths and hundreds of injuries, alongside extensive property damage.

## Significance

The riots served as a stark wake-up call for the British colonial administration regarding the fragility of public order and the profound entrenchment of external Chinese politics within the colony. In response, the government accelerated its efforts to monitor political activities, tightened regulations on societies and publications, and gradually expanded social welfare and housing programs to foster a distinct local Hong Kong identity and curb the influence of both foreign political parties.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Perspective**: The colonial administration viewed the riots primarily as a breakdown of public order driven by unruly external political agitators. Officials emphasized the necessity of firm police and military intervention to protect citizens and maintain Hong Kong's neutrality in the Cold War.
- **Beijing and Pro-Communist Perspective**: From the perspective of Beijing and local leftist groups, the disturbances were framed as a pogrom orchestrated by Kuomintang agents and triad elements operating under the tacit tolerance of the British authorities, resulting in the persecution of patriotic compatriots.
- **Pro-Nationalist Perspective**: Pro-KMT supporters and local rightist networks regarded the events as a spontaneous uprising in defense of their patriotic symbols and freedom of expression against what they perceived as biased interference by the colonial government.
