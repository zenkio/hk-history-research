## Background
In the post-war decades, Hong Kong experienced rapid population growth driven by refugees fleeing mainland China. As the territory transformed into a bustling industrial hub, the demand for affordable mass entertainment grew significantly. Prior to the late 1950s, local visual entertainment was dominated by cinema, radio broadcasts, and print media. **Rediffusion (Hong Kong) Limited** (香港麗的呼聲), a subsidiary of the British-based Rediffusion group, had been operating a successful wired radio broadcasting service in the colony since **1949** (一九四九年).

## What happened
On **May 29, 1957** (一九五七年五月二十九日), Rediffusion expanded its operations by launching **Rediffusion Television** (麗的映聲), widely recognized as Hong Kong's and the Chinese-speaking world's first television station. Because wireless television frequencies and widespread household receivers were not yet standard, the service was initially delivered via a wired cable network directly to subscribers' television sets. Viewers paid a monthly subscription fee to rent the television receivers and access the dual-channel service, which featured programs in both English and Chinese. The service gradually introduced local news, variety shows, and imported foreign serials, laying the foundational infrastructure for Hong Kong's broadcasting industry.

## Significance
The introduction of television fundamentally altered how local residents consumed information and entertainment, moving families away from communal radio listening and cinema outings toward domestic screen consumption. From the perspective of local media history, the station served as a critical training ground for generations of pioneering producers, directors, and actors who would later shape the golden age of Cantonese cinema and television broadcasting in the subsequent decades. It also created a shared visual culture among a diverse population of refugees and local residents, fostering a nascent sense of collective Hong Kong identity.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial perspective**: Colonial authorities viewed the expansion of commercial broadcasting as a positive modernization effort that demonstrated British technological enterprise and provided structured, regulated entertainment for a rapidly expanding urban population.
- **Local Hong Kong perspective**: For working-class residents, early subscription television was often seen as an expensive luxury item accessible mainly in upscale households or neighborhood shops, yet it simultaneously sparked immense local excitement for modern visual media and homegrown talent.
