## Background
In the aftermath of the Second World War and the Chinese Civil War, Hong Kong experienced an unprecedented influx of refugees, significantly expanding the local labor pool. Simultaneously, entrepreneurial migrants from Shanghai brought capital, machinery, and industrial expertise. Throughout the early 1950s, the colonial government maintained a relatively laissez-faire economic policy, providing a stable legal framework while avoiding direct market intervention. As global demand shifted and raw materials such as polyethylene became more accessible, local manufacturers sought new avenues for mass production.

## What happened
Around **1957**, the production of artificial plastic flowers surged across the territory, quickly becoming a cornerstone of Hong Kong's export-oriented manufacturing. Factories imported injection-molding machinery and synthetic resins, producing standardized components. A defining feature of this industrial boom was the utilization of outwork, or cottage industry labor. Factories distributed plastic parts to tenement apartments, where women, children, and elderly residents trimmed, assembled, and packaged the flowers at home. This decentralized network allowed manufacturers to scale production rapidly with minimal overhead costs, integrating thousands of households into the formal export economy.

## Significance
The plastic flower boom marked a critical turning point in Hong Kong's transition from an entrepôt reliant on re-export trade to a dynamic manufacturing center. It demonstrated the viability of light industry and paved the way for subsequent diversification into electronics, textiles, and toys. Furthermore, the widespread availability of home-assembly work altered domestic labor patterns, enabling higher household income contributions from women and reshaping urban working-class life during the postwar decades.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Government**: Colonial administrators viewed the manufacturing boom as a successful validation of free-market capitalism and export-led growth, which helped absorb surplus labor and stabilize the post-war territory without heavy state expenditure.
- **Local Workers and Families**: For many grassroots households in squatter settlements and tenements, the cottage industry provided vital supplementary income, though contemporaries and later oral histories also noted long working hours, low piece-rate wages, and poor home-working conditions.
- **Later Economic Historians**: Recent scholarship emphasizes that while the plastic flower boom was pivotal, its rapid scaling relied heavily on flexible, informal subcontracting networks that lacked standard labor protections, highlighting the complex social costs of early industrialization.
