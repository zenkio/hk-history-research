## Background
Following the Second World War and the Chinese Civil War, Hong Kong experienced an unprecedented influx of refugees, swelling the population and straining the urban capacity of the Kowloon peninsula and Hong Kong Island. At the same time, displaced entrepreneurs and industrialists from Shanghai brought capital, machinery, and labor expertise, creating an urgent demand for industrial land. The traditional rural market town of **Tsuen Wan (荃灣)**, located in the **New Territories (新界)**, possessed coastal access and open space, making it a prime candidate for decentralization and industrial zoning.

## What happened
Beginning in the late 1950s, and formalized around **1958**, the colonial administration initiated a master planning strategy to convert Tsuen Wan into Hong Kong's pioneer satellite town. The government reclaimed land, built infrastructure, and zoned large areas for textile mills, dyeing factories, and metalworking plants. Alongside industrial zones, high-density public housing estates and resettlement areas were constructed to house the burgeoning factory workforce. This initiative effectively merged the historic villages of Tsuen Wan, **Kwai Chung (葵涌)**, and **Tsing Yi (青衣)** into a cohesive manufacturing and residential complex.

## Significance
Tsuen Wan's development marked a major turning point in Hong Kong's urban planning history, shifting away from organic urban congestion toward state-directed new town strategies. It provided the manufacturing base that fueled Hong Kong's export-oriented economic boom throughout the 1960s and 1970s. Furthermore, the project established the precedent for subsequent generations of new towns in the New Territories, permanently altering the demographic and geographical landscape of the territory.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Viewed the satellite town strategy as a progressive, pragmatic solution to overcrowding and industrial land shortages, successfully modernizing Hong Kong's economy through centralized planning.
- **New Territories indigenous communities**: Often experienced the rapid urbanization as a disruption of traditional rural life, customary land rights, and agricultural livelihoods, as ancestral lands were acquired for industrial and infrastructure development.
