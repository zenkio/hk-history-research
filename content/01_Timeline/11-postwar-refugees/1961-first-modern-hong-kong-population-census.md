## Background

Following the Second World War and the Chinese Civil War, Hong Kong experienced an unprecedented influx of refugees and migrants, causing the population to swell rapidly within a short span of time. Prior to **1961**, colonial authorities relied largely on vital registration statistics, head counts, and housing estimates rather than a synchronized, comprehensive enumeration. By the late 1950s, the lack of precise demographic data hampered effective administration, particularly in planning housing, education, health services, and infrastructure to cope with the burgeoning manufacturing sector.

## What happened

On **March 7, 1961**, the Hong Kong government executed the first modern population census under the direction of the Census Commissioner, **K. M. A. Barnett** (貝納特). Thousands of enumerators, including many teachers and civil servants, were mobilized to visit households across the urban areas and the New Territories (新界) within a single designated period. The operation sought to record not only total headcount but also detailed socioeconomic metrics, including age distribution, employment status, housing conditions, and birthplace. The final published findings revealed a total population of approximately **3,133,131**, a figure significantly higher than many previous official estimates.

## Significance

The **1961** census marked a turning point in evidence-based governance in Hong Kong. By quantifying the massive demographic scale and high population density—particularly the large proportion of residents born outside the territory—the census underscored the urgent need for expanded public housing estates, schools, and transport networks. Historians note that this statistical clarity helped lay the groundwork for the social policy reforms and infrastructural expansion that accelerated through the remainder of the decade.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Colonial officials viewed the census as a triumph of modern administrative technique, providing the empirical foundation necessary to transform a temporary refugee haven into a stable, planned industrial economy.
- **Local Hong Kong and Refugee Communities**: Many working-class residents and recent arrivals regarded the massive enumeration with cautious anxiety, fearing that government data collection might lead to increased taxation, surveillance, or restrictions on informal housing.
- **Later Historical Scholarship**: Modern scholars analyze the census as a critical moment of state-building, highlighting how colonial authorities utilized quantification to manage social unrest and integrate a displaced population into the capitalist framework.
