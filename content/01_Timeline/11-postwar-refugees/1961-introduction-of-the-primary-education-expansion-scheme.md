## Background

Following the Second World War and the Chinese Civil War, Hong Kong experienced a massive influx of refugees, leading to a dramatic surge in the youth population. During the **1950s**, existing educational infrastructure was vastly inadequate, leaving thousands of children without access to formal schooling and resulting in rising illiteracy rates and child labor concerns. While private and missionary schools operated in the territory, many working-class families could not afford their fees. 

## What happened

In **1961**, the colonial administration introduced the Primary Education Expansion Scheme (小學擴展計劃). The initiative aimed to rapidly increase the availability of government and government-aided primary school places, particularly targeting working-class districts and resettlement areas. Through capital subsidies for non-profit sponsoring bodies and direct government construction, the scheme sought to provide a subsidized primary education foundation for children aged six to eleven. 

## Significance

This policy marked a turning point in the colonial government's social welfare responsibilities, shifting education from an elite privilege or purely charitable endeavor toward a more systematic public provision. By improving literacy and basic skill levels among the postwar generation, the expansion laid the groundwork for Hong Kong's subsequent economic transition into a manufacturing and industrial powerhouse in the **1960s** and **1970s**.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Officials framed the scheme as a progressive humanitarian and developmental achievement, demonstrating the government's commitment to social welfare and modernization for its burgeoning population.
- **Local Communities and Grassroots**: Working-class residents and refugee families welcomed the increased access to affordable schooling, viewing it as a vital channel for social mobility, though many felt the pace of expansion still lagged behind actual demand in crowded resettlement estates.
- **Later Scholarship**: Historians often analyze the expansion not merely as altruism, but as a strategic effort by the colonial state to maintain social stability and cultivate a disciplined, literate workforce suited for rapid industrialization.
