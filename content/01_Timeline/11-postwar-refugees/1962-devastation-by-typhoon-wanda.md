## Background
By **1962**, Hong Kong was experiencing a massive postwar population boom driven by refugees from mainland China. This rapid influx led to the proliferation of densely packed squatter settlements constructed from flimsy materials perched precariously on hillsides and low-lying coastal areas. Meteorological monitoring and typhoon preparedness were developing, but public warning systems and structural resilience against severe weather were not yet adequate for a storm of maximum intensity.

## What happened
On **September 1, 1962**, Typhoon Wanda (颱風溫黛) struck the territory with Category 5 intensity, bringing hurricane-force winds and a massive storm surge. Shattering local meteorological records, the storm caused severe flooding in low-lying agricultural and coastal regions such as Sha Tin (沙田) and Tsuen Wan (荃灣). Thousands of squatter huts were flattened or washed away, leaving tens of thousands homeless overnight. Official figures recorded over 130 fatalities and hundreds of injuries, though unofficial estimates sometimes suggested the actual toll among the most marginalized communities could have been higher due to unrecorded transient residents.

## Significance
The immediate aftermath of Typhoon Wanda forced a critical reassessment of government preparedness and urban planning. The disaster highlighted the extreme vulnerability of the refugee population housed in informal settlements, prompting calls for more robust public housing initiatives and improved meteorological forecasting. In the longer term, the catastrophe served as a catalyst for upgrading Hong Kong's emergency infrastructure, drainage systems, and building regulations, fundamentally reshaping how the authorities managed natural disasters in subsequent decades.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: The colonial government viewed the disaster primarily as an unprecedented meteorological event that overwhelmed existing emergency response capacities, subsequently using the crisis to justify accelerated resettlement schemes and tighter building controls.
- **Local Hong Kong and New Territories Communities**: Affected residents and villagers saw the devastation as a profound humanitarian tragedy that exposed the government's initial neglect of squatter areas and rural flood defenses, demanding more direct relief and permanent housing solutions.
