## Background

Following the Second World War and the establishment of the People's Republic of China in **1949**, Hong Kong experienced an unprecedented influx of refugees, capital, and entrepreneurial talent from mainland China. This demographic shift provided a vast, inexpensive labor pool that catalyzed the territory's transition from an entrepôt reliant on re-export trade to a dynamic manufacturing hub. The Chinese Manufacturers' Association of Hong Kong (香港中華廠商聯合會), established earlier to promote local commerce, played a central role in organizing industrial exhibitions to market locally produced goods both domestically and internationally.

## What happened

By the early **1960s**, the annual exhibition had grown into a massive civic and commercial spectacle, often held at major public sites such as the former Murray Road site or Victoria Park (維多利亞公園). The event featured expansive pavilions displaying a wide array of consumer goods, including textiles, garments, plastics, enamelware, and electronics, all stamped with the prominent 'Made in Hong Kong' label. Millions of local residents and international visitors attended the fair annually, transforming the exhibition grounds into a vibrant showcase of modern industrial capability and consumer culture.

## Significance

The exhibition was widely regarded as a pivotal morale booster, fostering a distinct local Hong Kong identity tied to industriousness, resilience, and economic self-sufficiency. It provided small and medium enterprises with a vital platform to secure international export orders, thereby integrating Hong Kong into global supply chains. Furthermore, the event symbolized the remarkable recovery and modernization of a society that had recently emerged from wartime devastation and severe social strain.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Viewed the thriving industrial exhibitions as evidence of successful laissez-faire economic policies and social stabilization, which helped legitimize colonial governance in the post-war era.
- **Local industrialists and workers**: Celebrated the fairs as a testament to grassroots ingenuity and collective hard work, fostering a distinct 'Hong Kong spirit' of resilience and upward mobility amid difficult living conditions.
- **Later scholarship**: Examines the exhibition as a crucial site for the construction of modern Hong Kong consumer culture and identity, while noting that the rapid industrial growth also masked deep labor exploitation and inadequate social welfare.
