## Background

Following the Second World War and the Chinese Civil War, Hong Kong experienced an unprecedented influx of refugees, swelling the population rapidly and creating an acute housing crisis. As industrialisation accelerated, private landlords often exploited the desperate demand for shelter by raising rents exorbitantly or issuing sudden eviction notices to tenants living in subdivided flats and older tenement buildings (tong lau 唐樓). While earlier postwar legislation had attempted to control rents, loopholes and enforcement challenges left many tenants vulnerable.

## What happened

In **1962**, the colonial administration enacted the Tenancy (Notice of Termination) Ordinance (租務(終止通知)條例). The legislation was designed to curb predatory landlord practices by introducing mandatory notice periods for termination and providing legal mechanisms for tenants to contest unreasonable rent hikes and arbitrary evictions. It sought to stabilize the residential rental market while the government concurrently ramped up its public housing construction programme, most notably through the resettlement estates initiative.

## Significance

The ordinance marked a critical turning point in the evolution of Hong Kong’s landlord-tenant law and the broader transition of the colonial state toward interventionist social welfare policies. By establishing statutory rights for tenants, the legislation mitigated some of the worst social pressures of the rapid industrialisation era. However, historians note that the effectiveness of the ordinance was mixed, as enforcement was occasionally difficult in densely packed urban enclaves, and some property owners found alternative ways to displace occupants or convert buildings for commercial use.

## Perspectives

- **Colonial Administration**: Viewed the ordinance as a pragmatic and necessary regulatory intervention to maintain social stability, prevent unrest stemming from homelessness, and protect the industrial workforce.
- **Property Owners and Landlords**: Often criticized the legislation as an infringement on private property rights, arguing that rent controls and restrictions on eviction discouraged investment in building maintenance and new residential construction.
- **Tenants and Working-Class Communities**: Welcomed the legal protections as a vital safeguard against exploitation by landlords, though many continued to face severe overcrowding and substandard living conditions.
