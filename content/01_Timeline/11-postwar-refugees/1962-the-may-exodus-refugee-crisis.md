## Background

By the early **1962**, the People's Republic of China was suffering through the catastrophic economic collapse and famine known as the **Great Leap Forward** (大躍進). Millions died of starvation across Guangdong and other provinces. Rumours began to circulate that the British colonial authorities in Hong Kong were adopting a more lenient border policy or issuing identity cards, which prompted hundreds of thousands of desperate residents to journey toward the southern border adjacent to the **New Territories** (新界).

## What happened

Beginning in **May 1962**, the influx escalated dramatically into a massive wave of migration. Tens of thousands of refugees gathered near the border, eventually breaching checkpoints or crossing the **Sham Chun River** (深圳河) en masse. Local villagers in the New Territories often provided food, water, and shelter to the exhausted arrivals, while the colonial government mobilized the police and the British military to manage the crisis. Many refugees were sheltered temporarily in urban areas such as **Rennie's Mill** (調景嶺) or makeshift camps before the authorities began systematic deportations.

Estimates regarding the total number of people who crossed and those subsequently repatriated vary significantly due to the chaotic nature of the border at the time. By late May and June, under increasing pressure from Beijing and through tightening internal security, the mass exodus was largely stemmed, though smaller-scale illegal immigration persisted for decades.

## Significance

The **May Exodus** (逃港潮) marked a turning point in Hong Kong's demographic and social evolution. It demonstrated the profound human cost of mainland political campaigns on the territory's borders and tested the limits of colonial infrastructure, housing, and social services. The crisis forced the government to re-evaluate its border control mechanisms and contributed to the burgeoning identity of a resilient local population rooted in the shared hardship of the era.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Faced with severe administrative strain, the government viewed the sudden influx as an unsustainable crisis that threatened public health, housing, and economic stability, ultimately prompting coordinated deportations back across the border.
- **Local Hong Kong and New Territories Communities**: Villagers and urban residents often felt deep sympathy for their starving compatriots, frequently offering humanitarian aid, food, and temporary shelter despite the legal risks and official crackdowns.
- **Later Scholarship and Historians**: Modern academic research emphasizes that the exodus was a direct humanitarian consequence of mainland state policies, while also highlighting the complex interplay between local benevolence, colonial security concerns, and international geopolitics.
