## Background

In the post-war decades, Hong Kong experienced a massive influx of refugees from mainland China, leading to a significant increase in the college-aged population. Prior to the 1960s, the University of Hong Kong (香港大學) was the sole institution of higher education in the territory, operating primarily in English and catering to an elite segment of the population. Graduates of Chinese-middle schools faced severely limited opportunities for tertiary education locally.

To address this gap, several private post-secondary institutions were established by educators and scholars who had relocated from the mainland. Three prominent colleges—New Asia College (新亞書院), Chung Chi College (崇基學院), and United College (聯合書院)—formed the core of what would become a new institution. In **1959**, the Hong Kong government appointed the Fulton Commission to advise on the creation of a new university that would utilize Chinese as a principal medium of instruction while maintaining academic standards comparable to international institutions.

## What happened

Following the recommendations of the Fulton Commission, legislative steps were taken to unite the three autonomous colleges under a single federal university structure. On **October 17, 1963**, the Chinese University of Hong Kong (香港中文大學) was officially established by ordinance. 

The newly formed university adopted a federal system, preserving the unique traditions and internal administration of the constituent colleges while centralizing degree-awarding powers and overall academic governance. **Li Cho-ming** (李卓敏) was appointed as the institution's first vice-chancellor. Initially operating across various temporary premises, planning soon began for a permanent campus in Sha Tin (沙田).

## Significance

The establishment marked a major milestone in local educational history by institutionalizing bilingual and bicultural higher education in the British colony. It provided expanded academic pathways for Chinese-middle-school graduates who might otherwise have had to study abroad or forego university education. Over time, the university grew into a major comprehensive research institution, though its internal structure evolved significantly in subsequent decades, particularly with reforms in the late 1970s and 2000s that shifted certain powers from the colleges to the central university administration.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Viewed the creation of the university as a pragmatic measure to expand higher education capacity, alleviate social pressures from the refugee population, and cultivate a moderate local leadership class.
- **Local Chinese educators and scholars**: Regarded the amalgamation as a vital victory for the preservation and promotion of Chinese culture and higher learning within a colonial setting, offering an alternative to Western-centric education.
