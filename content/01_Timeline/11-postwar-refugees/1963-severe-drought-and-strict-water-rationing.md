## Background

By the early **1963**, Hong Kong's rapid population growth, driven by an influx of refugees and postwar recovery, placed immense strain on the territory's limited water infrastructure. Reliance on local reservoirs such as **Tai Tam Reservoir (大潭水塘)** and **Shing Mun Reservoir (城門水塘)** proved increasingly precarious as seasonal rainfall fluctuated. Prior dry spells had already demonstrated the fragility of the water supply, setting the stage for one of the most severe environmental and administrative crises in the colony's history.

## What happened

An exceptionally meager monsoon season led to a severe lack of rainfall through **1963**, causing reservoir levels to plummet to critical lows. In **May 1963**, the colonial government implemented drastic water rationing measures. Initially, water was supplied for only a few hours a day, but as conditions worsened, the restrictions escalated to a staggering four hours every four days.

Local industries, particularly the expanding textile and dyeing manufacturing sectors which required vast amounts of water, faced severe production bottlenecks and financial losses. To alleviate the crisis, the government initiated emergency water-hauling schemes using tankers and negotiated agreements with the Guangdong provincial authorities to import water from the **Shum Chun River (深圳河)**, laying the groundwork for future cross-border water supply pacts.

## Significance

The **1963** drought served as a harsh turning point in Hong Kong's infrastructure planning. It exposed the absolute unsustainability of relying solely on local rainwater catchments for a rapidly industrializing and expanding population. The crisis accelerated large-scale engineering projects, most notably the construction of the **Plover Cove Reservoir (船灣淡水湖)**, which involved damming a coastal inlet to create a massive freshwater lake. Furthermore, it cemented a long-term economic and infrastructural reliance on mainland China for essential resources, fundamentally altering Hong Kong's relationship with its northern neighbor.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: The colonial government viewed the crisis as an engineering and logistical challenge, responding with emergency importation measures from Guangdong and accelerating ambitious infrastructure projects like Plover Cove to secure long-term self-sufficiency.
- **Local Hong Kong residents and industrial workers**: For ordinary citizens and factory workers, the period was remembered as one of profound daily suffering and economic precarity, where queuing for water dominated domestic life and threatened livelihoods.
