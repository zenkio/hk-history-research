## Background

By the mid-**1960s**, Hong Kong’s manufacturing sector had grown substantially, largely driven by an influx of capital and entrepreneurial talent from mainland China after **1949**, alongside a rapidly expanding labour force. Initially reliant on low-cost labour and simple assembly work—principally in textiles, garments, and light plastics—local industries faced growing competition internationally. Rising global standards and tightening trade regulations meant that simple mass production was no longer sufficient to sustain long-term economic growth.

## What happened

To address these structural challenges, the colonial government enacted legislation to establish the Hong Kong Productivity Council (香港生產力促進局) in **1965**. The institution was formally tasked with spearheading efforts to promote productivity across various manufacturing sectors. Its mandate included providing consultancy services, organizing technical training programmes, conducting industrial research, and introducing modern management techniques to local factories. By bridging the gap between traditional workshop methods and contemporary industrial science, the Council aimed to upgrade the technological capabilities of small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) that formed the backbone of the territory's economy.

## Significance

The founding of the Council marked a strategic pivot in economic policy. While the Hong Kong government traditionally adhered to a doctrine of positive non-interventionism, the creation of statutory bodies like the Productivity Council demonstrated a pragmatic willingness to provide institutional support where market failures or coordination challenges hindered industrial advancement. This initiative helped lay the groundwork for Hong Kong's eventual transition into a more sophisticated export economy capable of producing higher-value goods, thereby reinforcing its position as a major manufacturing hub in the Asia-Pacific region during the postwar decades.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Viewed the Council as a pragmatic, vital intervention to safeguard Hong Kong's export competitiveness without abandoning the broader framework of free-market capitalism.
- **Local industrialists and manufacturers**: Generally welcomed the technical assistance and training programmes, seeing them as necessary tools to upgrade factory floors and meet stringent overseas market demands.
