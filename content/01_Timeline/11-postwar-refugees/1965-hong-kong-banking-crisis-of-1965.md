## Background
In the post-war decades, Hong Kong experienced rapid industrialisation and population growth, leading to an expansion of the local banking sector. Many smaller, locally incorporated Chinese-owned banks emerged to serve growing commercial needs outside the dominant British-managed exchange banks.

## What happened
On **6 February 1965**, a severe banking crisis began with a run on the Ming Tak Bank (明德銀號), which was forced to suspend payments after failing to meet demands for cash withdrawals. The panic quickly spread to other local institutions, notably the Canton Trust and Commercial Bank (廣東信託商業銀行) and the Hang Seng Bank (恆生銀行). 

To stem the panic and prevent systemic collapse, the colonial government and the Hongkong and Shanghai Banking Corporation (香港上海滙豐銀行) intervened. **HSBC** acquired a 51 percent controlling stake in Hang Seng Bank, providing the necessary liquidity to restore public confidence. Other affected banks were either restructured, placed under government control, or wound down.

## Significance
The crisis exposed vulnerabilities in Hong Kong's loosely regulated financial system. In response, the colonial administration introduced tighter banking legislation, including the Banking Ordinance of 1965, which established stricter reserve requirements, limited lending practices, and granted greater oversight powers to the government, laying the foundation for modern financial regulation in the territory.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Viewed the crisis as a consequence of speculative excess among unregulated local banks, necessitating swift government intervention and legislative reform to protect Hong Kong's reputation as an international financial centre.
- **Local banking community**: Often saw the crisis as a painful reckoning that disproportionately consolidated power in the hands of major British-backed institutions like HSBC, while significantly curtailing the independence of locally owned Chinese banks.
