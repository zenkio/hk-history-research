## Background
Following the Second World War and the establishment of the People's Republic of China in **1949**, Hong Kong experienced an unprecedented influx of refugees, entrepreneurs, and skilled industrialists, primarily from Shanghai. This demographic shift provided a vast, cost-effective labor force alongside capital and technical expertise in textile manufacturing. Concurrently, the loss of traditional entrepôt trade with mainland China due to the Korean War embargoes forced the colonial economy to pivot aggressively toward industrialization.

## What happened
By **1965**, Hong Kong's manufacturing sector reached new heights, dominated by the textile and garment industries. Factories spread rapidly across urban areas and industrial districts like Kwun Tong (觀塘) and Tsuen Wan (荃灣). Thousands of small-to-medium enterprises operated alongside larger mills, producing cotton yarn, knitted fabrics, and ready-to-wear apparel for export to Western markets, particularly the United Kingdom and the United States. While plastics and toys also flourished, textiles remained the undisputed backbone of the local economy, absorbing the largest share of the industrial workforce.

## Significance
This industrial boom cemented Hong Kong's status as a global economic player and laid the foundation for its post-war modernization. The rapid growth created employment opportunities, shaped the urban landscape through public housing and industrial estates, and fostered a distinct local identity tied to hard work and entrepreneurial resilience. However, heavy reliance on exports also exposed the territory to international trade policies, leading to the eventual introduction of quota systems by Western nations.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Viewed industrialization and the rise of textile exports as a successful vindication of laissez-faire economic policies, transforming a refugee burden into a productive, self-sufficient colony.
- **Local industrialists and refugees**: Emphasized individual resilience, hard work, and entrepreneurial spirit in overcoming scarce land and capital to build world-class manufacturing enterprises from scratch.
- **Later labor and social historians**: Point out the intense exploitation, long working hours, and lack of labor protections that underpinned the economic miracle, highlighting the social costs of rapid industrialization.
