## Background
In the aftermath of **World War II**, Hong Kong experienced a rapid population influx and significant industrial growth, placing an immense strain on the territory's limited water resources. Throughout the early **1950s** and **1960s**, Hong Kong suffered from severe water rationing, which frequently restricted supply to a few hours per day. Faced with the geographical limitations of a mountainous, land-scarce territory, the colonial administration turned to radical engineering solutions to ensure a stable water supply for the burgeoning population and the textile-led manufacturing sector.

## What happened
The **Plover Cove Reservoir** (船灣淡水湖) project involved damming a large sea inlet in the **Tolo Harbour** (吐露港) area. Construction began in **1960** and was completed in **1966**. The engineering feat required the construction of a main dam and two subsidiary dams to enclose the inlet, followed by the systematic pumping out of the seawater and its replacement with freshwater from surface runoff and imported water from mainland China. At the time of its opening, it was the first project of its scale in the world to convert a coastal bay into a freshwater reservoir, significantly increasing the total storage capacity of the territory.

## Significance
The completion of the reservoir stands as a testament to the engineering ingenuity of the mid-20th century in Hong Kong. It alleviated the immediate pressure of water scarcity and provided a critical foundation for the continued expansion of the local economy. Beyond its utility, the project fundamentally altered the local ecology and geography of the **Tai Po** (大埔) region. It serves as a symbol of the 'can-do' spirit often cited in the narrative of Hong Kong’s postwar rise, though it also reflects the intense state-led development strategies characteristic of the colonial era. The reservoir remains a vital part of the city's water infrastructure, even as later projects, such as the **High Island Reservoir** (萬宜水庫), later expanded capacity further.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: The government viewed the reservoir as a triumph of modern engineering and a necessary administrative response to humanitarian and economic crises. It was promoted as a demonstration of the colonial state’s capability to provide essential services despite challenging environmental constraints.
- **New Territories Communities**: For the indigenous and rural communities in Tai Po, the project meant the permanent loss of ancestral farmlands and coastal access. Many villagers were resettled, resulting in a complex legacy regarding the displacement of traditional livelihoods in favor of urban-centric industrialization.
