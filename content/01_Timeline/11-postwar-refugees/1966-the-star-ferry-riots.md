## Background

In the aftermath of the Second World War, Hong Kong experienced rapid population growth driven by refugees, industrialisation, and severe housing shortages. By the mid-1960s, the working-class population faced considerable economic pressure, inflation, and a lack of formal channels for political representation under British colonial rule. Against this backdrop, the Star Ferry (天星小輪) company applied to the government in late 1965 to increase its first-class passenger fares by 25 percent, amounting to a five-cent increase from 20 cents to 25 cents. For many ordinary residents, even this minor rise was viewed as an unacceptable burden on daily commuting costs.

## What happened

On **April 4, 1966**, a young man named **Elsie Tu**'s associate, **So Sau-chung** (蘇守忠), began a hunger strike at the Central Star Ferry pier, distributing leaflets and protesting the fare hike. His arrest by police on April 5 galvanized public sympathy and triggered spontaneous demonstrations. Over the next several nights, crowds gathered in Kowloon, particularly around Tsim Sha Tsui (尖沙咀) and Yau Ma Tei (油麻地). What began as peaceful protests against the fare increase escalated into stone-throwing, attacks on police stations, and clashes with law enforcement. The colonial government imposed curfews, deployed the police tactical unit, and called in the military to restore order. By **April 8, 1966**, the unrest largely subsided, resulting in hundreds of arrests, numerous injuries, and at least one reported fatality.

## Significance

The Star Ferry Riots marked a turning point in Hong Kong's postwar history. While authorities initially attributed the unrest to criminal elements and hooliganism, subsequent official inquiries, such as the **Kowloon Disturbances 1966 Commission of Inquiry**, acknowledged that underlying social, economic, and generational grievances played a major role. The events highlighted the alienation of the postwar youth demographic and exposed the disconnect between the colonial administration and the general populace, prompting the government to consider administrative reforms and improve communication channels in the years leading up to the 1967 leftist disturbances.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Colonial authorities initially viewed the unrest as the work of criminal elements, agitators, and opportunistic youths taking advantage of a minor public policy issue. However, official inquiries later conceded that poor communication between the government and the public, along with socioeconomic pressures, contributed significantly to the escalation.
- **Local Hong Kong Residents and Activists**: Many local residents and social reformers saw the riots as a legitimate expression of public anger against rising living costs and an unresponsive colonial bureaucracy that ignored the daily struggles of the working class.
- **Later Scholarship**: Contemporary historians often interpret the 1966 riots as a crucial precursor to the identity shifts and political mobilisations of the late 1960s, demonstrating that postwar Hong Kong society was no longer politically passive.
