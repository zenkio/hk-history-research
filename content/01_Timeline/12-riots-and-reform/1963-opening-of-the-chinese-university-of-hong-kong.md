## Background
Prior to the 1960s, the University of Hong Kong was the territory's sole institution of higher education, operating primarily in English and catering to an elite minority. In the wake of rapid post-war population growth and the influx of mainland refugees, demand for tertiary education surged. To address these pressures and accommodate students educated in the Chinese language system, the colonial government explored the amalgamation of existing post-secondary Chinese-language colleges.

## What happened
On **October 17, 1963**, the Chinese University of Hong Kong (香港中文大學) was formally established as a federal university, integrating three existing institutions: **Chung Chi College** (崇基學院), **New Asia College** (新亞學院), and **United College** (聯合書院). **Lord Fulton** chaired the commission that recommended this federal structure, which combined traditional residential college systems with a centralized university administration. The institution later developed its scenic campus in **Ma Liu Shui** (馬料水).

## Significance
CUHK filled a crucial gap by offering tertiary instruction in Chinese, shaping generations of local scholars, writers, and public intellectuals. Throughout the turbulent decades of the 1960s and 1970s, its campus became a vibrant center for student activism, debates on Hong Kong identity, and movements advocating for social reform and Chinese language rights. Over time, the university evolved from its federal roots into a comprehensive research institution while retaining its distinct college identity.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Viewed the establishment of a Chinese-medium university as a pragmatic measure to expand higher education capacity and alleviate social tensions by channeling youth energy into academic pursuits.
- **Local Intellectuals and Founders**: Regarded the university as a vital custodian and continuation of traditional Chinese culture and higher learning outside of mainland China, particularly in the wake of the 1949 political shift.
