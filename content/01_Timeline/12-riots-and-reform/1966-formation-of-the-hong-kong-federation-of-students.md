## Background
During the mid-1960s, Hong Kong faced significant socioeconomic instability, characterized by rapid industrialization, poor living conditions for many, and limited political representation under British colonial governance. The **1966 Star Ferry riots**, which erupted in **April 1966** over proposed fare increases, exposed deep-seated social grievances. Amidst this climate, students at tertiary institutions began to recognize the need for a cohesive structure to express collective concerns regarding education policy and broader social justice issues.

## What happened
On **May 25, 1966**, the Hong Kong Federation of Students (HKFS) (香港專上學生聯會) was formally inaugurated. The organization was established by the student unions of the University of Hong Kong (香港大學) and the Chinese University of Hong Kong (香港中文大學), with the primary goal of providing a unified platform to represent the interests of the burgeoning tertiary student population. While its initial objectives were largely centered on academic life and internal university affairs, the federation quickly evolved to address public policy and social welfare concerns, influenced by the tense atmosphere following the **1966** disturbances.

## Significance
The formation of the HKFS is widely viewed by historians as a milestone in the development of Hong Kong's civil society. It signaled the emergence of a politically conscious, localized student movement that would eventually challenge colonial authorities on various fronts, including language rights and anti-corruption campaigns. The federation provided a training ground for future leaders and political figures, establishing a precedent for student involvement in the city's political discourse. Its establishment underscored a shift from the colonial administration’s previously paternalistic management of tertiary education toward a period where student organizations became formal stakeholders in the public sphere, creating a tension between institutional authority and grassroots political activism that persisted through the **1970s**.

## Perspectives

- **Colonial Administration**: The British colonial government initially viewed the development of formal student organizations with caution, balancing the need for intellectual development against concerns over radicalization and social disorder. Authorities frequently debated whether to treat the federation as a legitimate representative body or as an agitator that threatened the stability of the colony.
- **Student Activists**: For many early members, the federation represented a necessary evolution in Hong Kong's identity, moving beyond being a mere British colony to a place with a localized, critical student consciousness. They viewed the organization as an essential advocate for social reform and a check on colonial policy.
