## Background

By the mid-1960s, Hong Kong faced immense pressure on its social infrastructure due to rapid population growth driven by post-war migration and the local baby boom. Higher education options were severely limited, with the University of Hong Kong serving only a tiny fraction of secondary school graduates. To address the acute shortage of tertiary education places, various religious bodies and private organizations sought to establish post-secondary institutions, often operating under challenging financial and spatial constraints.

## What happened

Founded earlier as the Hong Kong Baptist College (香港浸會學院) in **1956** by the Baptist Convention of Hong Kong, the institution initially operated out of temporary premises. In **1966**, the college consolidated and expanded its operations by opening a dedicated permanent campus. This development allowed the institution to broaden its curriculum, accept larger cohorts of students, and improve its academic facilities. While it initially operated as a private post-secondary college without full university status, it played a crucial role in absorbing the burgeoning youth demographic that the public system could not yet accommodate.

## Significance

The establishment and expansion of tertiary institutions like Baptist College reflected the shifting landscape of Hong Kong education policy during the turbulent decade of the 1960s. Although widespread social unrest in **1966** and **1967** exposed deep-seated public grievances—including inadequate educational and economic opportunities for the youth—private and semi-private colleges helped alleviate some of this pressure. Later scholarship notes that the gradual institutionalization and government subsidization of post-secondary colleges in the subsequent **MacLehose era** laid the groundwork for the modern tertiary education system in Hong Kong.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Viewed private and religious post-secondary colleges as a pragmatic, cost-effective way to supplement the colonial government's limited higher education provisions and appease the growing youth population.
- **Local Religious and Educational Organizers**: Emphasized the moral and social necessity of filling gaps left by the government, aiming to provide accessible higher education rooted in holistic or religious values for local youth.
