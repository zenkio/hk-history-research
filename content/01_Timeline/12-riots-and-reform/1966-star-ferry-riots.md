## Background

In **April 1966**, the **Star Ferry Company** (天星小輪有限公司) sought approval from the British colonial government to raise its first-class fares by five cents, from twenty cents to twenty-five cents. While a seemingly minor economic adjustment, the proposed hike touched a nerve among the local population. Post-war Hong Kong (香港) was experiencing rapid industrialization alongside severe income inequality, overcrowding, and inadequate public services. For many working-class residents, public transport was a significant household expense.

## What happened

Public frustration quickly galvanized into direct action. On **April 4, 1966**, a young former Kowloon Wharf employee named **Elliott Ngok-shun** (蘇守忠) began a hunger strike at the Tsim Sha Tsui (尖沙咀) ferry pier, wearing placards protesting the fare increase. His solitary demonstration immediately attracted public sympathy and media attention. 

As days progressed, the peaceful protest drew crowds of sympathetic youths and students. However, the situation deteriorated by the evening of **April 5**. What began as a gathering of disgruntled youth spilled over into widespread disturbances, stone-throwing, and clashes with the police in the densely populated areas of Jordan (佐敦) and Yau Ma Tei (油麻地). 

Over the next two nights, the unrest intensified into riots characterized by property damage, looting, and arson. The colonial government deployed the **Hong Kong Police Force** (香港警察) and later the **British Army** to restore order. Authorities imposed curfews and arrested hundreds of participants. Order was largely restored by **April 8**, leaving dozens injured, one reported fatality, and significant material damage.

## Significance

The **Star Ferry Riots** shattered the complacency of the colonial administration. Rather than viewing the events merely as a localized criminal disturbance, subsequent analysis recognized the riots as a manifestation of deep-seated socioeconomic grievances and a widening generational divide. The unrest exposed a distinct disconnect between the colonial government and the local populace, who lacked meaningful political representation. In response, the administration initiated commissions of inquiry to investigate the root causes of the discontent, paving the way for the progressive social reforms later championed under Governor **Murray MacLehose** (麥理浩) in the 1970s.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Initial official assessments often emphasized the role of criminal elements, agitators, and external influences in subverting what began as a peaceful petition. However, subsequent official inquiries acknowledged genuine public grievances regarding livelihood and communication gaps between the government and the governed.
- **Local Hong Kong residents and youth**: For many ordinary citizens and the younger generation, the protests represented a long-overdue venting of frustration against an unresponsive, paternalistic colonial regime that ignored the daily economic struggles of working-class families.
