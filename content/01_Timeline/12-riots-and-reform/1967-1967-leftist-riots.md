## Background

By the mid-1960s, Hong Kong was experiencing rapid industrialization alongside stark social inequalities, low wages, and poor working conditions. These domestic grievances were amplified by external events, most notably the launch of the Cultural Revolution in mainland China in 1966. Local pro-communist organizations, emboldened by mainland rhetoric and radicalized activists, increasingly viewed the British colonial administration through a revolutionary lens. Tensions finally boiled over on **May 6, 1967**, during a labor dispute at the Artificial Flower Works in San Po Kong (新蒲崗), where striking workers clashed with management and subsequently with the colonial police.

## What happened

What began as a labor dispute rapidly transformed into widespread political unrest. The left-wing camp established the "Hong Kong and Kowloon compatriots of all circles to struggle against British persecution struggle committee" (港九各界同胞反對港英迫害鬥爭委員會), organizing massive protests, strikes, and demonstrations outside Government House. 

As the movement lost broad public support due to escalating disruptions, radical elements shifted tactics in July 1967 toward urban terrorism. Over the next several months, rioters planted thousands of real and dummy bombs across the territory, targeting public infrastructure, transport, and police stations. Several people, including innocent children and police officers, were killed by explosives. The colonial government responded with emergency regulations, mass arrests of leftist organizers, and the closure of several pro-communist schools and newspapers. The unrest largely subsided by the end of **December 1967** as the political climate in Beijing shifted and local fatigue set in.

## Significance

The **1967 Leftist Riots** marked a major turning point in Hong Kong’s modern history. The trauma of the bomb campaign and the realization of deep-seated public alienation forced the British administration under Governor **David Trench** (戴麟趾) and later **Murray MacLehose** (麥理浩) to radically rethink social policies, housing, education, and public engagement. This soul-searching laid the groundwork for the sweeping reforms of the 1970s that sought to build local identity and administrative legitimacy.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Viewed the events as a communist-inspired terrorist campaign orchestrated by external agitators aiming to destabilize public order, necessitating firm security measures and subsequent social reforms to win "hearts and minds".
- **Beijing and Pro-Communist Leftists**: Framed the mobilization as a patriotic anti-imperialist struggle against brutal colonial oppression, viewing the police suppression of protesters as fascist atrocities that justified popular resistance.
- **Local Hong Kong Residents**: Many ordinary citizens felt alienated by the disruption of daily life, economic instability, and the indiscriminate violence of the bomb campaign, which ultimately turned public opinion decisively against the leftist radicals.
