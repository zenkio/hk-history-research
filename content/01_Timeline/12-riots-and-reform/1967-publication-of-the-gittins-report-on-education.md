## Background
In the mid-1960s, Hong Kong's rapid post-war population growth placed immense strain on public infrastructure. The primary education sector was characterized by overcrowded classrooms, a widespread half-day schooling system, and a severe shortage of subsidized secondary school places. These conditions contributed to growing working-class grievances. Following the outbreak of the **1967 riots** (六七暴動), the colonial administration recognized an urgent need to evaluate and overhaul social services to address the root causes of public discontent.

## What happened
Led by **Hubert Roy Gittins** (吉廷斯), the committee produced a comprehensive review of the colony's educational framework. Published in **1967**, the Gittins Report criticized the heavy reliance on profit-driven private schools and the inadequacies of primary instruction. It highlighted that many children lacked access to quality education, which limited upward social mobility. The report recommended significant expansion of government and subsidized primary education, proposing measures to improve teacher training, reduce class sizes, and phase out the demanding Secondary School Entrance Examination where feasible.

## Significance
Although the report's recommendations were not immediately implemented in full, the Gittins Report served as a foundational blueprint for subsequent educational policies under Governor **Sir Murray MacLehose** (麥理浩). By identifying structural inequalities in schooling, the document helped shift colonial governance toward proactive social welfare reform, ultimately leading to the introduction of free and compulsory primary education in the 1970s.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Viewed the report as a vital technical assessment to modernize the civil service and upgrade social infrastructure, utilizing educational expansion as a key tool to foster civic loyalty and restore social order.
- **Local Hong Kong Public and Activists**: Saw the findings as long-overdue validation of the severe hardships faced by working-class families, arguing that the pre-existing system had long neglected basic human development in favor of economic laissez-faire policies.
