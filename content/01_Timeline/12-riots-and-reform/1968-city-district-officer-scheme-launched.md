## Background
Following the social unrest and leftist riots of **1967**, the British colonial administration recognized a severe disconnect between the government and the general population. Historically, the administration had relied heavily on elite intermediaries and tended to overlook grassroots grievances. To restore legitimacy and prevent future crises, colonial officials sought mechanisms to gauge public opinion more directly and respond to local needs.

## What happened
On **June 18, 1968**, the government officially launched the City District Officer (CDO) Scheme (民政主任計劃), dividing the urban areas of Hong Kong and Kowloon into distinct districts. The first batch of City District Officers was deployed to live and work among local residents. Their mandate was to explain government policies to the public, report local grievances back to the central administration, and foster a sense of civic participation.

## Significance
The scheme marked a significant pivot in colonial governance away from pure elitism toward managed consultation, laying foundational administrative groundwork for later reforms under Governor **Murray MacLehose** (麥理浩). It created a permanent bureaucratic apparatus dedicated to local administration, which later evolved into the District Boards and the modern District Council system.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Viewed the scheme as an enlightened administrative reform designed to bridge the gap between rulers and subjects, enhance public welfare, and secure social stability through proactive engagement.
- **Leftist and Pro-Beijing Circles**: Historically regarded such colonial outreach initiatives with suspicion, viewing them as paternalistic tools aimed at co-opting local leadership, defusing anti-colonial sentiment, and neutralizing grassroots political opposition.
