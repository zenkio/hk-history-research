## Background
During the 1950s and 1960s, Hong Kong's rapid industrialization driven by the manufacturing sector led to severe labor exploitation. Workers frequently faced long hours, unsafe conditions, and arbitrary dismissal without notice or compensation. The social tensions culminated in the 1967 riots, which prompted the colonial administration under Governor **David Trench** to reevaluate its laissez-faire approach to social and economic policy. Pressure mounted from both local workers and progressive figures to establish a legal framework that could prevent industrial unrest by addressing core grievances regarding worker welfare.

## What happened
In **1968**, the Legislative Council passed the Employment Ordinance (《僱傭條例》). The landmark legislation aimed to codify and improve basic employment conditions. It established statutory regulations governing employment contracts, termination procedures, notice periods, and the payment of wages. Over subsequent years, amendments progressively expanded its scope to include provisions for sickness allowances, severance pay, and paid annual leave, thereby laying the groundwork for modern Hong Kong labor law.

## Significance
Historians view the Employment Ordinance as a crucial turning point in Hong Kong's labor history, marking the transition from unregulated exploitation to a regulated labor market. While it did not immediately satisfy all demands of the labor movement, it institutionalized the government's role as a mediator in labor disputes. The ordinance set a precedent for subsequent welfare and labor reforms during the tenure of Governor **Murray MacLehose**, altering the relationship between the colonial state, employers, and the working population.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Viewed the ordinance as a pragmatic and necessary measure to maintain social stability and undercut the influence of pro-communist agitators by addressing legitimate worker grievances.
- **Local Labor and Left-wing Groups**: Argued that the initial legislation was too modest and heavily favored employers, though it represented a hard-fought step toward legal recognition and protection for workers.
- **Business and Industrial Sector**: Initially expressed concern that increased statutory regulations and costs would harm Hong Kong's global economic competitiveness, though many later adapted to the standardized rules.
