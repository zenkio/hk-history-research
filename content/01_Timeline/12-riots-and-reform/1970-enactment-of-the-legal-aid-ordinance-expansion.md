## Background
Prior to the 1970s, formal legal aid in Hong Kong (香港) was extremely limited, leaving indigent residents largely unable to afford representation in court. Following the social unrest of **1966** and **1967**, the colonial administration recognized an urgent need to reform public services, curb systemic grievances, and enhance institutional trust among the working class. 

## What happened
Building upon initial legislative steps taken in **1967**, the government enacted an expanded Legal Aid Ordinance in **1970**. This measure broadened the scope of financial assistance for litigants, enabling qualifying individuals to secure professional legal counsel for a wider range of civil and, subsequently, criminal proceedings. The establishment of a dedicated Legal Aid Department operationalized these services, separating them somewhat from the traditional police and judicial machinery.

## Significance
The expansion of legal aid is widely regarded as a foundational pillar of the broader reform era that preceded Governor **Murray MacLehose**'s tenure. By bridging the justice gap for underprivileged communities, the policy contributed to a gradual stabilization of social relations in Hong Kong, fostering a growing perception of procedural fairness within the colonial legal system.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Viewed the reform as a progressive modernization of governance, demonstrating the administration's commitment to the rule of law and the welfare of all subjects following the turbulent late 1960s.
- **Local Hong Kong residents and grassroots advocates**: Generally welcomed the measures as a long-overdue protection against institutional inequality, though many community organizers argued that financial thresholds remained too restrictive for the poorest workers.
