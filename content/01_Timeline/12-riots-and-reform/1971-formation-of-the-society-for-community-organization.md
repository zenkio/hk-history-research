## Background

Following the social unrest of **1966** and **1967**, the British colonial administration in Hong Kong recognized the urgent need to address deep-seated public grievances regarding housing, labor conditions, and social welfare. Rapid post-war industrialization and a massive influx of refugees had strained urban infrastructure, leaving many residents living in squatter settlements and tenement buildings without adequate public services. Within this environment of urban deprivation, religious and social activists began exploring new models of community development and rights-based advocacy to bridge the gap between the populace and the government.

## What happened

In **1971**, the **Society for Community Organization (SOCO)** (香港社區組織協會) was established by social activists and religious figures, notably including Catholic clergy influenced by Vatican II and liberation theology. Rather than relying solely on traditional top-down charity, SOCO adopted a grassroots mobilization approach. Organizers went directly into squatter areas, resettlement estates, and industrial tenements to help residents identify common problems—such as exorbitant rents, poor sanitation, unsafe working conditions, and inadequate compensation—and form self-help mutual aid committees. By training local leaders to voice their own demands collectively, SOCO facilitated rent strikes, petitions, and public demonstrations, shifting the paradigm of social welfare in Hong Kong toward active citizen participation and rights consciousness.

## Significance

The formation of SOCO in **1971** represented a watershed moment in Hong Kong's civil society development. It coincided with the reformist governance of **Governor Murray MacLehose** (麥理浩), who took office in **1971** and subsequently launched sweeping initiatives in public housing, education, and anti-corruption. While the colonial government expanded the bureaucratic apparatus for social welfare, grassroots groups like SOCO acted as an external pressure mechanism, ensuring that official policies genuinely addressed the needs of the poorest segments of society. Later scholarship highlights how SOCO laid the institutional and ideological groundwork for modern Hong Kong human rights advocacy and social work practice, fostering a distinct local identity centered on civic engagement and social justice.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Officials initially viewed grassroots organizing with caution, fearing it could reignite the political instability seen in the late 1960s, but eventually engaged with community groups as channels for feedback and gauges of public sentiment.
- **Local grassroots and social activists**: Activists saw the founding of SOCO as an essential breakthrough that broke a culture of political silence, empowering ordinary citizens to demand dignity, basic welfare, and structural accountability from the authorities.
- **Beijing and pro-establishment perspective**: Historical accounts from mainland-aligned perspectives often emphasize the broader anti-imperialist awakening of Hong Kong workers and youth during this era, viewing local mobilization through the lens of growing national consciousness alongside socioeconomic demands.
