## Background
For decades, family law in colonial Hong Kong operated under a complex pluralistic system that attempted to accommodate traditional Chinese customs alongside British law. Under Qing legal traditions, which were largely preserved for local Chinese residents, men could legally take concubines (妾) alongside their principal wife (妻). However, these customary practices lacked formal registration and often left women and children in vulnerable legal and financial positions upon the death or desertion of the household head.

By the late 1960s, rapid industrialization, changing social norms, and increasing pressure from local reform groups—such as the Hong Kong Council of Women—made the legal pluralism regarding marriage increasingly untenable. Activists and progressive legal scholars argued that the continuation of concubinage undermined gender equality and failed to protect women within modern urban society.

## What happened
In **1971**, the colonial legislature passed the Matrimonial Causes Ordinance Reform. This landmark legislation officially abolished the institution of concubinage in Hong Kong and mandated monogamy as the strict legal requirement for all future marriages. Furthermore, the ordinance introduced more equitable grounds for divorce and aimed to secure better maintenance and property rights for women and children.

The reform sought to align Hong Kong's domestic legal framework with contemporary international standards and the shifting expectations of an increasingly urbanized local population, effectively ending the official recognition of customary concubinage that had persisted since the early days of British administration.

## Significance
The **1971** ordinance is widely regarded by historians as a foundational moment in the advancement of women's rights and family law modernization in Hong Kong. It dismantled a centuries-old patriarchal legal structure, providing formal legal protection and status to wives while rendering concubinage legally obsolete.

At the same time, the transition highlighted the complex tensions between colonial governance and traditional Chinese custom, particularly concerning property inheritance and family succession, issues that would continue to spark legal and social debates in the New Territories for decades to follow.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Colonial officials viewed the reform as a necessary step to modernize Hong Kong's legal infrastructure, bring local laws into alignment with international human rights standards, and demonstrate progressive governance during the turbulent post-1967 era.
- **Local Women's Rights Advocates**: Reformers and women's organizations celebrated the ordinance as a long-overdue victory for gender equality, arguing that it finally dismantled an outdated patriarchal system that had marginalized women and children for generations.
- **Traditional New Territories Communities**: Many rural residents and traditional lineage leaders viewed the forced imposition of monogamy and the abolition of customary practices as an unwarranted colonial interference in long-standing Chinese family customs and inheritance traditions.
