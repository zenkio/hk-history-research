## Background

Following the 1967 riots, the colonial administration under **Governor Murray MacLehose** (**麥理浩**) initiated wide-ranging social, administrative, and economic reforms to improve governance and stability in Hong Kong. As part of the broader effort to develop the New Territories (新界) beyond traditional rural frameworks, the government sought to address housing shortages and regulate haphazard rural building while managing relations with indigenous inhabitants.

## What happened

On **December 1, 1972** (**一九七二年十二月一日**), the Hong Kong government officially implemented the **Small House Policy** (**小型屋宇政策**, commonly known as *Ding Yu* or 丁屋). Under this policy, any male indigenous villager descended through the male line from a recognized 1898 resident of a New Territories village was granted a one-time, lifelong right to apply for permission to build a small house (typically up to three stories high) on his own land or on government land via private treaty grants, often at concessional premiums.

## Significance

The policy became a cornerstone of New Territories land policy, balancing colonial administrative control with the appeasement of rural power structures like the **Heung Yee Kuk** (**鄉議局**). However, it rapidly transformed into a contentious political and social issue. Later scholars and critics argue that the policy consumes disproportionate amounts of scarce land, lacks environmental sustainability, and entrenches hereditary privileges that clash with modern egalitarian principles. Conversely, indigenous communities view it as an inalienable right protected under historical assurances and land customs.

## Perspectives

- **Indigenous New Territories Communities**: Villagers and bodies such as the Heung Yee Kuk view the policy as a fundamental customary right and a formal commitment made by the colonial government to protect indigenous interests upon the 1898 lease of the New Territories.
- **Urban Critics and Later Scholarship**: Urban concern groups and academic researchers frequently criticize the policy as an outdated, gender-discriminatory privilege that exacerbates Hong Kong's severe land and housing shortages while benefiting private developers through suspected 'Ding rights' trafficking.
