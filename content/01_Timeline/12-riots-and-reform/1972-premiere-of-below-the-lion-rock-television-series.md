## Background
In the wake of the turbulent **1966 and 1967 riots**, the colonial government under Governor **Murray MacLehose** initiated sweeping social, housing, and administrative reforms aimed at improving public welfare and fostering a greater sense of belonging. Concurrently, **Radio Television Hong Kong (RTHK)**, then operating as the Government Information Services' broadcasting division, sought innovative ways to communicate with the public and address pressing social issues. As the influx of refugees from mainland China settled into newly established public housing estates, urban neighborhoods faced challenges related to poverty, labor conditions, and cultural dislocation.

## What happened
On **October 18, 1972**, RTHK broadcast the premiere of the television drama series **"Below the Lion Rock" (獅子山下)**. Initially produced as a 15-minute black-and-white program using 16mm film, the series later transitioned to color and expanded into longer narrative formats. The show depicted the daily struggles, resilience, and interpersonal relationships of ordinary Hong Kong residents living under the shadow of Lion Rock (獅子山). Directors and writers utilized realistic settings—such as resettlement blocks, street markets, and factories—to portray themes of upward mobility, mutual aid, and familial perseverance amidst hardship.

## Significance
The series is widely regarded by cultural historians as a catalyst for the crystallization of a unique **Hong Kong local identity**. While early episodes sometimes carried explicit public information or government-sanctioned messaging, the program gradually evolved into an independent artistic platform that critically engaged with social inequities. The titular phrase and its associated spirit of perseverance became a central metaphor for the collective ethos of the population. However, scholars continue to debate the extent to which the "Lion Rock spirit" genuinely reflected organic grassroots solidarity versus a state-promoted narrative designed to depoliticize social grievances and strengthen allegiance to the colonial administration.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Officials viewed the series as an effective public communication tool that could bridge the gap between the government and the populace, promote civic awareness, and alleviate social tensions following the unrest of the late 1960s.
- **Local Hong Kong cultural scholars**: Many researchers argue that the program provided an authentic mirror for grassroots experiences, allowing ordinary citizens to see their daily lives dignified on screen and fostering a cohesive, non-partisan local identity.
- **Critical and revisionist historians**: Some contemporary analysts suggest that the emphasis on individual perseverance in the series served to romanticize systemic poverty and deflect attention away from structural inequalities inherent in colonial governance.
