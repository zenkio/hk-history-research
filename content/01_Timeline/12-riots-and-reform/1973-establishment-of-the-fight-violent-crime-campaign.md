## Background
In the early **1970s**, Hong Kong faced severe social challenges, including rapid urbanization, poverty, and rampant crime syndicates known as triads (三合會). Public trust in law enforcement was exceptionally low due to systemic corruption within the Royal Hong Kong Police Force (香港警察隊). Violent crime rates, extortion, and syndicate-controlled vice operations plagued local communities, creating an atmosphere of widespread insecurity.

## What happened
On **February 1, 1973**, the colonial administration under Governor **Murray MacLehose** (麥理浩) officially launched the **Fight Violent Crime Campaign** (撲滅暴力罪行運動). The initiative relied heavily on a massive public relations blitz, utilizing television, radio, posters, and community committees to encourage citizens to report crimes and cooperate with authorities. Banners and advertisements featuring the iconic slogan "A city depends on all of us" flooded the streets. Concurrently, the establishment of the Independent Commission Against Corruption (**ICAC**, 廉政公署) in **1974** reinforced the campaign's objectives by targeting the institutional roots of police-triad collusion.

## Significance
Historians generally view the campaign as a watershed moment in the relationship between the Hong Kong populace and the colonial state. By successfully fostering community-wide participation, the government curbed overt triad dominance and gradually rehabilitated the public image of law enforcement. The mobilization techniques pioneered during this era laid the groundwork for future civic campaigns and fostered a distinct local Hong Kong identity tied to civic order and community vigilance.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial perspective**: Colonial officials framed the campaign as a triumph of progressive governance and community policing, demonstrating the administration's capacity to restore law and order through cooperative social engineering.
- **Local Hong Kong and New Territories communities**: Many residents welcomed the crackdown on violent crime and extortion, viewing it as a long-overdue relief from daily intimidation, though some remained initially skeptical of police motives due to entrenched corruption.
- **Later scholarship**: Recent academic analyses suggest the campaign was part of a broader strategy by Governor MacLehose to legitimize colonial rule and cultivate civic loyalty in the wake of the turbulent **1967 riots**, using social stability as a substitute for democratization.
