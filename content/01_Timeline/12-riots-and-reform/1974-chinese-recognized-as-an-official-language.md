## Background
For the majority of the colonial period, English remained the sole official language of government and the judiciary in Hong Kong, despite the vast majority of the population speaking Cantonese. This created a profound linguistic barrier between the predominantly Chinese-speaking populace and the administration. By the late **1960s** and early **1970s**, shifting social attitudes, the aftermath of the **1967** left-wing riots, and a rising sense of local identity culminated in the **Chinese Language Movement** (中文運動). University students, notably from the **Chinese University of Hong Kong** (香港中文大學), spearheaded protests demanding that Chinese be granted official status to ensure equal access to justice, public services, and government employment.

## What happened
On **March 13, 1974**, the colonial government enacted the Official Languages Ordinance. This landmark legislation officially declared both English and Chinese as the official languages of Hong Kong for communication between the government and the public. While the ordinance marked a major legislative breakthrough, it initially specified that both languages possessed equal status without explicitly defining which specific Chinese topolect or script was standard, though Cantonese remained the spoken lingua franca and traditional Chinese characters were used in official written communications.

## Significance
The elevation of Chinese to an official language was a critical turning point in the evolution of Hong Kong governance, laying the groundwork for broader administrative reforms under Governor **MacLehose** (麥理浩). It improved communication between the state and society, enhanced the representation of local Chinese speakers within the civil service, and signaled a departure from exclusive British colonial governance toward a more inclusive administrative approach that acknowledged the cultural identity of the majority population.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Viewed the reform as a pragmatic modernization step necessary to maintain administrative efficiency, social stability, and public trust in the wake of the 1967 unrest.
- **Local students and activists**: Regarded the legislation as a hard-won victory for linguistic equality, civil rights, and local dignity against decades of institutional colonial discrimination.
