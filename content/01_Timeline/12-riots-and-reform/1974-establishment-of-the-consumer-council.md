## Background
During the early 1970s, Hong Kong underwent a profound socio-economic transformation. Following the social unrest of the late 1960s, the administration under Governor **Murray MacLehose** prioritized the stabilization of society through improved social welfare and institutional reforms. As the manufacturing sector matured and the middle class expanded, the public became increasingly exposed to sophisticated retail practices. Simultaneously, the lack of formal regulation led to concerns over unfair trade practices, such as deceptive advertising and inconsistent pricing, which began to draw public criticism.

## What happened
On **April 15, 1974**, the colonial government formally established the Consumer Council (消費者委員會). Created as a statutory body, its primary mission was to protect and promote the interests of consumers by investigating complaints and advising the government on legislative needs. Unlike some previous administrative committees, the Council was empowered to disseminate information to the public, notably through its monthly magazine *Choice* (選擇), which provided independent product testing and price comparisons. The Council operated with a degree of independence from the government, acting as a mediator between the private sector and the purchasing public.

## Significance
The establishment of the Consumer Council is often viewed as a pivotal step in the evolution of Hong Kong’s governance model under the **MacLehose** era. By shifting towards institutionalized consumer protection, the administration sought to address the grievances of a population transitioning from a subsistence mindset to one defined by modern consumerism. Scholars note that this development reflected the government's broader strategy of 'administrative absorption of politics,' where the state provided tangible public services and regulatory protections to foster social legitimacy. While the Council initially focused on education and advocacy, its existence laid the groundwork for later, more robust trade practices legislation, signaling a move away from the traditional policy of 'positive non-interventionism' in areas where market failures directly impacted the welfare of the growing populace.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: The government viewed the Council as a practical and necessary tool to improve the quality of life for residents and reduce social friction. It was framed as a progressive administrative reform that demonstrated the colonial state's commitment to modern governance and the well-being of its subjects.
- **Local Consumer Advocacy Groups**: Many local activists welcomed the Council but argued that its initial mandate lacked teeth, as it could only advise and not enforce. They pushed for stronger legislative powers to penalize corporations, reflecting a desire for more direct and proactive state intervention in the market.
