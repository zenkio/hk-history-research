## Background
By the late 1960s and early 1970s, corruption within the Hong Kong government—particularly the Royal Hong Kong Police Force—was systemic and pervasive. Bribes, known locally as "tea money" (茶錢), were routinely required to secure basic public services, avoid police harassment, or operate illegal enterprises. Public outrage reached a boiling point following the **1973** scandal involving **Peter Godber** (葛柏), a senior police superintendent who fled Hong Kong while under investigation for massive unexplained wealth. His escape triggered massive street protests and public demands for decisive government action.

## What happened
Recognizing that existing anti-corruption units within the police were ineffective due to institutional conflicts of interest, Governor **Murray MacLehose** (麥理浩) bypassed the traditional police structure. On **February 15, 1974**, he established the Independent Commission Against Corruption (ICAC) (廉政公署) under the Independent Commission Against Corruption Ordinance. Operating independently of the civil service and directly under the Governor, the ICAC was granted sweeping powers of investigation, arrest, and detention. The agency adopted a three-pronged approach combining rigorous investigation, preventative structural reforms within government departments, and community relations campaigns to shift public attitudes.

## Significance
The creation of the ICAC marked a watershed moment in Hong Kong's institutional history. Over the subsequent decade, the agency successfully dismantled major syndicates within the police force and civil service, notably confronting entrenched resistance such as the police mutiny in **1977**. By significantly reducing systemic bribery, the ICAC played a crucial role in establishing Hong Kong's reputation for administrative efficiency, rule of law, and a clean business environment, laying the groundwork for its subsequent economic boom.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: The establishment of the ICAC was framed as a bold and decisive intervention by Governor Murray MacLehose to restore public trust in the rule of law and modernize colonial governance. Officials viewed the independent agency as a necessary structural shield to cleanse the civil service and ensure Hong Kong's long-term economic stability.
- **Local Hong Kong community and public**: For the general populace, the ICAC was seen as a long-overdue response to years of systemic extortion and police corruption that directly affected daily life. The agency's early successes provided a rare instance where ordinary citizens felt the colonial government was genuinely responsive to grassroots demands for justice.
