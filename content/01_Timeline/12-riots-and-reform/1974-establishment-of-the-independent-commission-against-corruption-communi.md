## Background
By the early **1970s**, corruption had become endemic across nearly all levels of the Hong Kong government and civil society. Bribes, locally known as "tea money" (飲茶錢), were routinely required to secure public services, police protection, medical care, and even fire services. Public tolerance for such graft reached a breaking point following the **1973** scandal involving **Peter Godber** (葛柏), a British-born Chief Superintendent of Police who fled the territory while under investigation for corruption.

Faced with mounting public outrage and a severe crisis of governance, Governor **Sir Murray MacLehose** (麥理浩爵士) recognized that traditional policing methods alone could not dismantle systemic corruption. He resolved to create an independent anti-corruption agency. On **February 15, 1974**, the Independent Commission Against Corruption (ICAC) (廉政公署) was officially established under the Independent Commission Against Corruption Ordinance.

## What happened
From its inception, the ICAC was deliberately structured into three distinct branches: Operations, Investigation, and the Community Relations Department (CRD) (社區關係處). While the Operations branch focused on enforcement and arrest, the CRD was tasked with tackling the cultural roots of corruption through public education, prevention, and community outreach.

The CRD adopted a three-pronged strategy combining investigation, prevention, and education. Early community relations officers launched extensive campaigns to educate the public on their legal rights and the detrimental effects of bribery. They established district offices across Hong Kong Island, Kowloon, and the New Territories (新界) to make the agency accessible to ordinary citizens. Through schools, mass media, and community associations, the department worked to transform public perception, shifting the prevailing local attitude from passive acceptance of bribery to active rejection.

## Significance
The creation of the CRD marked a pioneering milestone in global anti-corruption strategies, demonstrating that sustainable reform required winning the hearts and minds of the populace rather than relying solely on criminal prosecution. By fundamentally shifting societal attitudes toward graft, the department helped restore public trust in civil institutions and contributed significantly to Hong Kong's transformation into one of the world's least corrupt jurisdictions by the end of the twentieth century.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Colonial officials viewed the establishment of the ICAC and its Community Relations Department as a triumph of administrative reform, essential for modernizing the civil service, stabilizing British rule, and restoring institutional legitimacy after the tumult of the 1960s.
- **Local Hong Kong Public**: Many ordinary residents initially viewed the new anti-corruption efforts with skepticism due to a long history of unchecked police corruption, but over time came to embrace the CRD's educational outreach as a genuine effort to protect citizens from extortion.
- **Hong Kong Police Force**: The establishment of the ICAC caused severe friction within the police force, where officers felt unfairly targeted by aggressive investigations, leading to protests and a tense standoff in **1977** before an amnesty curtailed some retroactive inquiries.
