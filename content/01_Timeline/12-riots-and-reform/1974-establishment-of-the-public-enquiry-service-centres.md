## Background
Following the social unrest of **1966** and **1967**, the British colonial administration recognized an urgent need to reform its governance and improve channels of communication with the local population. Governor **Murray MacLehose** initiated a series of administrative and social reforms aimed at fostering a greater sense of belonging and addressing public grievances before they escalated into open protest. The City District Officer scheme, introduced earlier, laid the groundwork for more localized government presence, but there remained a distinct need for accessible physical locations where ordinary residents could seek information, submit complaints, and navigate bureaucratic procedures.

## What happened
In **1974**, the government rolled out the Public Enquiry Service Centres (民政處諮詢服務中心) across various districts in the urban areas and later the New Territories. These centers were designed to serve as a one-stop shop for citizens interacting with the state. Staffed by civil servants trained in public relations and local administration, the centers provided information on government policies, distributed official publications, and accepted statutory declarations and applications for various public services. They offered a direct, face-to-face interface between the colonial bureaucracy and a populace that had historically viewed the administration as distant and aloof.

## Significance
The establishment of the Public Enquiry Service Centres marked a notable shift in the governing style of the Hong Kong government toward a more consultative and responsive administration. By decentralizing government information and making bureaucratic processes more transparent, the administration sought to preempt social instability and build local legitimacy. While the centers did not grant citizens direct democratic representation, they contributed significantly to the routinization of petitioning and public consultation that came to characterize the MacLehose era.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Viewed the centers as a progressive administrative innovation that successfully democratized access to government information, fostered civic trust, and modernized the civil service to better serve the public.
- **Local Hong Kong Communities**: Welcomed the practical utility of the centers in navigating complex bureaucratic red tape, though some residents remained skeptical of whether the administration genuinely addressed deeper structural grievances.
