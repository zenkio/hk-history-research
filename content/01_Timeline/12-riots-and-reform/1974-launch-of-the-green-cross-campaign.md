## Background
By the early **1970s**, Hong Kong’s economy was heavily dependent on labor-intensive manufacturing industries. Rapid industrialization had outpaced the development of safety regulations, resulting in a surge of workplace injuries. As the colonial administration under **Sir Murray MacLehose** sought to stabilize society following the social unrest of the late **1960s**, public pressure mounted to improve the quality of life and safety conditions for the working class. The government recognized that the existing laissez-faire approach to factory management was no longer sufficient to sustain a stable workforce.

## What happened
In **1974**, the Labour Department (勞工處) initiated the Green Cross Campaign (綠十字運動). The campaign centered on an educational approach, encouraging factory owners and workers to adopt rigorous safety standards. It featured the introduction of the 'Green Cross' symbol, which was displayed prominently in industrial areas and on factory floors to signify a commitment to accident-free operations. The initiative promoted the formation of safety committees and the dissemination of training materials designed to mitigate the risks associated with heavy machinery and hazardous factory environments. By emphasizing shared responsibility, the government aimed to reduce the frequency of industrial mishaps without immediately imposing heavy-handed legislative penalties that might have disrupted industrial productivity.

## Significance
The Green Cross Campaign represented a significant transition in Hong Kong’s governance. It illustrated a move toward a more interventionist state that took active responsibility for the well-being of its citizens. While earlier policies focused primarily on economic output, the campaign signaled that the colonial government viewed industrial safety as a matter of social stability. Later scholarship often debates whether the campaign was a genuine effort to empower workers or a strategic attempt to deflect criticism and forestall more radical labor union demands. Despite its limitations, the campaign served as a precursor to more robust occupational safety and health legislation enacted in the subsequent decades.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Government**: The administration viewed the campaign as a pragmatic and collaborative method to foster a 'safety-first' culture. They emphasized voluntary compliance and partnership between the state, employers, and employees to ensure the continuity of economic growth.
- **Labor Unions and Local Workers**: Many workers felt the campaign was largely symbolic and did not go far enough to address the root causes of accidents, such as long working hours and poor maintenance of machinery. Critics frequently pointed out that the lack of strict enforcement or financial penalties meant that factory owners were not sufficiently incentivized to invest in safer infrastructure.
