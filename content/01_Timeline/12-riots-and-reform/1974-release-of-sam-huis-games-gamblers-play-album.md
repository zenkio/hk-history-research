## Background

Prior to the mid-1970s, the Hong Kong popular music market was dominated by Western rock and roll, Mandarin-language pop songs imported from Taiwan, and traditional Cantonese opera extracts. Cantonese colloquialisms were widely regarded by the educated elite as vulgar and unsuitable for serious artistic expression. However, rapid socioeconomic changes, rapid urbanization, and a growing sense of local Hong Kong identity during the 1970s created a rising demand for cultural products that directly reflected the daily lives, slang, and concerns of ordinary working-class residents.

## What happened

On **October 17, 1974**, **Sam Hui** (許冠傑) released the music album **Games Gamblers Play** (鬼馬雙星), which served as the soundtrack to the comedy film of the same name starring the Hui brothers. The title track and other songs on the record utilized lively, colloquial Cantonese lyrics combined with upbeat Western rock and folk-pop instrumentation. The album achieved unprecedented commercial success, breaking sales records across the territory and instantly capturing the public imagination.

## Significance

The massive popularity of the album is widely credited by music historians with breaking the social stigma previously attached to the Cantonese language in mass media. By demonstrating that Cantonese could be used to create sophisticated, commercially viable, and culturally resonant pop music, **Sam Hui** paved the way for the golden age of Cantopop. This shift contributed significantly to the elevation of Cantonese as a prestigious cultural medium and fostered a distinct local Hong Kong consciousness separate from both British colonial culture and mainland Chinese politics.
