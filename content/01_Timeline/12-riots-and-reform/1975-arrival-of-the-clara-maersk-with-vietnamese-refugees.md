## Background

Following the fall of Saigon and the end of the Vietnam War in **April 1975**, thousands of people began fleeing Vietnam by sea. The geopolitical landscape of Southeast Asia shifted dramatically, creating a massive humanitarian crisis as civilians sought refuge from the new communist administration.

## What happened

On **May 4, 1975**, the Danish-registered cargo vessel **Clara Maersk** entered Hong Kong waters carrying **3,700** Vietnamese refugees who had been rescued at sea. The colonial government, led by Governor **Sir Murray MacLehose** (麥理浩), faced an unprecedented logistical and humanitarian challenge. Initially, the authorities denied the ship permission to immediately disembark the passengers, leading to a tense standoff in the harbor while diplomatic negotiations ensued regarding responsibility for the displaced people. Eventually, permission was granted for the refugees to land temporarily, setting a precedent for handling subsequent boat arrivals.

## Significance

The arrival of the **Clara Maersk** is widely recognized by historians as the definitive beginning of Hong Kong's role as a "port of first asylum." Over the next twenty-five years, the territory would receive over **200,000** Vietnamese boat people and refugees, leading to the establishment of numerous detention camps and protracted social, economic, and political debates that lasted until the final closure of the camps around **2000**.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Government**: Faced with an unexpected humanitarian crisis, colonial authorities viewed the initial arrival as an emergency requiring temporary accommodation, while subsequently urging the international community to share the burden of permanent resettlement.
- **Local Hong Kong Community**: While many residents initially showed sympathy for the refugees' plight, the continuous influx over subsequent years caused mounting social anxiety, strain on public resources, and local friction regarding security and camp locations.
