## Background

By the mid-**1970s**, under the administration of Governor **MacLehose** (麥理浩), Hong Kong was undergoing sweeping social reforms to improve public welfare and governance following the turbulence of the **1960s**. Despite these modernizing efforts, legal frameworks retained numerous colonial-era statutes and traditional customs—particularly regarding New Territories land inheritance and employment practices—that disadvantaged women. The designation of **1975** as International Women's Year by the United Nations placed renewed global and local pressure on the British colonial administration to review gender equality.

## What happened

On **October 12, 1975**, the Hong Kong government moved to advance several legislative and administrative adjustments aimed at dismantling legal disparities. While comprehensive anti-discrimination laws took years to fully evolve, this period marked a crucial turning point where equal pay for equal work in the civil service began to gain traction, and traditional practices governing female property ownership and familial rights faced formal scrutiny. Activists, local women's associations, and Legislative Council members lobbied for broader protections against gender-based discrimination in both public and private sectors.

## Significance

These mid-1970s initiatives laid the groundwork for modern gender equality legislation in Hong Kong. By aligning local policy with international human rights standards promoted by the United Nations, the administration signaled a departure from traditional laissez-faire governance on social issues. However, the pace of reform varied significantly across different communities, highlighting the tension between evolving urban standards and customary laws.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Officials viewed the reforms as a progressive alignment with international standards set by the United Nations, helping to modernize Hong Kong's legal system and bolster government legitimacy.
- **New Territories Indigenous Communities**: Rural and indigenous groups often perceived legal interventions into family and property rights as a disruption of traditional customary laws and patrilineal heritage protected under the New Territories Ordinance.
- **Later Scholarship**: Modern historians debate whether these reforms constituted genuine structural liberation or were largely symbolic measures designed to appease international pressure while preserving the underlying social hierarchy.
