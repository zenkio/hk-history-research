## Background
During the late **1960s** and early **1970s**, Hong Kong faced extreme pressure on housing and public services due to rapid population growth. Following the social unrest of **1967**, the colonial administration, particularly under Governor **Murray MacLehose**, recognized that the traditional urban cores on Hong Kong Island and the Kowloon Peninsula were severely overcrowded. Planners looked to the New Territories, specifically the **Sha Tin (沙田)** valley, as a viable space for large-scale development to accommodate hundreds of thousands of residents.

## What happened
The development of Sha Tin was an ambitious engineering feat involving significant reclamation of the **Shing Mun River (城門河)** estuary. In **1976**, the first residents began moving into newly constructed public housing estates. The project was designed to be self-contained, integrating residential blocks with commercial centers, schools, and transport links. By shifting the geographical focus, the colonial government effectively expanded the functional limits of the metropolitan area beyond the 'Lion Rock' mountain range. Construction continued throughout the late **1970s** and early **1980s**, turning what was once a rural valley of scattered villages and agricultural land into a sprawling urban satellite city.

## Significance
Sha Tin served as a model for subsequent New Town projects across Hong Kong. It represented a technological and administrative shift, showcasing the government's ability to reshape the landscape to meet societal demands. The transition from a rural environment to an urban one fundamentally altered the socio-economic structure of the area. While the project provided much-needed housing for a burgeoning population, it also necessitated the dismantling of traditional village lifeways and agrarian practices that had defined the region for generations. Historians remain divided on whether this 'New Town' approach primarily served the goal of social stability or if it was an efficient mechanism to industrialize and manage the territory's growing labor force.

## Perspectives

- **Colonial Administration**: The administration viewed the project as an essential modernization effort to improve living conditions and stave off social discontent. Officials emphasized the success of centralized planning in delivering efficient housing and infrastructure for a growing population.
- **New Territories Indigenous Communities**: Local village communities often viewed the reclamation and development as an intrusion upon their ancestral lands and cultural heritage. Many residents felt that the rapid urbanization prioritized the needs of urban migrants over the established rural populations, leading to disputes over land rights and compensation.
