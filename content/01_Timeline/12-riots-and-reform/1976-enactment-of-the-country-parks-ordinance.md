## Background
By the mid-1970s, Hong Kong experienced unprecedented economic growth, rapid industrialization, and massive public housing expansion. The urban areas of Victoria Harbour and Kowloon were becoming increasingly dense, while New Towns were rapidly transforming the rural landscape of the New Territories (新界). Amidst these pressures, conservationists, hikers, and colonial officials grew concerned about the unregulated destruction of forests, water catchments, and hillsides due to unchecked development, hill fires, and quarrying.

## What happened
On **August 16, 1976**, the Legislative Council enacted the Country Parks Ordinance. The legislation provided the statutory basis for the designation, development, and management of country parks and special areas. It established the Country Parks Authority and the Country Parks Board to oversee conservation, recreation, and educational use. Over the subsequent decades, the government progressively designated extensive areas of land—ultimately covering around 40% of Hong Kong's total land mass—safeguarding mountain ranges, reservoirs, and coastal zones from urban encroachment.

## Significance
The ordinance played a defining role in shaping Hong Kong's unique geographic and urban character, creating a striking juxtaposition of high-density urban environments immediately adjacent to protected wilderness. While initially conceived partly to protect vital water catchment areas and prevent soil erosion, the system evolved into an essential recreational outlet for the urban populace, profoundly influencing the territory's quality of life and environmental identity.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Colonial officials viewed the ordinance as a progressive environmental achievement and a key component of Governor **Murray MacLehose**'s wider reform program to improve social welfare, public health, and recreational infrastructure.
- **New Territories indigenous communities**: Some rural and indigenous inhabitants expressed concern over how statutory restrictions within designated parks might affect traditional land use rights, customary practices, and private land ownership within the New Territories.
- **Urban residents and conservationists**: Urban citizens and hiking groups largely welcomed the legislation as a vital safeguard for green spaces, though later commentators sometimes noted tensions between conservation mandates and recreational development.
