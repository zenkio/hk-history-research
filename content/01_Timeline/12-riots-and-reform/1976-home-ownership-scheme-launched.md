## Background
By the mid-1970s, the Hong Kong government's massive public housing program, sparked by the Shek Kip Mei fire in 1953, had successfully housed a significant portion of the population in rental estates. However, rapid economic growth created a growing lower-to-middle income bracket that earned too much to qualify for traditional public rental housing, yet could not afford soaring private sector property prices. Recognizing the social instability caused by inadequate housing and inflation, the administration under Governor **Sir Murray MacLehose** (**麥理浩**) sought new ways to address structural housing demands and foster a more stable society following the turbulent **1967 riots** (**六七暴動**).

## What happened
On **December 28, 1976**, the government officially introduced the Home Ownership Scheme (**居者有其屋計劃**), commonly known as HOS. Administered by the Housing Authority (**香港房屋委員會**), the initiative was designed to build subsidized apartments and sell them to eligible families at prices below market value. The first phase of construction involved developments in various urban and new town locations, offering citizens an alternative tenure path between public rental housing and private homeownership. Eligible buyers were typically required to meet income limits and adhere to resale restrictions to prevent immediate speculative flipping in the volatile property market.

## Significance
The launch of the Home Ownership Scheme became a cornerstone of Hong Kong's domestic policy, fundamentally reshaping the urban landscape and creating a property-owning middle class. While initially conceived as a supplementary measure to public rental housing, HOS eventually became a permanent fixture of local governance, influencing generational wealth accumulation and social mobility. Later scholarship often debates whether the scheme successfully insulated citizens from market volatility or inadvertently tied the population's economic well-being too closely to the fluctuations of the real estate sector.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Viewed the scheme as a progressive social reform necessary to stabilize the colony, foster a stake in society, and alleviate the housing pressures that contributed to past civil unrest.
- **Local real estate developers**: Initially expressed concern that government-subsidized housing would distort the property market and compete directly with private sector developments, though a complementary relationship eventually evolved.
