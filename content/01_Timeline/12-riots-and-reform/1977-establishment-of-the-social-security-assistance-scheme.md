## Background
Prior to the 1970s, social welfare in Hong Kong was largely shouldered by voluntary agencies, religious bodies, and traditional clan associations, with the colonial government limiting its role primarily to disaster relief and modest ad-hoc assistance. Following the social unrest of **1966** and **1967**, and under the progressive administration of Governor **Murray MacLehose** (麥理浩), the government began rethinking its social obligations. Rapid industrialization, urban growth, and the breakdown of traditional family support networks rendered older, fragmented relief systems inadequate for the territory's vulnerable populations.

## What happened
On **April 1, 1977**, the government officially launched the **Social Security Assistance Scheme** (社會保障援助計劃). This consolidated financial safety net brought together several disparate assistance programs under a single administrative framework. Designed to provide non-contributory financial support, the scheme targeted specific demographics deemed unable to work or sustain themselves, notably the elderly, the severely disabled, and the destitute. By establishing standardized criteria and regular monetary payouts funded through general taxation, the initiative transformed government relief from a discretionary charity into a statutory right for qualifying residents.

## Significance
The introduction of the scheme is widely viewed by historians as a watershed moment in the evolution of Hong Kong's public administration. It signaled a departure from the traditional laissez-faire governance model toward a more interventionist social policy, laying the groundwork for the modern welfare state. While subsequent administrations debated the adequacy of the benefit levels and the strictness of the means tests, the framework established in **1977** endured as a foundational pillar of Hong Kong's social safety net, reflecting a growing recognition of government accountability toward its citizens' basic livelihood.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Officials framed the scheme as a progressive modernization of governance, demonstrating the administration's genuine care for the welfare of all residents and helping to defuse social tensions after the turbulent previous decade.
- **Local social activists and critics**: Grassroots advocates argued that while the scheme was a welcome step, the initial payout levels were insufficient to meet the actual cost of living in urban Hong Kong, viewing the means-testing procedures as overly bureaucratic and restrictive.
- **Later scholarship**: Modern historians analyze the reform as a pragmatic tool for political legitimation, suggesting that expanding state welfare was essential for maintaining social stability and economic growth during the Cold War era.
