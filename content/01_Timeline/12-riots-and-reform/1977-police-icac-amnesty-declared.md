## Background

Established in **February 1974**, the Independent Commission Against Corruption (**ICAC** / 廉政公署) was tasked with eradicating systemic, pervasive corruption within the Hong Kong civil service, particularly the Royal Hong Kong Police Force (**RHKPF** / 香港警察隊). Prior to the ICAC's formation, bribery had been an entrenched part of daily operations in many departments. 

As the ICAC aggressively pursued investigations into past corrupt practices, anxiety and resentment mounted within the police force. Officers felt unfairly targeted for practices that had previously been tolerated or considered institutional norms. Tensions boiled over in **October 1977**, when hundreds of police officers and their supporters marched on ICAC headquarters, leading to violent clashes and open defiance of the colonial government's authority.

## What happened

Faced with a potential breakdown of law and order and a mutinous police force, Governor **Sir Murray MacLehose** (**麥理浩**) took decisive action. On **November 5, 1977**, the Governor used his executive powers under the Letters Patent to announce a partial amnesty. 

Under this declaration, proceedings for corruption offences committed before **January 1, 1977**, would generally not be pursued, provided the offences did not involve violence or major syndicated corruption that was still actively being dismantled. This pragmatic concession successfully defused the immediate crisis, persuading the protesting officers to return to their duties and restoring relative calm to the colony.

## Significance

The **1977 amnesty** remains one of the most controversial episodes in modern Hong Kong history. While it averted a dangerous confrontation between the government and its primary enforcers of law and order, it was viewed by many as a compromise of the foundational principle that no one is above the law. Nonetheless, the ICAC continued its operations against syndicated corruption thereafter, gradually transforming the civil service into one of the most transparent in the region while reshaping the institutional relationship between the government and the police.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Viewed the partial amnesty as a necessary, pragmatic compromise to prevent the collapse of law and order and to avert a violent mutiny within the security forces, allowing long-term anti-corruption reforms to eventually proceed.
- **Local Public and Reform Advocates**: Often criticized the amnesty as a betrayal of justice that capitulated to lawbreakers within the police force, undermining the absolute principle of equality before the law and protecting corrupt officials.
