## Background
In **May 1977**, tensions boiled over at the Precious Blood Golden Jubilee Secondary School (金禧中學). Teachers and students alleged that the school principal and management committee had engaged in financial mismanagement and corruption. The controversy tapped into broader public anxieties regarding institutional accountability, transparency, and the governance of grant schools subsidized by the colonial government.

## What happened
Student and teacher protests escalated into a sustained movement that drew widespread public sympathy and active participation from tertiary-level student unions. Protesters held rallies, petitioned government offices, and demanded structural reforms in how subsidized schools were managed. The colonial administration, led by the Education Department, attempted to mediate the dispute but ultimately decided to close the school in **July 1978** to restore order, displacing hundreds of students and terminating the employment of protesting teachers. This heavy-handed response provoked further outrage, leading to sit-ins, hunger strikes, and broader solidarity demonstrations across the territory.

## Significance
The Golden Jubilee Incident is widely viewed by historians as a watershed moment in Hong Kong's social and political history. It marked a transition in the local student movement from anti-imperialist and nationalist causes of the late 1960s and early 1970s toward domestic, rights-based, and reform-oriented civic engagement. The mobilization trained a new generation of activists, social workers, and politicians who would later shape Hong Kong's civil society during the transitional period leading up to the 1984 Sino-British Joint Declaration.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Government**: Authorities viewed the incident primarily as an administrative and labor dispute within a private, religious-affiliated institution that threatened public order. The Education Department's interventions were framed as pragmatic measures to restore stability and protect students' education, though critics argued the closure was an attempt to suppress political agitation.
- **Students and Teachers**: Participants and supporting local activists regarded the movement as a righteous fight against systemic corruption, authoritarian school management, and bureaucratic indifference. For them, the protests represented a fundamental assertion of citizens' rights to fair administration and freedom of expression under British rule.
- **Later Scholarship**: Recent historical analyses position the incident as a crucial catalyst in the rise of Hong Kong's domestic civil society and identity. Scholars note how the mobilization bridged campus politics with grassroots community concerns, foreshadowing later social movements of the 1980s.
