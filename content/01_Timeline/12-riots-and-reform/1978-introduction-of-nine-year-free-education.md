## Background
Prior to the late 1970s, while primary education had become largely accessible and free, secondary education in Hong Kong remained selective and tuition-based, limiting opportunities for children from lower-income families. Rapid industrialization throughout the 1960s and 1970s created an urgent demand for a more literate, skilled, and adaptable local workforce. Under the administration of Governor **Murray MacLehose** (麥理浩), the colonial government initiated sweeping social reforms in housing, social welfare, and education to address public grievances highlighted by the unrest of the **1966-1967 riots**.

## What happened
On **September 1, 1978**, the government officially implemented nine years of free and compulsory education, combining six years of primary schooling with three years of junior secondary schooling. This policy ensured that every child, regardless of socioeconomic background, was guaranteed access to education up to the age of roughly 15. To accommodate the massive influx of students, the government embarked on an extensive school-building program and expanded subsidies for private and aided schools, while gradually abolishing junior secondary entrance examinations.

## Significance
This educational expansion profoundly elevated the overall literacy and skill level of the local workforce, laying a crucial foundation for Hong Kong's later economic transition into a global financial and service hub. It fostered greater social mobility for the baby-boomer generation and fostered a distinct local Hong Kong identity. However, critics and historians have debated the extent to which the reform was driven by genuine humanitarianism versus economic pragmatism to meet the demands of capital.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Framed the policy as a progressive humanitarian achievement that promoted social harmony, equality of opportunity, and modernization under an enlightened administration.
- **Local working-class communities**: Welcomed the policy as vital financial relief and a tangible pathway for their children to achieve upward social mobility in a competitive society.
- **Critical scholarship**: Argues that the reform was primarily designed to supply a disciplined, adequately educated workforce for industrial and economic restructuring rather than to foster critical civic education.
