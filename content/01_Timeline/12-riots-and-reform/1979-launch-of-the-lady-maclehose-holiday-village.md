## Background
During the late **1970s**, the Hong Kong government under Governor **Murray MacLehose** (麥理浩) pursued an ambitious agenda of social reforms aimed at improving public welfare, housing, and living standards following the social unrest of the **1960s**. Rapid industrialization had concentrated large numbers of working-class families in dense urban environments, creating a pressing need for recreational spaces outside the city.

## What happened
In **1979**, the **Lady MacLehose Holiday Village** (麥理浩夫人度假村) was officially opened in **Sai Kung** (西貢). Situated in a scenic rural area previously used for other purposes, the facility was designed to offer affordable holiday camp accommodations for local residents. It provided organized recreational activities, cottage-style lodgings, and natural surroundings, enabling families of modest means to experience vacations away from the congested urban core.

## Significance
The establishment of the holiday village reflected the colonial administration's growing emphasis on improving the quality of life and fostering a distinct local Hong Kong identity through shared recreational experiences. It set a precedent for subsequent public leisure developments in the New Territories (新界), balancing rapid urban expansion with rural conservation and public accessibility.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Viewed the holiday village as part of a progressive, benevolent policy initiative to uplift working-class standards of living, promote social stability, and demonstrate the government's commitment to public welfare.
- **Local Hong Kong working-class communities**: Welcomed the facility as a rare and affordable opportunity for family leisure and respite from grueling factory labor and cramped urban housing conditions, though demand often outstripped capacity.
