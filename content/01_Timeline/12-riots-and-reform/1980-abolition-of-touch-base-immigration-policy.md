## Background

Following the establishment of the People's Republic of China and subsequent political upheavals such as the Cultural Revolution, Hong Kong experienced waves of migration from mainland China. In 1974, the colonial government introduced the **'Touch Base' policy** (壘上基政策), which stipulated that illegal immigrants who managed to evade border patrols and reach the urban areas of Hong Kong could apply for a Hong Kong identity card, while those intercepted at the border or in the New Territories were immediately repatriated.

By the late 1970s, the sheer volume of arrivals placed immense strain on Hong Kong's housing, infrastructure, medical, and educational systems. Tensions also grew within local communities, particularly in the New Territories (新界), where residents frequently encountered undocumented migrants traversing their lands.

## What happened

On **October 23, 1980**, following consultation with the Executive Council and tacit understanding with authorities in Guangdong, the colonial government abruptly abolished the 'Touch Base' policy. Simultaneously, the government introduced stringent new regulations making it mandatory for all residents aged 15 and above to carry identity cards at all times, with police empowered to conduct spot checks. Anyone found harboring or employing illegal immigrants faced severe penalties. Within days of the announcement, the government implemented a grace period allowing unregistered immigrants already in the urban areas to register, leading to frantic last-minute queues before the final crackdown.

## Significance

The abolition marked a definitive turning point in Hong Kong's immigration history, transforming the border with mainland China from a permeable administrative line into a heavily enforced international boundary. It severely curtailed the influx of undocumented labor and fundamentally reshaped the demographic relationship between Hong Kong and the mainland, paving the way for the later implementation of the One-Way Permit quota system as the primary legal channel for migration.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Viewed the policy change as a pragmatic and urgent necessity to prevent the complete collapse of public services and maintain social order amid unsustainable population growth.
- **Local Hong Kong and New Territories communities**: Expressed relief over the curbing of soaring population pressures and crime, though New Territories residents had long felt disproportionately burdened by the transit of migrants through their rural areas.
- **Mainland Chinese migrants and families**: Regarded the sudden termination with heartbreak and anxiety, as it permanently severed a vital escape route from political and economic hardship on the mainland.
- **Later scholarship**: Analyzes the shift as a crucial moment in the crystallization of a distinct 'Hong Kong identity,' as the territory formally demarcated its population from that of the mainland.
