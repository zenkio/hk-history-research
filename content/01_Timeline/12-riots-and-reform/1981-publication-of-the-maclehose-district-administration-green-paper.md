## Background

By the late **1970s** and early **1980s**, under the governorship of **Sir Murray MacLehose** (麥理浩), the British administration in Hong Kong faced a rapidly growing, urbanized population with rising expectations for public consultation and local governance. Previous advisory structures, such as the Urban Council (市政局) and rural committees in the New Territories, were widely perceived as insufficient for addressing the complex social and infrastructural needs of a modern metropolis. Following the earlier turbulence of the **1966** and **1967** riots, the government sought to bridge the gap between the administration and the grassroots population by improving communication channels and fostering a greater sense of civic belonging.

## What happened

On **June 5, 1981**, the government released the *Green Paper: A Pattern of District Administration in Hong Kong* (《香港地方行政模式綠皮書》). The document proposed a comprehensive overhaul of local administration by dividing the territory into distinct districts and establishing **District Boards** (區議會) across both urban areas and the New Territories. These boards were designed to comprise a mix of official, appointed unofficial members, and eventually elected members, tasked with advising the government on local public works, cultural activities, and social welfare services. Public consultation followed the publication, leading to the formal enactment and rollout of the district administration scheme in subsequent years.

## Significance

The publication of the Green Paper is widely regarded by historians as a foundational milestone in the evolution of Hong Kong's political system. By introducing localized representative bodies, the reform laid the crucial groundwork for wider democratic participation and the eventual introduction of direct elections to the Legislative Council later in the **1980s**. It transformed the previously top-down colonial bureaucracy into a more consultative regime, institutionalizing a framework for civic engagement that persisted well beyond the handover of sovereignty in **1997**.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: Viewed the reforms as a pragmatic and progressive measure to enhance administrative efficiency, gather grassroots feedback, and preempt social unrest by offering controlled political participation without destabilizing executive dominance.
- **Local Pro-Democracy Advocates**: Welcomed the District Boards as a long-overdue step toward representative government, though many critics initially argued that the heavy reliance on appointed members and official figures fell far short of true democracy.
- **New Territories Indigenous Communities**: Evaluated the integration of rural and urban administrative structures with a mixture of caution and pragmatism, keen to ensure that traditional local rights and the influence of bodies like the Heung Yee Kuk (鄉議局) were not marginalized by the new urban-style boards.
