## Background

During the early 1980s, Hong Kong faced mounting economic instability alongside political uncertainty regarding its future. As the Sino-British negotiations over the territory's sovereignty commenced following British Prime Minister **Margaret Thatcher**'s visit to Beijing in September 1982, confidence in the local economy wavered. The floating exchange rate system, adopted in November 1974, proved increasingly vulnerable to speculative capital outflows and political headlines. By **September 1983**, public panic triggered a severe currency crisis, often referred to as "Black Saturday" (黑色星期六), during which the Hong Kong dollar (港元) plummeted to an all-time low of 9.60 against the US dollar.

## What happened

To restore public confidence and stabilize the financial system, Financial Secretary **John Bremridge** announced the implementation of a linked exchange rate system on **October 17, 1983**. Under this newly established peg, the Hong Kong dollar was officially fixed at a rate of 7.80 HKD to 1 USD. The mechanism relied on a unique note-issuing arrangement whereby the territory's two primary note-issuing banks—the Hongkong and Shanghai Banking Corporation and the Standard Chartered Bank—were required to deposit US dollars with the Exchange Fund (外匯基金) at the fixed rate in order to obtain certificates of indebtedness required for printing local banknotes.

## Significance

While initially conceived as an emergency measure to quell panic and curb inflation, the peg successfully restored monetary stability and laid the groundwork for Hong Kong's continued emergence as a premier global financial hub. The system effectively imported US monetary policy, which occasionally created economic friction when local business cycles diverged from those of the United States. Nonetheless, the fixed exchange rate survived numerous regional and global financial crises over the subsequent decades, remaining a foundational pillar of Hong Kong's monetary architecture well beyond the 1997 handover.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Viewed the peg as a necessary and pragmatic technical intervention to rescue a collapsing market, insulating the colonial economy from political hysteria surrounding the Sino-British talks.
- **Beijing and mainland officials**: Generally welcomed the stabilization of Hong Kong's financial markets, recognizing that a stable currency was essential for maintaining economic prosperity and ensuring a smooth transition of sovereignty.
- **Local business and banking communities**: Expressed immense relief at the restoration of exchange rate predictability, though some merchants initially struggled with the sudden deflationary adjustments and high interest rates required to defend the peg.
