## Background
During the early **1980s**, the colonial government faced a significant challenge in developing the New Territories (新界). Unlike the dense urban environments of Kowloon and Hong Kong Island, the New Territories were characterized by complex land rights held by indigenous inhabitants under the Block Crown Lease. As the government sought to expand satellite towns, they needed a mechanism to acquire private land for public infrastructure and housing without causing local unrest or stalling construction.

## What happened
The government introduced the New Territories Land Exchange Entitlements, commonly known as **Letter B** (乙種換地權益書) in **1983**. This policy replaced the earlier, more informal 'Letter A' system. Under this scheme, landowners whose land was resumed by the government for public development were issued 'Letter B' entitlements. These documents granted the holder the right to be allotted a plot of land elsewhere in the developed urban areas, usually within the same new town, once the land was serviced and ready for building. The system essentially allowed landowners to trade rural agricultural plots for serviced urban land, effectively turning landowners into stakeholders in the development process.

## Significance
The introduction of **Letter B** played a central role in the rapid expansion of the New Territories. By creating a tradable instrument, the policy fueled a speculative property boom, as these entitlements could be sold on the open market. While it successfully accelerated the provision of land for public housing and infrastructure, it also transformed the socioeconomic structure of the region. Critics argue it prioritized developers and large-scale landholders, while supporters maintain it was the only pragmatic way to manage land resumption in a region where property rights were historically entrenched and complex. The legacy of these entitlements continued to shape the urban landscape long after the **1997** handover, with the last of these entitlements being phased out in later years.

## Perspectives

- **Colonial Administration**: The administration viewed 'Letter B' as a necessary administrative innovation to resolve the bottleneck of land acquisition in the New Territories. They argued it was a fair, market-based mechanism that incentivized development while providing compensation to landholders.
- **Indigenous Villagers**: Many rural communities viewed the policy with suspicion, fearing that it eroded their ancestral land rights and shifted control from traditional families to corporate developers. Some argued that the valuation of land for entitlements consistently favored the government over the private holder.
- **Property Developers**: Developers often welcomed the policy as it created a secondary market for land, allowing them to accumulate large plots for residential projects. They viewed the entitlements as a critical tool that stabilized supply during a period of high demand.
