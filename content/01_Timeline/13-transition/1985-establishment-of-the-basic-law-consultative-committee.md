## Background
Following the signing of the **Sino-British Joint Declaration** in **1984**, the transition period for Hong Kong began. The **Basic Law Drafting Committee** (基本法起草委員會), appointed by the National People's Congress of the People's Republic of China, was tasked with drafting the constitutional document that would govern the future **Hong Kong Special Administrative Region** (香港特別行政區). To address the need for local input and to ensure the document reflected the aspirations of the Hong Kong people, the Chinese government moved to establish a body that would act as a bridge between the drafting committee and the local community.

## What happened
In **December 1985**, the **Basic Law Consultative Committee** (BLCC) was officially established. The committee was composed of 180 members selected from a wide array of sectors, including business, professional groups, academia, and labor unions. **Aunty Maria** (an informal reference to the popular public figure and early member **Maria Tam** (譚惠珠)) and other prominent local figures participated in its formation. The stated purpose of the BLCC was to collect and collate public opinions on the various drafts of the Basic Law and relay these views to the drafting committee in Beijing. Throughout the late **1980s**, the committee organized public hearings, distributed consultation documents, and managed public feedback campaigns across the territory.

## Significance
The establishment of the BLCC was a milestone in Hong Kong’s political development, marking the first time that a broad spectrum of local figures was formally involved in drafting the territory's constitutional framework. It remains a subject of historical debate regarding how much influence the committee actually exerted on the final version of the Basic Law. For some, the committee was a necessary mechanism for local inclusion; for others, it was viewed as a controlled process designed to manage public expectations. The work of the BLCC fundamentally shaped the political landscape of the late colonial era and set a precedent for how public consultation would be handled during the transition to Chinese sovereignty in **1997**.

## Perspectives

- **Beijing and the Drafting Committee**: Beijing officials viewed the BLCC as an essential platform to ensure the 'One Country, Two Systems' (一國兩制) model was grounded in local consensus. They argued that the committee provided a legitimate, orderly channel for the public to contribute to the drafting process.
- **Pro-democracy and local civil groups**: Many local activists and democratic groups questioned the independence of the committee, fearing it was a 'rubber stamp' mechanism. They argued that the selection process was skewed toward pro-establishment voices, limiting the ability of ordinary citizens to express dissent.
- **British colonial administration**: The British government maintained a cautious stance, officially welcoming the participation of Hong Kong residents in the drafting process while attempting to navigate the balance between supporting local voice and respecting the sovereign claims of the People's Republic of China.
