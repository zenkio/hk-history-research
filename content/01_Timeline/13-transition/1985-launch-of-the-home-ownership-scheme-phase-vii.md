## Background
By the mid-1980s, Hong Kong had entered a delicate and uncertain historical transition following the signing of the Sino-British Joint Declaration in December **1984**, which set the date for the 1997 handover of sovereignty. Amid widespread anxiety regarding the colony's political and economic future, the colonial government sought domestic stability through social welfare and housing policies. The Home Ownership Scheme (HOS), introduced by the Hong Kong Housing Authority (香港房屋委員會) in the late 1970s, was designed to bridge the gap between public rental housing and private property markets, enabling lower-middle-income families to become homeowners.

## What happened
On **September 1, 1985**, the government officially launched Phase VII of the Home Ownership Scheme, releasing several new housing developments for application. These projects provided thousands of subsidized flats across various urban and new town locations, such as Shatin (沙田) and Tuen Mun (屯門). Demand for the flats was substantial, reflecting persistent housing shortages and the high cost of private real estate. The application process attracted widespread public attention, drawing long queues of prospective buyers eager to secure long-term stakes in the territory.

## Significance
The expansion of the HOS through phases like Phase VII served as a crucial instrument for anchoring the emerging local middle class. By facilitating property ownership, the administration aimed to foster a stronger sense of local belonging and stakeholding, mitigating emigration pressures triggered by the impending transfer of sovereignty. While primarily an urban planning initiative, the scheme played a vital role in maintaining domestic confidence and social order during a turbulent geopolitical decade.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Viewed expanded public homeownership as an essential tool to foster social cohesion, stabilize property markets, and demonstrate effective governance during the anxious transition period leading up to 1997.
- **Local Hong Kong residents and applicants**: Saw the subsidized flats as a rare and vital pathway to financial security and upward mobility amid soaring private property prices and political uncertainty.
- **Beijing and pro-Beijing organizations**: Generally supported policies that maintained economic prosperity and social stability in Hong Kong, viewing a settled middle class as beneficial for a smooth transition of sovereignty.
