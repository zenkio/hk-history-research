## Background
By the early 1980s, amidst ongoing Sino-British negotiations regarding Hong Kong's future, the colonial administration recognized an urgent need to upgrade the territory's cultural infrastructure. The prominent site of the former Kowloon-Canton Railway Terminus in Tsim Sha Tsui (尖沙咀) was selected for a grand performing arts complex. While the clock tower was preserved, the demolition of the old station building in 1978 had already sparked local debate over heritage preservation versus modernization.

## What happened
On **November 10, 1986**, the formal construction and design phase of the Hong Kong Cultural Centre (香港文化中心) was inaugurated. Designed by architects from the Public Works Department and later managed by the Architectural Services Department, the project faced architectural scrutiny for its distinctive windowless, sloping brick-and-granite facade. The complex was envisioned to house a grand concert hall, a large theatre, and exhibition spaces to serve the growing cultural demands of the populace.

## Significance
The creation of the iconic Tsim Sha Tsui cultural hub symbolized Hong Kong's ambition to become a premier international arts and performance metropolis. Completed in 1989 and officially opened by **Governor Sir David Wilson** (衛奕信) and **Prince Charles**, the Centre became a physical manifestation of the late colonial government's efforts to boost civic confidence and project stability during the transitional era leading up to the 1997 handover.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Viewed the ambitious cultural project as a testament to Hong Kong's continued stability, economic vitality, and administrative competence during the politically sensitive transitional period.
- **Local arts community and heritage advocates**: Expressed mixed feelings; while welcoming much-needed modern performance venues, many criticized the earlier demolition of the historic railway station and questioned the brutalist, windowless aesthetic of the new design.
