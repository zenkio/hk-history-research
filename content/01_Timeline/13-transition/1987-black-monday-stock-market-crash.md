## Background

By the late **1980s**, Hong Kong had firmly established itself as a leading international financial hub, operating within a unique colonial administrative framework during the Sino-British transition period. The local stock market, overseen by the Hong Kong Stock Exchange (香港聯合交易所) under the leadership of **Ronald Li** (李福兆), had experienced rapid growth alongside speculative trading in index futures. However, the market's infrastructure and regulatory oversight lagged behind its rapid expansion, particularly regarding margin requirements and settlement guarantees.

## What happened

On **1987-10-19**, known globally as Black Monday, stock markets around the world suffered historic drops. When the panic reached Asia, the Hang Seng Index plummeted by more than **10%** at the opening on Tuesday, **1987-10-20**, before cascading further. Facing catastrophic default risks among brokers in the futures market, **Ronald Li** and the exchange committee made the controversial decision to suspend all trading on the Hong Kong Stock Exchange (香港聯合交易所) and the Hang Seng Index Futures Market for four business days until **1987-10-26**. 

The closure drew fierce international condemnation from foreign investors and institutional bodies who viewed it as an unprecedented and damaging intervention in free-market operations. When trading resumed, the market suffered a historic single-day crash of over **33%**, reflecting pent-up panic and a loss of international confidence.

## Significance

The crisis exposed critical flaws in Hong Kong's financial self-regulation. In response, the colonial government commissioned a review committee led by **Ian Hay Davison**, which resulted in the **1988** Davison Report. This landmark document recommended stripping the exchange of its self-regulatory powers and establishing an independent statutory body. These recommendations directly led to the formation of the Securities and Futures Commission (證券及期貨事務監察委員會) in **1989**, fundamentally reshaping Hong Kong's financial regulatory landscape and paving the way for its modern institutional framework.

## Perspectives

- **Colonial Administration and International Investors**: British officials and foreign institutional investors heavily criticized the four-day market closure as an arbitrary intervention that damaged Hong Kong's reputation as a reliable, free-market international financial centre.
- **Local Exchange Leadership**: Exchange chairman Ronald Li and local brokers defended the suspension as a necessary emergency measure to prevent a total systemic collapse and widespread bankruptcies across the local financial community.
