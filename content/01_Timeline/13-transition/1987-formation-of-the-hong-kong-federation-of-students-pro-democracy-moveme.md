## Background
During the mid-1980s, the signing of the **Sino-British Joint Declaration (中英聯合聲明)** in **1984** set the stage for the transition of sovereignty. As the **Basic Law Drafting Committee (基本法起草委員會)** began its work, the political future of Hong Kong became a central concern for the populace. Student activists, organized primarily under the **Hong Kong Federation of Students (香港專上學生聯會, HKFS)**, transitioned from campus-based advocacy to broader social and political activism, reflecting a growing desire for local self-determination before the **1997** handover.

## What happened
In **1987**, the **HKFS** mobilized significantly to lobby for direct elections to the **Legislative Council (立法局)**. Activists argued that without a representative government, the transition to Chinese rule would lack legitimacy and fail to protect civil liberties. Throughout the year, student leaders organized petitions, public forums, and sit-ins aimed at influencing the public consultation process regarding the **Green Paper on the Further Development of Representative Government in Hong Kong (代議政制綠皮書)**. While the student movement was diverse in its internal opinions, it successfully elevated the profile of political reform, forcing colonial authorities and Beijing officials to address the demands of the youth demographic.

## Significance
This period marked a turning point in Hong Kong’s political culture, as student organizations became institutionalized vehicles for democratic advocacy. The political awareness generated by the **1987** movement served as a precursor to the massive mobilization seen in **1989**. Furthermore, the student efforts created a sustained pressure point that compelled drafters to debate the pace and scope of democratic development within the **Basic Law (基本法)** framework. The movement left an enduring legacy in Hong Kong’s civil society, establishing a precedent for student-led political participation that would persist well beyond the handover.

## Perspectives

- **Colonial Administration**: The British colonial government generally viewed the student activism of 1987 with caution, often balancing the need for public order with the strategic advantage of using grassroots pressure to negotiate with Beijing for gradual democratic concessions.
- **Beijing/Mainland Authorities**: Beijing officials typically interpreted the 1987 student-led calls for immediate direct elections as potentially destabilizing and an interference in the internal affairs of the transition, often preferring a more controlled and gradualist approach to political reform.
- **Local Student Activists**: Local activists viewed their mobilization as a vital democratic necessity, asserting that only through direct elections could the people of Hong Kong secure their autonomy and safeguard their way of life under the 'One Country, Two Systems' (一國兩制) framework.
