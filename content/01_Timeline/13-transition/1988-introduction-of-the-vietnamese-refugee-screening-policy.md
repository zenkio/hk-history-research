## Background

Since the end of the Vietnam War in 1975, Hong Kong had received hundreds of thousands of Vietnamese boat people (越南船民). Initially designated as "first asylum" seekers, arrivals were generally permitted temporary shelter while awaiting resettlement in Western countries. However, by the late 1980s, the international resettlement capacity had slowed significantly, and the demographic makeup of the arrivals increasingly shifted toward individuals originating from northern Vietnam, whom officials and the public increasingly viewed as economic migrants rather than political refugees escaping persecution. 

## What happened

On **June 16, 1988**, following an agreement reached between the Hong Kong government, British authorities, and representatives from Vietnam, the administration implemented a formal screening policy. Under this new framework, all new arrivals from Vietnam were subjected to a determination process to assess whether they qualified for refugee status under international standards. Those who failed the screening were classified as unauthorized economic migrants and detained pending deportation back to Vietnam. Simultaneously, the government introduced closed detention camps, replacing the more open settings of earlier years and confining the screened-out population under restrictive security conditions.

## Significance

The implementation of the screening policy marked a fundamental turning point in Hong Kong's humanitarian and immigration history. It drastically altered the legal and physical landscape of the boat people crisis, leading to years of administrative detention, social tension, and protracted negotiations over repatriation. The closed camps became a site of significant controversy, sparking protests, security incidents, and intense domestic debate regarding human rights, administrative burden, and Hong Kong's international obligations as the transition toward the **1997** handover drew closer.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Viewed the screening policy as a necessary and pragmatic measure to stem the unsustainable influx of migrants and manage a mounting humanitarian and financial crisis that the international community was increasingly failing to share.
- **Local Hong Kong community**: Expressed growing frustration and anxiety over the prolonged presence of the boat people, frequently viewing the camp populations as a security threat and a burden on public resources while demanding faster repatriation.
- **Human rights advocates and later scholarship**: Argued that the screening procedures were often opaque and flawed, and criticized the harsh conditions within the closed detention camps as violations of basic human dignity and international refugee protections.
