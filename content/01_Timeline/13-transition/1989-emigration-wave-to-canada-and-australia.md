## Background
During the **1980s**, the negotiations between the United Kingdom and the People's Republic of China regarding the future of Hong Kong led to the **1984** Sino-British Joint Declaration (中英聯合聲明). While the agreement promised a 'high degree of autonomy' under the 'One Country, Two Systems' (一國兩制) framework, the lead-up to the **1997** handover was marked by fluctuating levels of public confidence. The turning point for many was the **1989** Tiananmen Square crackdown (八九民運), which fundamentally altered the political perception of the city's future among the middle and professional classes.

## What happened
Between **1989** and **1992**, the city witnessed an unprecedented surge in emigration, commonly referred to as the 'brain drain'. During this period, tens of thousands of Hong Kong residents applied for permanent residency in Western countries, with Canada (加拿大) and Australia (澳洲) being the primary destinations due to their points-based immigration systems and established migrant communities. Emigration rates peaked around **1990** and **1992**, as families sought 'insurance policies'—typically foreign passports—to ensure a secondary place of residence. Many professionals left established careers in medicine, law, engineering, and the civil service, leading to significant turnover in both the public and private sectors. Some of these migrants, often termed 'astronauts' (太空人), would later return to Hong Kong for work while their families remained overseas to satisfy residency requirements.

## Significance
The mass emigration reshaped the social and demographic fabric of the city. While the departure of experienced professionals created immediate personnel shortages, it also facilitated a new era of upward mobility for younger, remaining workers who filled the resulting vacancies. The phenomenon forced the colonial government to rethink its retention policies and accelerated the localization of the civil service. Sociologically, this period expanded the global reach of the Hong Kong diaspora, fostering long-term cultural and economic ties between the city and countries like Canada and Australia. The legacy of this wave continues to influence current demographics, as many families maintain dual residency status today.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Government**: The administration viewed the exodus as a crisis of confidence that required institutional stability and the promotion of a 'Bill of Rights' to reassure the populace. Officials were concerned primarily with the loss of specialized talent required to run the administration effectively during the transition period.
- **Beijing/PRC Authorities**: Beijing officials often dismissed the emigration wave as a temporary emotional reaction to misinformation or foreign agitation. They maintained that the long-term economic prospects of Hong Kong under the Motherland would remain superior, and they actively encouraged professionals to remain and build the city.
- **Local Hong Kong Residents**: For many families, emigration was a pragmatic survival strategy rather than a purely political statement. It reflected deep-seated anxieties regarding the rule of law and the stability of the transition, leading to a pragmatic decision to secure citizenship rights elsewhere.
