## Background
Following the signing of the Sino-British Joint Declaration in **1984**, the future of Hong Kong (香港) was set on a course toward Chinese resumption of sovereignty on July 1, **1997**. To implement the principle of "one country, two systems" (一國兩制), the Chinese government initiated the drafting of a constitutional document that would govern the territory after the transfer of sovereignty. The Drafting Committee for the Basic Law was established in **1985**, comprising members from both mainland China and Hong Kong. Public consultations were held on drafts released in **1988** and **1989**, occurring amidst the political trauma and heightened anxieties following the Tiananmen Square crackdown.

## What happened
On April 4, **1990**, the Third Session of the Seventh National People's Congress (全國人民代表大會) formally promulgated the Hong Kong Basic Law (中華人民共和國香港特別行政區基本法), alongside the official design of the regional flag and emblem. The document outline guaranteed rights, freedoms, and the continuation of the capitalist system for fifty years following the 1997 handover. However, the legislative process was marked by intense debate over the pace of democratization, the power of final adjudication, and the interpretation rights of the Standing Committee of the National People's Congress.

## Significance
The Basic Law became the constitutional cornerstone of the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region (香港特別行政區). It attempted to bridge two fundamentally different legal and political systems: the socialist system of the People's Republic of China and the common law tradition established under British colonial rule. Its interpretation and implementation have remained central to Hong Kong's political development, legal autonomy, and international standing in the decades following the handover.

## Perspectives

- **Beijing and Chinese officials**: Viewed the Basic Law as a historic embodiment of national reunification and the practical application of 'one country, two systems,' demonstrating considerable flexibility by granting a high degree of autonomy while upholding national sovereignty.
- **Pro-democracy advocates and local Hong Kong groups**: Often expressed disappointment, arguing that the drafting process was insufficiently responsive to local public opinion, particularly regarding the acceleration of direct democratic elections for the Legislative Council and the safeguarding of civil liberties.
- **British colonial administration and legal scholars**: Scrutinized the text to ensure compatibility with existing international obligations and common law protections, expressing ongoing concerns about the scope of Beijing's power to interpret the document and its potential impact on judicial independence.
