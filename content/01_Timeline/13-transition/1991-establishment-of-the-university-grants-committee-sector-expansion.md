## Background
Following the signing of the Sino-British Joint Declaration in **1984**, Hong Kong entered its transitional period. Concerns over the future sovereignty transfer triggered significant emigration waves among professionals and intellectuals, often referred to as the 'brain drain'. To stabilize the local economy, retain talent, and foster a knowledge-based society, the colonial administration sought to drastically increase the proportion of young people receiving local university education.

## What happened
In **1991**, the government officially adopted a policy to significantly expand the higher education sector. The University Grants Committee (大學教育資助委員會) directed substantial public funds to scale up enrollment capacity across existing institutions, such as the University of Hong Kong (香港大學) and the Chinese University of Hong Kong (香港中文大學), while also upgrading polytechnics and colleges—including City Polytechnic of Hong Kong (香港城市理工學院) and Hong Kong Polytechnic (香港理工學院)—to full university status. The stated goal was to enable roughly 18 percent of the relevant age cohort to enter first-year degree programs by the mid-1990s.

## Significance
This structural expansion fundamentally transformed Hong Kong's educational landscape, shifting higher education from an elite privilege to a mass system. While it successfully produced a larger pool of local degree holders to support the transitioning economy, it also altered the social mobility trajectories of an entire generation entering the workforce immediately before and after the **1997** handover.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Viewed the expansion as a vital investment in human capital and social stability, designed to instill confidence in the local population and secure Hong Kong's status as an international financial and professional hub during the volatile transition period.
- **Later scholarship**: Often analyzes the higher education boom as a strategic response to the post-1989 political climate and escalating emigration, noting that rapid institutional upgrading posed significant challenges in maintaining academic quality and faculty recruitment.
