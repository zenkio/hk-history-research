## Background
Following the signing of the **Sino-British Joint Declaration** in 1984, the political atmosphere in Hong Kong became increasingly focused on the preservation of civil liberties after the transfer of sovereignty. Following the **1989 Tiananmen Square protests** (六四事件), public anxiety regarding the protection of individual rights under future Chinese rule intensified. The colonial government sought to establish a robust legal framework to safeguard these liberties, turning to the **International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights** (公民權利和政治權利國際公約), which had been extended to Hong Kong by the United Kingdom in 1976.

## What happened
On **June 8, 1991**, the **Legislative Council** (立法局) passed the **Hong Kong Bill of Rights Ordinance** (香港人權法案條例). The ordinance served to domesticate the provisions of the Covenant, binding the government and public authorities to specific standards of human rights. It functioned by ensuring that existing and future legislation would be interpreted in a manner consistent with these human rights obligations. To support this, the **Letters Patent** (英皇制誥) were also amended to entrench the Bill of Rights, effectively preventing the legislature from enacting laws that contravened its provisions during the remaining years of British rule.

## Significance
The ordinance marked a pivotal moment in Hong Kong’s constitutional development. By creating a mechanism for judicial review based on human rights standards, it shifted the balance of power between the executive branch and the judiciary. However, its longevity remained a subject of intense debate throughout the transition period. Beijing viewed the introduction of the ordinance as an attempt by the British administration to unilaterally alter the legal landscape established by the Basic Law. Conversely, advocates within Hong Kong saw it as an essential shield to protect the autonomy and freedoms promised in the Joint Declaration.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: The administration viewed the ordinance as a necessary legal instrument to maintain social stability and international confidence in Hong Kong's legal system. They argued that incorporating the Covenant was a standard procedure to ensure that fundamental rights were legally enforceable before the transition of power.
- **Beijing (PRC Government)**: The Chinese government characterized the Bill of Rights as an unnecessary and provocative move by the British. They contended that it created legal contradictions with the Basic Law and asserted that the British lacked the authority to entrench legislation that would bind the future Special Administrative Region government.
- **Local Hong Kong Civil Society**: Many local activists and legal professionals welcomed the ordinance as a crucial safeguard against potential future encroachments on civil liberties. However, some expressed concern that the ordinance could be diluted or repealed after 1997, viewing it as a limited but vital defense.
