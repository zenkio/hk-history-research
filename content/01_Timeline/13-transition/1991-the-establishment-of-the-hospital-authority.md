## Background
Prior to the **1990s**, Hong Kong’s public healthcare system was fragmented. Hospitals were managed separately under either the Medical and Health Department or various subvented organizations, such as the Tung Wah Group of Hospitals (東華三院) and the Po Leung Kuk (保良局). By the **1980s**, the colonial government faced mounting pressure to address inefficiencies, inconsistent standards of care, and aging infrastructure as the population expanded rapidly.

In **1985**, the government commissioned the **Scott Report**, chaired by **W.D. Scott**, which evaluated the existing healthcare delivery system. The report concluded that the bifurcated management structure hindered service delivery and recommended the creation of a unified, statutory body to manage all public hospitals, separate from the civil service.

## What happened
The recommendation was formally adopted, leading to the enactment of the Hospital Authority Ordinance in **1990**. On **December 1, 1991**, the Hospital Authority (醫院管理局), often referred to simply as the HA, was officially established. The organization took over the management of all public hospitals, transitioning staff from the civil service to the new statutory entity. This structural change aimed to professionalize hospital management, unify resource allocation, and standardize clinical protocols across the territory.

## Significance
The creation of the Hospital Authority represents one of the most significant administrative reforms in Hong Kong's history. By centralizing management, the HA ensured that hospitals could share resources and expertise, theoretically reducing waiting times and improving equitable access to specialized treatment. For the growing population, this shift signaled the government’s commitment to providing a consistent quality of care regardless of the institution. However, the transition also sparked concerns regarding the loss of autonomy for long-standing charitable organizations that had historically run many hospitals, as well as anxieties among civil servants regarding their pension and status protections during the transfer process.

## Perspectives

- **Colonial Administration and Pro-reformists**: The administration viewed the HA as a necessary move toward modernizing public services and improving efficiency through centralized fiscal control and managerial accountability. Proponents argued that a unified system was the only way to manage the escalating demands of a modern city effectively.
- **Subvented Hospital Boards**: Representatives of long-standing charitable groups expressed concerns that the centralization would dilute the traditional ethos of local community-based healthcare. They feared that a bureaucratic, government-controlled entity would lack the personal touch and community-specific focus that these organizations had provided for decades.
