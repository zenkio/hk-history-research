## Background
By the early 1990s, the colonial administration and the British government were heavily invested in the transition of sovereignty. Following the **1984 Sino-British Joint Declaration**, Hong Kong faced a dual challenge: maintaining economic momentum during a period of political uncertainty and addressing the physical limitations of Kai Tak Airport. In 1989, the government announced the **Port and Airport Development Strategy (PADS)**, an ambitious master plan to shift aviation facilities to Lantau Island (大嶼山). The Tsing Ma Bridge (青馬大橋) was identified as a critical link to connect the new airport at Chek Lap Kok (赤鱲角) to the urban areas of Kowloon (九龍) and Hong Kong Island (香港島).

## What happened
In **1992**, following years of intensive diplomatic negotiation over funding and implementation, the construction phase officially began. The project was one of the most technically demanding engineering feats of its time, designed as a double-decked suspension bridge capable of carrying both vehicular traffic and the MTR’s Airport Express line. The scale of the work required immense financial commitment and international technical collaboration. While early planning was largely dictated by the British colonial government, the project evolved amidst the complex political landscape of the **Sino-British Joint Liaison Group**, where Beijing expressed concerns regarding the fiscal burden this would place on the future Special Administrative Region government.

## Significance
The bridge symbolizes the "Airport Core Programme," a series of projects that fundamentally altered the geography of Hong Kong. For many, it stands as a testament to the advanced engineering prowess present in Hong Kong during the twilight of British rule. Historians often note that the construction served a dual purpose: it was a functional necessity for the city’s expansion and a political statement. By committing to such a large-scale, long-term project during the final years of colonial rule, the British administration signaled a continued, active management of Hong Kong’s infrastructure, despite the impending transfer of sovereignty to the People’s Republic of China. The bridge remains a physical manifestation of the infrastructure boom that defined the 1990s, bridging the gap between the colonial era and the early years of the HKSAR.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: The government viewed the project as a necessary investment to secure Hong Kong’s future as a global aviation and logistics hub. They maintained that building the bridge was an essential prerequisite for economic continuity regardless of the change in administration.
- **Beijing/PRC Officials**: Initial skepticism arose regarding the potential financial strain on the future government's reserves. Officials closely monitored the project's costs, fearing that excessive spending on "lavish" projects might deplete the fiscal health of the incoming SAR.
- **Local Hong Kong Public**: For the general public, the bridge represented both pride in local engineering progress and a symbol of stability during a period of anxiety. Some residents focused on the practical benefits of improved connectivity, while others remained concerned about the impact on the environment and the transparency of the project's financing.
