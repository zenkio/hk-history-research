## Background

By the early 1990s, the countdown to the **1997** transfer of sovereignty over Hong Kong was well underway, governed by the Sino-British Joint Declaration signed in **1984** and the promulgation of the Basic Law in **1990**. Following the **1989** Tiananmen Square crackdown, anxieties ran high among the local population regarding future civil liberties and political autonomy. In **July 1992**, **Chris Patten** assumed office as the last British Governor of Hong Kong (香港), breaking the precedent of appointing career diplomats by sending a senior politician to handle the delicate final phase of colonial rule.

## What happened

On **October 7, 1992**, in his first Policy Address, Governor **Chris Patten** unveiled a package of constitutional reform proposals aimed at increasing democratic representation in the Legislative Council (立法局) for the upcoming **1995** elections. The proposals sought to lower the voting age, abolish appointed seats at the municipal and district levels, and crucially, introduce new functional constituencies that effectively widened the franchise so that nearly every working person in Hong Kong could vote. By redefining the electorate of existing functional constituencies through creative legal interpretation, Patten's plan maximized the democratic space within the constraints of the existing Basic Law framework without formally breaching its text.

## Significance

Patten's proposals triggered an immediate and furious response from the government of the People's Republic of China, which accused London of violating the Sino-British Joint Declaration, the principle of convergence with the Basic Law, and previous diplomatic understandings. The resulting diplomatic deadlock suspended normal cooperative transitional mechanisms. Although the Legislative Council ultimately passed the reform package in **1994**, Beijing vowed to dismantle the elected bodies upon the resumption of sovereignty in **1997**, leading to the temporary establishment of a Provisional Legislative Council (臨時立法會).

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Government**: Presented the reforms as a fulfillment of a moral obligation to the people of Hong Kong, arguing that a more robust democratic foundation was necessary to ensure long-term stability and to protect the rule of law and autonomy promised under the Sino-British Joint Declaration.
- **Qing/Beijing Government**: Condemned the proposals as a 'three-violations' breach—violating the Joint Declaration, the principle of convergence with the Basic Law, and previous Sino-British agreements—and viewed Patten's actions as a deliberate attempt to create political instability and plant a pro-British opposition force before the handover.
- **Local Hong Kong Communities**: Exhibited deeply polarized reactions; pro-democracy advocates and civic groups welcomed the expansion of the franchise as a long-overdue step toward genuine representation, while the business community and pro-Beijing figures feared the reforms would provoke mainland authorities and disrupt economic prosperity.
