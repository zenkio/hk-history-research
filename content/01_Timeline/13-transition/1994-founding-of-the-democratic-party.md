## Background
In the years following the 1984 Sino-British Joint Declaration, Hong Kong experienced a rapid development of political parties as the colonial government moved toward a representative system. The **United Democrats of Hong Kong (港同盟)**, founded in 1990 by figures including **Martin Lee (李柱銘)** and **Szeto Wah (司徒華)**, had emerged as the most prominent electoral force, having achieved significant success in the 1991 Legislative Council elections. Parallel to this, the smaller group **Meeting Point (匯點)**, led by figures like **Lau Nai-keung (劉乃強)** and later **Anthony Cheung (張炳良)**, focused on a more moderate approach toward the integration of Hong Kong with mainland China. As the 1997 handover approached, both organizations sought to consolidate their influence and strengthen their position regarding the democratic development of the future Special Administrative Region.

## What happened
On **October 2, 1994**, the two organizations officially merged to form the **Democratic Party (民主黨)**. The merger was driven by a perceived need to provide a unified voice for the pro-democracy movement amidst the tense political negotiations between London and Beijing over the scope of electoral reforms proposed by Governor **Chris Patten (彭定康)**. The new party adopted a platform calling for full direct elections and the protection of human rights. While the merger brought together diverse factions of the democratic camp, it also led to internal debates regarding the party's ideological direction, particularly concerning the extent of cooperation or confrontation with the Chinese government.

## Significance
The formation of the Democratic Party represented the most significant consolidation of electoral power in Hong Kong's history up to that point. It created a coherent, city-wide political force that dominated the democratic spectrum for over a decade. The party became the primary institutional vehicle for advocacy regarding the 'One Country, Two Systems' framework. However, its existence also highlighted the deep-seated political divisions within the city; for the Beijing authorities, the party was often viewed with suspicion due to its close ties with international democratic movements and its resistance to the constraints imposed on political liberalization during the transition. Locally, the party served as a major representative for voters who favored a faster pace of democratization, although later scholarship notes that the party's influence faced constant challenges from both pro-Beijing political entities and shifting public sentiments regarding the balance between economic stability and democratic reform.

## Perspectives

- **Pro-Democracy Supporters**: Many democratic advocates viewed the merger as a strategic necessity to protect the rule of law and civil liberties as 1997 approached. They argued that a unified party was essential to defend Hong Kong's autonomy against potential interference from the mainland.
- **Beijing and Pro-Beijing Circles**: Beijing officials and aligned political figures often criticized the party's platform as confrontational and uncooperative. They argued that the party's stance undermined the spirit of the Joint Declaration and destabilized the transition process.
