## Background
Established in **1974** following the escape of Chief Superintendent **Peter Godber**, the Independent Commission Against Corruption (廉政公署), or **ICAC**, was initially conceived as a surgical strike against systemic police corruption. Throughout the 1970s and 1980s, the commission evolved from an aggressive investigative force into a tripartite agency focusing on investigation, prevention, and community relations. As the **1984 Sino-British Joint Declaration** (中英聯合聲明) set the territory on a path toward the 1997 handover, the preservation of the rule of law and civil service integrity became a primary concern for both the colonial government and the local populace.

## What happened
By **1994**, the ICAC intensified its efforts to entrench a 'clean' culture within the private sector and the general public, moving beyond its traditional focus on the civil service. This period saw a significant expansion in outreach, including school-based educational programs and mass media campaigns designed to promote the social costs of corruption. These initiatives were aimed at fostering an expectation of transparency among citizens as they looked toward the uncertainty of the post-1997 political landscape. The commission's visible presence at the community level during this time helped to normalize ethical standards, essentially creating a 'social contract' where public trust in the institution replaced the pervasive 'grease' culture that had characterized the post-war era.

## Significance
The consolidation of the ICAC’s culture by the mid-1990s serves as a critical bridge between colonial governance and the Special Administrative Region. For the British administration, the commission represented a legacy institution intended to ensure that Hong Kong’s economic competitiveness remained intact through bureaucratic integrity. For the local community, the ICAC became a symbol of civil protection that transcended political allegiance. Whether the ICAC’s success was primarily due to legislative power or the active participation of a changing public remains a subject of ongoing historical discussion, yet its status as a vital component of Hong Kong's identity by **1997** is undisputed.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Perspective**: The British administration largely viewed the ICAC’s institutionalization as a triumph of the Westminster-style rule of law and bureaucratic transparency. They emphasized the professionalization of the civil service as a necessary precursor to the stable transfer of power.
- **Local Hong Kong Perspective**: Many local residents viewed the ICAC’s 'clean' culture as a protection against both police brutality and the perceived threat of administrative decline leading up to the handover. For them, it was less about British values and more about a grassroots demand for fairness in an increasingly complex society.
