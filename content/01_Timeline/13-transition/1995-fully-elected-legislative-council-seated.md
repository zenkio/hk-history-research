## Background

Following the signing of the Sino-British Joint Declaration in 1984, the political future of Hong Kong was set toward a transition of sovereignty to the People's Republic of China in 1997. Throughout the late 1980s and early 1990s, the structure of Hong Kong's Legislative Council (立法局) evolved to incorporate more elected elements alongside traditional appointed and functional constituency seats. 

In 1992, **Chris Patten** assumed office as the last Governor of Hong Kong (香港總督). Soon after, he introduced a series of political reform proposals designed to expand the franchise for the 1995 Legislative Council elections. These reforms lowered the voting age, expanded the voter base for functional constituencies, and created new functional constituency seats that effectively gave every working resident a vote, circumventing previous restrictions. 

## What happened

On **September 17, 1995**, Hong Kong held its Legislative Council elections under Patten's revised framework. For the first time in the territory's history, all 60 seats in the legislature were filled by election, comprising 20 geographical constituency seats, 30 functional constituency seats, and 10 seats chosen by the Election Committee (選舉委員會) composed largely of district board members. 

Turnout was notably high, with over 35 percent of eligible voters casting ballots. The pro-democracy camp, led by figures such as **Martin Lee** (李柱銘) and his Democratic Party (民主黨), performed strongly in the geographical constituencies, securing the largest bloc of seats in the new legislature. 

## Significance

This election represented the culmination of democratic development under British colonial rule, producing Hong Kong's most representative legislature to date. However, it also marked a severe breakdown in Sino-British cooperation. Because Beijing argued that Patten's unilateral reforms violated the Sino-British Joint Declaration, the Basic Law, and previous understandings between the two governments, the Chinese authorities declared the legislature "through train" arrangement void. Consequently, the 1995 Legislative Council was slated to be replaced upon the handover, leading to the establishment of the Provisional Legislative Council (臨時立法會) in 1996.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Governor Chris Patten and British officials maintained that the reforms were a modest and necessary step to fulfill Britain's moral obligation to leave behind a robust, accountable, and democratic system for the people of Hong Kong.
- **Qing/Beijing government**: Chinese authorities viewed the 1995 election as a violation of the Sino-British Joint Declaration and the Hong Kong Basic Law, arguing that Patten's unilateral changes breached prior diplomatic understandings and necessitated the creation of a provisional legislature.
- **Local Hong Kong pro-democracy community**: Many local activists and democratic politicians celebrated the election as a hard-won victory for representation and popular sovereignty, while later criticizing Beijing's subsequent replacement of the elected body as a rollback of democratic rights.
