## Background

Rennie's Mill, known in Cantonese as Tiu Keng Leng (調景嶺), originated in the early 1950s following the Chinese Civil War. After the establishment of the People's Republic of China in 1949, thousands of Kuomintang (KMT) soldiers and their families fled to Hong Kong. The colonial government settled these refugees in a remote area in the eastern New Territories. Over several decades, the area developed into a unique community characterized by strong ideological alignment with the Republic of China (ROC) government in Taiwan. Residents frequently displayed the ROC flag, and the settlement became a visible anomaly within the British colony as the date for the 1997 handover approached.

## What happened

By the mid-1990s, the Hong Kong government initiated plans to redevelop the Tiu Keng Leng area into a modern residential zone. **July 1996** marked the final phase of the camp's demolition. The process involved the relocation of thousands of residents to public housing estates, primarily in Tseung Kwan O (將軍澳). The clearance was met with various degrees of resistance and negotiation, as the government sought to normalize the land for civilian development. While the government emphasized the necessity of infrastructure upgrades and urban planning, many long-term residents felt that their history and community ties were being forcibly uprooted ahead of the transition to Chinese sovereignty.

## Significance

The closure of Rennie's Mill is widely viewed by historians as the final dismantling of the Cold War ideological frontline within Hong Kong. For decades, the enclave had functioned as a symbol of the rivalry between the KMT and the Chinese Communist Party (CCP). By clearing the site, the British administration removed a potential source of political friction in the lead-up to the 1997 handover. However, the event also signaled the end of a unique sociocultural chapter in Hong Kong history, where a marginalized group had managed to carve out a self-sustaining identity based on their displaced status. The physical erasure of the camp facilitated the total integration of the area into the broader Hong Kong urban fabric, effectively ending the enclave's role as a refuge for KMT loyalists.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Government**: The administration framed the demolition as a routine urban renewal project intended to improve living standards and housing conditions for residents. They maintained that the camp's original infrastructure was obsolete and unsuitable for the requirements of a modernizing Hong Kong.
- **Former Rennie's Mill Residents**: Many residents interpreted the closure as a politically motivated act designed to appease the incoming PRC government by erasing pro-Taiwan symbols before 1997. They emphasized the community's long-standing social cohesion and viewed the relocation as a loss of their ancestral and ideological home.
