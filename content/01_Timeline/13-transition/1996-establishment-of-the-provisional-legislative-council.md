## Background
Following the **1992** announcement of political reform proposals by Governor **Chris Patten** (彭定康), the relationship between the British colonial administration and the People's Republic of China deteriorated. **Patten** sought to broaden the democratic base of the Legislative Council (LegCo), a move the Chinese government viewed as a violation of the Sino-British Joint Declaration and the principle of 'convergence' with the Basic Law. By **1994**, after the 'through-train' arrangement for legislators failed to materialize, Beijing announced it would establish a shadow legislature to ensure continuity upon the 1997 handover.

## What happened
On **December 21, 1996**, the **60-member** Selection Committee, a body appointed by the Preparatory Committee of the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region, convened in Shenzhen (深圳) to hold an election for the **Provisional Legislative Council (PLC)** (臨時立法會). The election resulted in the selection of 60 members who would serve as the interim legislative body. Many members of the incumbent Legislative Council who were elected during the **1995** elections did not participate in the body, as the British administration refused to recognize the PLC, arguing it had no legal basis under existing colonial law.

## Significance
The creation of the **PLC** represented a direct challenge to the authority of the British-led Legislative Council during the final months of the transition period. The body operated from Shenzhen until the midnight handover on **July 1, 1997**, at which point it officially convened in Hong Kong to pass its first piece of legislation. Its establishment serves as a pivotal example of the legal and political friction between the outgoing colonial administration and the incoming sovereign power, illustrating the divergent interpretations of how Hong Kong's transition to Chinese sovereignty should be managed.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Government**: The British administration viewed the Provisional Legislative Council as an illegal body that circumvented the democratic reforms established in 1995. Officials argued that the PLC lacked a mandate from the Hong Kong public and violated the spirit of the Joint Declaration.
- **Beijing/PRC Government**: The Chinese government maintained that the PLC was a necessary measure to prevent a legal vacuum upon the end of British rule. They argued that the 1995 reforms were a unilateral breach of prior agreements, necessitating a new legislative body aligned with the Basic Law.
