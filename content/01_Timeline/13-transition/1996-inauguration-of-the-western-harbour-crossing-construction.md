## Background
By the early **1990s**, the rapid expansion of Hong Kong’s financial sector and the growing population in the New Territories (新界) placed significant strain on existing cross-harbour traffic. The existing Cross-Harbour Tunnel (海底隧道), completed in **1972**, and the Eastern Harbour Crossing (東區海底隧道), opened in **1989**, were operating near or at capacity. To address these bottlenecks and facilitate better movement between the Central and Western District (中西區) and the West Kowloon (西九龍) reclamation areas, the colonial government proposed a third harbour tunnel. This project was conceived as an integral part of the Port and Airport Development Strategy (機場核心計劃).

## What happened
Construction of the Western Harbour Crossing (西區海底隧道) officially began in **1996**. The project was awarded under a Build-Operate-Transfer (BOT) franchise to the Western Harbour Tunnel Company (西松建設). The engineering plan utilized an immersed tube method, connecting Sai Ying Pun (西營盤) on Hong Kong Island to the West Kowloon reclamation (西九龍填海區). As the territory approached the **1997** transfer of sovereignty, the rapid progression of this work became a focal point of the final colonial administration’s push to solidify infrastructure integration before the handover.

## Significance
For the British colonial government, the tunnel represented the culmination of decades of urban planning aimed at sustaining Hong Kong’s status as a global financial center. The project was designed to streamline logistics between the financial districts of Central and the newly expanded transport corridors leading to the then-under-construction Chek Lap Kok Airport (赤鱲角機場). However, the project also generated significant public debate regarding its toll structure and the long-term sustainability of private-sector concessions on essential public transport routes. Following the **1997** handover, the tunnel became a critical artery in the territory's transport network, though its high toll prices remained a recurring topic in public discourse regarding the accessibility of cross-harbour infrastructure.

## Perspectives

- **British Colonial Administration**: The administration viewed the project as a necessary investment in the territory’s economic future, essential for maintaining efficiency during the transition period. They emphasized the project’s success in securing private investment through a BOT model, characterizing it as a legacy of prudent, long-term infrastructure planning.
- **Local Hong Kong Public and Commuter Groups**: Many residents welcomed the reduction in traffic congestion but remained critical of the high toll rates required to satisfy the BOT franchise. This perspective often contrasted the utility of the infrastructure with concerns over the privatization of essential transit links, which some argued prioritized commercial return over affordable public access.
