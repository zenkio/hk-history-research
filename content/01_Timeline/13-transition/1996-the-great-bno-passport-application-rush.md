## Background
As the Sino-British Joint Declaration set the course for Hong Kong's sovereignty transfer on July 1, 1997, the status of local residents under British nationality law underwent significant changes. The British Nationality Act 1981 created a new class of nationality known as British Dependent Territories Citizen (BDTC), which most Hong Kong residents held. Following the 1984 Joint Declaration, the British government announced that BDTC status connected to Hong Kong would expire on June 30, 1997. To address this, the **British Nationality (Hong Kong) Order 1986** established the British National (Overseas) [_British National (Overseas)_] or BNO status, allowing individuals to register before a strict statutory deadline.

## What happened
On **March 30, 1996**, and the surrounding weeks, unprecedented scenes unfolded across the territory as the registration deadline approached. Tens of thousands of anxious residents formed massive, snaking overnight queues outside the Immigration Department headquarters in Wan Chai (灣兒/灣仔) and other regional offices. Families and individuals waited for hours, enduring chaotic conditions to submit their applications for BNO passports. The rush reflected widespread anxiety regarding legal identity, travel documentation, and future security under the upcoming Chinese administration.

## Significance
The BNO application rush highlighted the complex identity politics and deep-seated anxieties of Hong Kong people during the transitional period. While the BNO status did not grant the right of abode in the United Kingdom, it provided essential consular protection and visa-free travel access to numerous countries, making it a highly sought-after safeguard. The massive turnout demonstrated both a practical desire for international mobility and a psychological coping mechanism in the face of impending political change.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial perspective**: The British government viewed the creation of BNO status and its registration deadlines as a fulfillment of administrative obligations to provide a non-right-of-abode travel document, carefully managing nationality issues without extending full settlement rights to millions of Hong Kong subjects.
- **Beijing and Qing legacy perspective**: Chinese authorities generally regarded Hong Kong compatriots as Chinese nationals regardless of colonial travel documents, viewing foreign passports primarily as administrative tools for international travel rather than altering national allegiance.
- **Local Hong Kong perspective**: For ordinary residents, securing a BNO passport was viewed as a pragmatic necessity for global mobility and an insurance policy providing psychological reassurance amid the uncertainties of the 1997 transition.
