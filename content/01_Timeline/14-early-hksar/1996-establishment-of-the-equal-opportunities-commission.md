## Background
In the final years of British colonial administration, growing pressure from local advocacy groups and changing international norms prompted the Hong Kong government to reconsider its approach to civil rights. Historically, the colonial administration maintained a relatively laissez-faire stance regarding social legislation, preferring administrative handling of grievances over statutory enforcement. However, as the 1997 handover to the People's Republic of China approached, civil society organizations pushed for stronger legal protections against discrimination based on sex, disability, and eventually race and family status. The enactment of the Sex Discrimination Ordinance and the Disability Discrimination Ordinance provided the legislative framework required to address these systemic issues.

## What happened
On **September 20, 1996**, the Equal Opportunities Commission (平機會) was formally established as a statutory body under the Sex Discrimination Ordinance. Its primary mandate was to eliminate discrimination, harassment, and vilification on the grounds of sex, marital status, pregnancy, and disability, as well as to promote equality of opportunity between men and women and among persons with and without disabilities. The commission was empowered to investigate complaints, facilitate conciliation, issue codes of practice, and conduct formal investigations and research. Initial operations faced challenges in defining the commission's scope and securing adequate funding and political independence from the colonial government.

## Significance
The creation of the commission represented a departure in Hong Kong's legal history, moving from uncodified social customs and administrative grievance-handling toward institutionalized human rights protections. It served as a precedent for future statutory human rights bodies established in the early period of the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region (HKSAR). While some early critics questioned whether the commission would possess sufficient enforcement powers without the ability to initiate legal proceedings independently in all cases, it nonetheless laid the groundwork for subsequent anti-discrimination laws covering race and other protected characteristics in the decades that followed.

## Perspectives

- **British colonial administration**: Framed the establishment of the commission as a progressive fulfillment of international human rights obligations and a legacy of good governance to be left behind before the handover.
- **Local advocacy groups**: Viewed the commission as a long-overdue mechanism, though many activists argued its initial enforcement powers were too weak to effectively challenge entrenched workplace and social discrimination.
