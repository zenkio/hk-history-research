## Background

In July **1997**, sovereignty over Hong Kong was transferred from the United Kingdom to the People's Republic of China, establishing the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region (香港特別行政區). During the final years of British colonial rule, the Hong Kong property market had experienced unprecedented booms, driven by high demand, limited land supply, and speculative investment. The peg of the Hong Kong dollar to the US dollar, maintained by the Hong Kong Monetary Authority, provided monetary stability but exposed the territory to external economic shocks.

## What happened

In October **1997**, speculative attacks swept across Southeast Asian currencies, rapidly spreading to Hong Kong. To defend the linked exchange rate, interest rates spiked dramatically, causing liquidity to dry up. The stock market tumbled, and the property market—long considered an infallible engine of wealth—suffered a catastrophic correction. Property values plummeted by over fifty percent from their 1997 peaks over the next few years. Thousands of mortgage holders found themselves in "negative equity" (負資產), owing banks more than their properties were worth. This wealth destruction dampened domestic consumption and led to a prolonged deflationary spiral.

## Significance

The property crash effectively ended the high-growth, high-property-price consensus that had characterized late colonial governance. It forced both the SAR government and citizens to confront severe economic vulnerability. While the government eventually phased out the ambitious 85,000 housing units per year policy to stabilize prices, the crisis left a lasting psychological mark on society, altering household debt behavior and redefining the relationship between the public, the property developers, and the administration.

## Perspectives

- **Local Hong Kong homeowners and middle-class residents**: Many viewed the sudden collapse as a devastating blow to their life savings and upward mobility, with negative equity trapping families in unsellable homes while broader economic hardship mounted.
- **Beijing and SAR government officials**: Authorities often characterized the downturn as an external shock driven by international speculators, requiring defensive market interventions such as the government's unprecedented stock market purchase in August 1998 to protect financial stability.
- **Later economic scholarship**: Researchers frequently analyze the crisis as an inevitable correction exacerbated by structural policy flaws of the peg system and the historical over-reliance on land-related revenues in Hong Kong's fiscal model.
