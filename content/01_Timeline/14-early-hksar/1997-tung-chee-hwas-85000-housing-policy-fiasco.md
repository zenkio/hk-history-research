## Background

In July **1997**, sovereignty over Hong Kong was transferred from the United Kingdom to the People's Republic of China. Longstanding issues regarding soaring property prices and inadequate public housing plagued the newly established Hong Kong Special Administrative Region (HKSAR). In his inaugural policy address on **October 8, 1997**, the first Chief Executive, **Tung Chee-hwa** (**董建華**), introduced a sweeping initiative to tackle the housing crisis. The core of the strategy was a commitment to build at least 85,000 flats per year in the public and private sectors combined, aiming to boost homeownership to 70 percent within a decade and lower housing costs for the general population.

## What happened

Shortly after the policy was announced, the Asian Financial Crisis struck Hong Kong with unexpected severity, triggered by speculative attacks on regional currencies and the Hong Kong dollar peg. Property values, which had reached historic peaks prior to the handover, began to plummet rapidly. As supply targets remained high under the 85,000 policy despite cratering market demand, housing prices eventually dropped by over 50 percent from their 1997 highs.

This dramatic depreciation plunged hundreds of thousands of middle-class homeowners into negative equity, where their outstanding mortgage loans exceeded the market value of their properties. Public dissatisfaction mounted as household wealth evaporated and bankruptcies rose. Although the government quietly phased out the numerical target in subsequent years—officially declaring it dead by **2002**—the damage to the administration's popularity and public trust was already profound.

## Significance

The 85,000 housing policy became a defining misstep of the early HKSAR era, severely damaging the governance and credibility of **Tung Chee-hwa**'s administration. It fundamentally altered Hong Kong's political landscape by galvanizing middle-class discontent, contributing to massive protests such as the historic **July 1st march** in **2003**. The legacy of the crisis cast a long shadow over subsequent land and housing planning, making successive administrations exceptionally cautious about aggressive supply-side interventions.

## Perspectives

- **Local Hong Kong middle-class homeowners**: Felt deeply betrayed by government policies that destroyed their life savings, turning homeownership aspirations into a heavy financial burden through widespread negative equity.
- **Later economic scholarship**: Often argues that while the policy's rigid targets exacerbated the panic, the primary driver of the property crash was the external shock of the Asian Financial Crisis rather than the policy alone.
