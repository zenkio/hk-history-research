## Background
Following the 1997 handover, the HKSAR government sought to reform the education system to foster a stronger sense of national identity and to align pedagogical practices with the demographics of the student population. Historically, during the colonial period, English-medium instruction (EMI) was highly prized as a gateway to professional success and university entry. By the mid-1990s, however, educational studies suggested that students taught in their mother tongue—Cantonese—could grasp complex concepts more effectively than those struggling with a foreign language.

## What happened
In **1998**, the **Education Department (教育署)** officially implemented the 'Medium of Instruction for Secondary Schools' policy. Under this mandate, approximately 75% of secondary schools were required to switch to **Chinese as the medium of instruction (母語教學)**. Only a select group of schools, deemed to have sufficient student ability and staff capability, were permitted to continue utilizing English as the medium of instruction. This effectively created a stratified system where schools were categorized based on their language of instruction, a move that critics argued created a tiered perception of school prestige.

## Significance
The policy sparked intense social and political debate. Proponents argued that it democratized high-quality education by removing the linguistic barrier inherent in EMI schools, allowing students to focus on content rather than rote memorization of English vocabulary. Conversely, opponents, including many parents and elite school alumni, expressed concerns that the decline in English proficiency would diminish Hong Kong’s competitiveness as an international financial center. The policy also raised questions about social mobility; as proficiency in English remained a prerequisite for many high-paying professions, the categorization of schools was seen by some as a potential hurdle for students in 'Chinese-medium' institutions to access elite tertiary education. The long-term effects of this policy remained a subject of ongoing scrutiny throughout the early 2000s, as educators monitored university entrance outcomes and the evolving proficiency levels of graduates.

## Perspectives

- **HKSAR Government Officials**: The government maintained that the policy was evidence-based and designed to improve student cognitive development. They argued that learning in the mother tongue would ultimately bridge the gap between classroom instruction and student comprehension.
- **Parents and Pro-EMI Advocacy Groups**: Many parents feared that the policy was a social experiment that disadvantaged their children by depriving them of English fluency. They viewed the categorization of schools as a direct threat to the perceived prestige and subsequent career prospects of students in Chinese-medium institutions.
