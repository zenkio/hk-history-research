## Background
In the late 1990s, following the 1997 handover to China and the Asian Financial Crisis, the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region (HKSAR) government sought to diversify its economy beyond traditional real estate and financial sectors. Chief Executive **Tung Chee-hwa** (董建華) championed high-technology industries as a key driver for future economic growth, leading to the conceptualization of the Cyberport (數碼港) project in Telegraph Bay (資訊港/鋼線灣), Pok Fu Lam (薄扶林).

## What happened
On **March 3, 1999**, the government officially announced that the development rights for the multi-billion-dollar Cyberport project had been awarded directly to Pacific Century Group (盈科拓展), controlled by **Richard Li** (李澤楷), the younger son of tycoon **Li Ka-shing** (李嘉誠). Bypassing the standard public tender process, the administration argued that the project was of paramount strategic importance and required urgent implementation to capture the rapidly growing dot-com boom. Critics immediately pointed out that the arrangement allowed Pacific Century CyberWorks (PCCW) (電訊盈科) to build lucrative residential properties alongside the high-tech offices, significantly boosting the project's commercial viability for the developer.

## Significance
The no-bid award became a watershed moment in early HKSAR history, crystallizing public anxieties regarding the continuation of elite developer dominance—often termed "developer hegemony" (地產霸權)—under the new administration. While proponents argued that the pragmatic approach successfully bypassed bureaucratic red tape to establish a vital IT infrastructure, detractors viewed it as a prime example of crony capitalism. The controversy severely dented the government's credibility regarding fair competition and transparency, leaving a lasting impact on public discourse concerning government-business relations in post-colonial Hong Kong.

## Perspectives

- **HKSAR Government and Proponents**: Officials and supporters maintained that bypassing standard procedures was a necessary, pragmatic measure to secure rapid foreign investment and establish Hong Kong's foothold in the global digital economy during a post-crisis downturn.
- **Critics, Opposition Politicians, and Free-Market Advocates**: Detractors argued that the lack of an open tender favored politically connected tycoons at the expense of fair competition, exemplifying a return to colonial-era crony capitalism and developer hegemony.
