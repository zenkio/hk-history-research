## Background

Following the transfer of sovereignty over Hong Kong on **July 1, 1997**, questions arose regarding Article 24 of the Basic Law (基本法), which defines the right of abode. Numerous children born in the mainland to Hong Kong permanent residents claimed the right of settlement in the territory. The Hong Kong government argued that such an interpretation could lead to an influx of over one million migrants, straining public infrastructure and social services. In **January 1999**, the Court of Final Appeal (終審法院) delivered a landmark ruling in the case of *Ng Ka Ling v. Director of Immigration*, affirming the right of abode for these children and famously declaring the court's power to review acts of the National People's Congress (全國人民代表大會) for consistency with the Basic Law.

## What happened

Faced with the prospect of large-scale migration, the Hong Kong government sought a resolution. In an unprecedented move, the Chief Executive, **Tung Chee-hwa** (董建華), invited the State Council to submit a report to the Standing Committee of the National People's Congress (NPCSC) (全國人民代表大會常務委員會) requesting an interpretation under Article 158 of the Basic Law. On **June 26, 1999**, the NPCSC issued its interpretation. It concluded that the Court of Final Appeal's earlier ruling had misinterpreted the intent of the Basic Law, determining that mainland children only acquired the right of abode if at least one parent was a permanent resident at the time of their birth, effectively excluding those born before their parents obtained permanent residency.

## Significance

The **June 26, 1999** interpretation marked a watershed moment in the history of the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region. It represented the first time Beijing intervened in the legal autonomy of Hong Kong, establishing a constitutional precedent where the NPCSC could override local judicial decisions. While supporters viewed the intervention as a necessary constitutional check to prevent unmanageable demographic pressures and uphold the balance of power envisioned in the Basic Law, critics, including the Hong Kong Bar Association (香港大律師公會) and legal scholars, viewed it as a severe blow to the rule of law, judicial independence, and the high degree of autonomy promised under the 'One Country, Two Systems' (一國兩制) principle.

## Perspectives

- **Hong Kong Government and Beijing Authorities**: Proponents of the interpretation argued that the NPCSC acted fully within its constitutional authority under Article 158 of the Basic Law. They maintained that the intervention was a pragmatic necessity to avert overwhelming social and economic burdens on Hong Kong caused by potentially massive demographic influxes.
- **Local Legal Community and Civil Society**: Many local legal professionals, human rights advocates, and pro-democracy groups viewed the intervention as a profound threat to judicial independence. They contended that bypassing the Court of Final Appeal undermined the finality of local adjudication and eroded the legal safeguards guaranteed in the Sino-British Joint Declaration.
