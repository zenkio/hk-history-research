## Background
By the late **1990s**, following the **1997** handover of sovereignty to the People's Republic of China, Hong Kong's agricultural sector was already in a gradual decline. Urban encroachment, high land values, and competition from Mainland Chinese imports had steadily squeezed traditional farming in the **New Territories (新界)**. Despite this decline, local farmers still supplied a notable portion of fresh vegetables to the territory's markets, maintaining a distinct rural economy and lifestyle that had persisted for decades.

## What happened
Around **1999**, a destructive outbreak of **Phytophthora**—a genus of oomycetes known for causing root rot and blight—struck local crop yields, particularly affecting staple greens and tomato cultivation. The pathogen, thriving in humid conditions, spread rapidly through farming plots in districts such as **Yuen Long (元朗)** and **Tai Po (大埔)**. Because many farms lacked the resources for large-scale chemical intervention or rapid soil sterilization, the infection caused catastrophic crop losses. Farmers faced immediate financial ruin, while local agricultural output plummeted further.

## Significance
The **1999** Phytophthora outbreak served as a critical turning point for Hong Kong's rural economy. Rather than recovering, the agricultural sector contracted sharply as many farmers abandoned their fields permanently, leasing their land out or transitioning to other employment. This biological crisis underscored the territory's near-total reliance on imported foodstuffs from the **Mainland**, cementing Hong Kong's status as a hyper-dense, service-oriented financial hub where primary production was no longer economically viable.

## Perspectives

- **Local New Territories Farmers**: For rural communities, the outbreak represented a devastating final blow to traditional livelihoods that had been practiced for generations, exacerbated by a perceived lack of adequate long-term structural support from the government.
- **Government and Economic Analysts**: Authorities and market analysts viewed the agricultural decline and subsequent shocks as an inevitable market correction, aligning with Hong Kong's evolution into a high-density international financial and logistics center reliant on regional trade.
