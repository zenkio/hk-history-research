## Background
In the late 1990s, the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region (HKSAR) government sought to diversify the local economy and establish the city as a regional hub for information technology. Following the 1997 handover, the administration under Chief Executive **Tung Chee-hwa** (董建華) prioritized high-tech development as a cornerstone of economic recovery after the Asian Financial Crisis.

## What happened
In **March 1999**, the government announced the development of Cyberport (數碼港), a multi-billion dollar information technology and residential project to be located in Telegraph Bay (鋼綫灣). Rather than holding a competitive public tender, the government granted the development rights to the **Pacific Century Group** (盈科拓展集團), headed by **Richard Li** (李澤楷), the son of prominent tycoon **Li Ka-shing** (李嘉誠). The project was structured as a public-private partnership where the developer would build the tech infrastructure in exchange for the rights to develop and sell high-end residential units on the same site. Because the contract was awarded through a private agreement rather than an open bidding process, the government faced immediate and intense scrutiny from the Legislative Council (立法會) and the media regarding the lack of transparency.

## Significance
The Cyberport incident became a flashpoint for debates concerning 'crony capitalism' (裙帶資本主義) in the early years of the HKSAR. Critics argued that the non-tender process bypassed established procedures that ensure fair competition and fiscal prudence, potentially favoring a well-connected business entity. The government defended the decision by citing the urgency of the economic situation and the necessity of partnering with a firm that possessed both capital and technical expertise. This event marked one of the first major public challenges to the governance style of the HKSAR administration, setting a precedent for future discussions regarding government-business collusion and the influence of powerful property conglomerates in Hong Kong's policy-making process. The controversy also forced a review of how large-scale infrastructure projects were managed, influencing public expectations for government accountability and procurement transparency in the following decade.

## Perspectives

- **HKSAR Government**: The government maintained that the development was a strategic necessity to jumpstart the IT sector during an economic downturn. Officials argued that the single tender approach allowed for faster implementation and provided the necessary financial incentives to ensure private sector commitment.
- **Legislative Critics and Public Activists**: Critics viewed the arrangement as a departure from the competitive principles previously upheld in Hong Kong. They expressed concern that the lack of an open tender deprived the public of potential benefits that a competitive bidding process might have secured and questioned the fairness of the government's selection criteria.
