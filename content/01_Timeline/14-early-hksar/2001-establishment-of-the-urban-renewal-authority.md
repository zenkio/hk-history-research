## Background
Following the transfer of sovereignty in 1997, the newly formed Hong Kong Special Administrative Region (HKSAR) government sought to address the challenges posed by aging urban infrastructure and deteriorating building stocks. The predecessor body, the Land Development Corporation (LDC), had been established in 1988 with a mandate to undertake urban renewal, but it was frequently constrained by a lack of statutory powers to expedite land acquisition and a limited financial remit. As property values fluctuated and public concerns over urban decay in densely populated areas grew, the government proposed a more robust institutional framework to manage large-scale redevelopment projects.

## What happened
The **Urban Renewal Authority (市區重建局)** was formally established on **May 1, 2001**, under the Urban Renewal Authority Ordinance. Unlike the LDC, which operated with more commercial autonomy, the URA was granted broader powers to facilitate the resumption of private land for development. The authority adopted a strategy centered on the '4Rs': redevelopment, rehabilitation, revitalisation, and preservation. In the early years of its operation, the URA launched several high-profile projects in historic districts such as **Wan Chai (灣仔)** and **Kwun Tong (觀塘)**, shifting the urban landscape toward high-density residential and commercial towers.

## Significance
The creation of the URA represented a move toward a more interventionist state approach to urban planning. While proponents argued that the centralized authority was necessary to modernize the city’s skyline and improve fire safety in 'tong lau' tenements, the resulting projects were often criticized for prioritizing profitability and architectural uniformity over the maintenance of community social networks. The transition from the LDC to the URA set the precedent for the 'comprehensive development area' model, which remains a central, albeit debated, feature of Hong Kong urban planning. The impact of these redevelopment projects continues to be a subject of study, particularly regarding the tension between the necessity of modernizing outdated infrastructure and the preservation of Hong Kong’s local architectural and social heritage.

## Perspectives

- **HKSAR Government**: The government viewed the URA as an essential mechanism for curing urban blight and providing modern housing standards. They emphasized that the URA's enhanced powers were necessary to overcome the fragmented property rights that hindered private developers.
- **Local Community and Heritage Advocates**: Many residents and heritage activists argued that the URA’s focus on large-scale redevelopment often destroyed the organic character of old neighborhoods. They expressed concern that the displacement of long-term residents and the demolition of historic streetscapes prioritized developer profits over social cohesion.
