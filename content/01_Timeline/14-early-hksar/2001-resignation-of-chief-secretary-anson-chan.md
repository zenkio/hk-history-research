## Background

Following the handover of sovereignty in **1 July 1997**, **Anson Chan Fang On-sang** (陳方安生) remained in office as Chief Secretary—the head of the civil service—under the principle of "one country, two systems" and the *Hong Kong Basic Law* (香港基本法). Regarded by many local residents and international observers as a steadfast defender of the rule of law and civil service neutrality, **Chan** had previously served with distinction under the British colonial administration.

However, tensions soon emerged between her institutional approach and the governing style of Chief Executive **Tung Chee-hwa** (董建華). Disagreements reportedly spanned the implementation of accountability systems, freedom of speech, and the degree of deference owed to Beijing.

## What happened

On **13 January 2001**, **Chan** unexpectedly announced that she would step down in **April 2001**, several years ahead of the official retirement age. She stated that her decision was personal and that she had discussed the matter with Chief Executive **Tung** well in advance. Despite official denials of a rift, the announcement triggered widespread public speculation about political pressure from the Central People's Government in Beijing, which had previously expressed public dissatisfaction with the civil service's alignment with executive policy.

**Chan** formally left office on **30 April 2001**, and was succeeded by **Donald Tsang Yam-kuen** (曾蔭權), the Financial Secretary at the time.

## Significance

**Chan's** departure is widely viewed by historians as a symbolic turning point for the early HKSAR administration. It signaled the erosion of the old colonial-era bureaucratic autonomy and the gradual consolidation of a politically aligned executive-led government. While her supporters saw her exit as a regrettable loss for institutional checks and balances, critics and Beijing loyalists viewed it as a necessary step to ensure the civil service fully supported the Chief Executive's policy agenda.

## Perspectives

- **Pro-democracy and local Hong Kong perspective**: Viewed Anson Chan's resignation as a major blow to the integrity and independence of Hong Kong's civil service, interpreting it as forced out by Beijing due to her defense of the rule of law.
- **Beijing and pro-establishment perspective**: Framed her departure as a normal personnel transition and maintained that senior civil servants must show absolute loyalty to the Chief Executive and cooperate fully with executive-led governance.
