## Background
Prior to **July 1, 2002**, Hong Kong's governance heavily relied on its permanent, politically neutral civil service. Secretaries heading policy branches were career civil servants rather than political appointees. Following the **1997** handover, critics and the administration itself increasingly felt that the traditional model struggled to address complex social and economic challenges, as civil servants lacked a direct electoral or political mandate to absorb public pressure.

## What happened
At the start of his second term on **July 1, 2002**, Chief Executive **Tung Chee-hwa** (董建華) officially launched the Principal Officials Accountability System (主要官員問責制). Under this framework, key secretaries—such as the Chief Secretary for Administration, Financial Secretary, and Secretary for Justice—alongside newly created bureau director positions, stepped outside the civil service structure. Instead, they became political appointees serving at the pleasure of the Chief Executive and were required to resign if they committed serious policy failures. The reform aimed to enhance governance efficiency and foster stronger political leadership.

## Significance
Later scholarship and political analysts view the accountability system as a watershed moment in the governance of the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region. While designed to bridge the gap between the government and the public through enhanced political responsiveness, the system fundamentally altered the traditional neutrality and internal promotion pathways of the civil service. Debates persist over whether the reform successfully achieved genuine public accountability or merely concentrated power within the executive branch.

## Perspectives

- **Pro-establishment and Government View**: Advocates argued that the accountability system was essential for modernizing governance, ensuring officials took direct responsibility for their policy domains, and increasing executive efficiency.
- **Democratic and Civil Society View**: Critics contended that without a democratically elected Chief Executive or legislature, the system merely rebranded appointed officials without providing true accountability to the citizens of Hong Kong.
- **Civil Service Perspective**: Some within the traditional civil service expressed concern that injecting political appointees at the top levels could politicize a previously neutral bureaucracy and disrupt established institutional structures.
