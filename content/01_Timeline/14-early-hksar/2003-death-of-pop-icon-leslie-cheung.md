## Background
By the early 2000s, **Leslie Cheung** (張國榮) was widely recognized as one of the founding pillars of Cantopop and a defining icon of modern Hong Kong cinema. Rising to fame in the 1980s, he helped shape the golden age of Hong Kong popular culture alongside contemporaries like **Anita Mui** (梅艷芳). Beyond his commercial success, Cheung was celebrated for his artistic versatility, memorable film roles in masterpieces such as *Farewell My Concubine* (霸王別姬) and *Days of Being Wild* (阿飛正傳), and his public openness regarding his personal life during an era when the local entertainment industry rarely embraced sexual diversity.

## What happened
On **April 1, 2003**, Cheung fell from the 24th floor of the Mandarin Oriental Hotel (文華東方酒店) in Central, Hong Kong (香港島). He was rushed to Queen Mary Hospital (瑪麗醫院), where he was pronounced dead at the age of 46. Police later recovered a suicide note indicating that he had been suffering from severe depression. The tragedy occurred while Hong Kong was already gripped by intense anxiety and isolation due to the unfolding SARS (severe acute respiratory syndrome) epidemic.

## Significance
The sudden loss of such a major cultural figure compounded the collective trauma of a city already suffering from economic stagnation and the public health crisis. Later cultural scholarship often views Cheung's death as the symbolic end of an era of peak Hong Kong cultural confidence and creativity, frequently referred to as the city's golden age. Despite government appeals to avoid large gatherings due to the SARS outbreak, tens of thousands of grieving fans gathered outside the hotel and funeral venues to pay their final respects, demonstrating the profound emotional bond between the artist and the public.

## Perspectives

- **Local Hong Kong Public and Fans**: For many local residents, Cheung's suicide felt like a devastating personal loss and a dark psychological nadir for Hong Kong, arriving precisely when the city was besieged by the fear and isolation of the SARS epidemic.
- **Cultural Historians and Later Scholarship**: Academic analyses frequently interpret Cheung's death not merely as a personal tragedy, but as a poignant cultural metaphor marking the fading of the unique, cosmopolitan golden age of Hong Kong cinema and Cantopop at the turn of the millennium.
