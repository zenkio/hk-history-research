## Background
For decades, the Hong Kong government pursued a policy of land reclamation to expand the city's limited developable space. Victoria Harbour (維多利亞港) was significantly narrowed through these projects, which were often viewed as essential for economic growth and infrastructure development. By the late 1990s, however, public concern regarding the environmental impact and the loss of the harbor as a public asset began to intensify. The **Society for Protection of the Harbour** (保護海港協會) was founded in 1996 by **Christine Loh** (陸恭蕙) to advocate for the preservation of the remaining waterfront.

## What happened
The conflict culminated in legal action against the Town Planning Board's (城市規劃委員會) proposed reclamation projects in the Wan Chai (灣仔) district. On **January 9, 2003**, the Court of First Instance ruled in favor of the Society, declaring that the Town Planning Board had failed to adhere to the Protection of the Harbour Ordinance (保護海港條例). The court determined that the government must prove an "overriding public need" for any future reclamation projects. This ruling effectively halted several pending projects and forced the administration to rethink its urban planning strategies regarding the harbor.

## Significance
The legal victory is widely regarded by scholars as a watershed moment for civic engagement in the early HKSAR. It demonstrated that environmental and public interest groups could successfully challenge government policy through the judicial system. The event fostered a new era of 'civic environmentalism,' where citizens increasingly demanded transparency and sustainability in urban development. This movement shifted the discourse from purely economic utility to the recognition of the harbor as an irreplaceable heritage and public space.

## Perspectives

- **Government Planners and Pro-Development Advocates**: Authorities historically argued that reclamation was an unavoidable necessity to accommodate a rapidly growing population and essential transportation links. From this perspective, the judicial intervention introduced significant delays and costs to critical infrastructure projects intended for the public good.
- **Civic Activists and Environmentalists**: Activists viewed the harbor as a collective cultural and ecological asset that had been relentlessly degraded by decades of unchecked development. For this group, the court ruling represented a triumph of the rule of law over administrative convenience and a validation of the public's right to participate in urban planning.
