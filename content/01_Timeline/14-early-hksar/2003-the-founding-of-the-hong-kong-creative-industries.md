## Background
In the years following the 1997 handover, the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region (HKSAR) faced a series of profound economic challenges. The Asian Financial Crisis, combined with the structural transition of the manufacturing base toward the Pearl River Delta, left the economy in a state of volatility. This instability was exacerbated in **2003** by the outbreak of Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome (SARS), which severely impacted tourism, retail, and local consumer confidence. Facing a stagnant economy and high unemployment, the administration sought new pathways for growth that could leverage Hong Kong’s unique cultural position at the intersection of East and West.

## What happened
In **July 2003**, the government formally integrated the promotion of creative industries into its policy agenda. The strategy was articulated in the 2003 Policy Address, where it was argued that Hong Kong possessed the inherent human capital, legal framework, and international connectivity to become a regional hub for design, film, music, and digital media. The government initiated a series of task forces and consultative committees to identify 'creative industries'—a broad category encompassing sectors such as architecture, design, advertising, and the arts. This policy shift marked a transition from a strictly laissez-faire approach toward a more interventionist industrial policy designed to encourage high-value-added sectors. By **2004**, the government began mapping the economic contribution of these industries to the Gross Domestic Product (GDP), signaling a long-term commitment to institutionalizing cultural and creative production.

## Significance
The formal prioritization of creative industries represented a significant pivot in Hong Kong’s economic identity. By shifting focus toward 'soft power' and intellectual property, the government sought to diversify an economy previously reliant on finance, logistics, and real estate. While the policy successfully institutionalized the creative sector through the creation of future bodies like the Create Hong Kong (創意香港) office, it also sparked a long-standing debate regarding the degree to which state involvement can foster organic cultural development. The legacy of this 2003 initiative remains central to current discussions regarding the city's role within the Greater Bay Area (粵港澳大灣區) and the tension between economic growth and cultural autonomy.

## Perspectives

- **Governmental/Economic Perspective**: The administration viewed the creative industries as a vital engine for economic diversification and human capital development. Officials argued that state-led coordination was necessary to overcome market fragmentation and compete globally.
- **Local Cultural Community**: Some local artists and independent creators expressed concern that government prioritization might lead to 'sanitized' or state-approved art. They argued that creative autonomy could be compromised if funding and development were tied too closely to official economic metrics.
