## Background
Established originally during the British colonial administration in **1984**, the Committee on the Promotion of Civic Education (公民教育委員會) was tasked with advising the government on civic education matters. Following the transfer of sovereignty to the People's Republic of China on **July 1, 1997**, the committee underwent structural and thematic shifts. By the mid-2000s, particularly around **June 2004**, the body increasingly emphasized fostering a sense of national identity, Chinese history, and patriotism among the local population, moving away from strictly localized rights-based civic frameworks.

## What happened
The government-backed committee rolled out various campaigns, funding schemes, and educational toolkits aimed at strengthening ties between Hong Kong youth and the mainland. These initiatives frequently intersected with broader political discussions regarding Article 23 of the Basic Law and the scope of national education. While proponents argued that understanding China's modern development and cultural heritage was essential for post-colonial integration, critics contended that the campaigns prioritized political loyalty to the state over universal human rights, critical thinking, and local democratic participation.

## Significance
The evolution of the committee illustrates the complex ideological transition of Hong Kong's governance during the early HKSAR period. It highlights the ongoing tension between cultivating a state-aligned national identity and preserving distinct local civic traditions rooted in the city's unique socio-political history. The debates surrounding the committee's curriculum and promotional materials foreshadowed later, more intense societal polarization over national education reforms in the following decade.

## Perspectives

- **Pro-Beijing and Government Perspective**: Proponents maintained that strengthening national education and patriotism was a natural and necessary aspect of decolonization, helping residents reconnect with their Chinese roots and understand their responsibilities as citizens of the People's Republic of China.
- **Local Pro-Democracy and Civil Society Perspective**: Critics and local activists expressed concern that increased emphasis on patriotism was a form of political assimilation that undermined universal values, critical civic engagement, and the distinct local identity developed during the colonial era.
