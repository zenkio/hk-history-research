## Background

Following the Asian Financial Crisis and a subsequent property market downturn, the Hong Kong Housing Authority (香港房屋委員會) faced severe financial deficits. To divest from non-core operations, the government planned to package 180 shopping centres and car parks attached to public housing estates into a real estate investment trust known as Link REIT (領匯房地產投資信託基金, later renamed 領展). The initial public offering (IPO), scheduled for late 2004, was designed to raise billions of dollars and transfer the management of these community commercial facilities to the private sector.

## What happened

On **December 14, 2004**, just hours before the multi-billion dollar share offering was to be priced, public housing tenant **Lo Siu-lan** (盧少蘭) secured a legal stay from the Court of Appeal. Representing herself with pro bono legal assistance, **Lo Siu-lan** argued that the Housing Ordinance (房屋條例) did not empower the Housing Authority to divest itself of its core retail and parking facilities, and that the privatization would negatively impact vulnerable residents. Although the Court of Final Appeal ultimately ruled in favor of the Housing Authority in July 2005—allowing the listing to proceed—the eleventh-hour legal challenge forced a dramatic postponement and galvanized public opposition.

## Significance

The episode marked a watershed moment in early HKSAR history, highlighting the rising use of judicial review by ordinary citizens against government policies. It exposed deep societal anxieties regarding the neoliberal restructuring of public goods. While the government viewed the divestment as a necessary fiscal reform, critics and grassroots advocates argued that privatizing essential neighborhood amenities threatened community welfare and led to steep rent hikes under subsequent private management.

## Perspectives

- **HKSAR Government and Pro-Business Sectors**: Authorities maintained that the divestment was a vital fiscal measure to rationalize government operations and transfer commercial assets to the private market, where they could be managed more efficiently. They argued that the public consultation process was thorough and that the legal challenge unnecessarily delayed economic restructuring.
- **Grassroots Activists and Public Housing Tenants**: Community groups and residents viewed the challenge as a heroic defense of grassroots livelihoods against corporate encroachment. They contended that privatizing estate markets and car parks compromised the social safety net, leaving vulnerable tenants at the mercy of profit-driven landlords.
