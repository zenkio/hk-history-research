## Background

Following the transfer of sovereignty over Hong Kong in **1997**, the governance of the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region (香港特別行政區) was governed by the Basic Law (香港基本法). Articles 45 and 68 of the Basic Law outlined a potential progression toward the ultimate aim of selecting the Chief Executive and all members of the Legislative Council by universal suffrage. As the terms for the 2007 Chief Executive and 2008 Legislative Council approached, pro-democracy groups and political parties in Hong Kong increasingly campaigned for a concrete timetable to implement direct elections.

## What happened

On **April 6, 2004**, the Standing Committee of the National People's Congress (全國人民代表大會常務委員會) made a formal interpretation of Annex I and Annex II of the Basic Law. The NPCSC ruled out the introduction of universal suffrage for the 2007 Chief Executive election and the 2008 Legislative Council elections. It further stipulated that any amendments to the election methods must follow a strict "five-step process" involving initiation by the Chief Executive, approval by the NPCSC, and a two-thirds majority vote in the Legislative Council. This decision effectively halted immediate hopes for a rapid transition to full direct democracy in the territory.

## Significance

The April 2004 ruling marked a major turning point in Hong Kong's constitutional development under the "One Country, Two Systems" (一國兩制) framework. It clarified the central government's ultimate authority over Hong Kong's political reform process while deeply polarizing the local political landscape. While proponents of democracy viewed the decision as a setback for political rights and autonomy, pro-establishment figures and Beijing authorities emphasized the necessity of maintaining executive-led governance, social stability, and gradual, orderly political development.

## Perspectives

- **Beijing / Pro-Establishment**: Beijing authorities and pro-establishment politicians argued that the NPCSC interpretation was necessary to maintain constitutional order, ensure a smooth and gradual political evolution, and safeguard prosperity and stability under executive-led governance.
- **Hong Kong Pro-Democracy Movement**: Pro-democracy activists and politicians viewed the decision as a breach of the spirit of Hong Kong's high degree of autonomy and a direct denial of the public's aspirations for a representative and accountable government.
