## Background
Following the transfer of sovereignty in 1997, Hong Kong experienced a period of socio-economic transition. By the early 2000s, public dissatisfaction—epitomized by the mass protests on **July 1, 2003**—created a vacuum for alternative political discourse. Traditional news outlets were often perceived as cautious, leading to the rise of 'phone-in' or 'talk radio' formats on Commercial Radio Hong Kong (CRHK) (香港商業電台). These shows moved beyond simple news reporting to become interactive platforms where hosts offered sharp critiques of government policies.

## What happened
In **2004**, the influence of high-profile radio hosts reached a zenith. Notable figures such as **Albert Cheng** (鄭經翰) and **Wong Yuk-man** (黃毓民) utilized their programs to galvanize public opinion, frequently criticizing the HKSAR government and its supporters. The direct, often confrontational style of these shows resonated with a public seeking accountability. However, this period was marked by intense friction. In May **2004**, citing 'personal' reasons and pressure, several prominent hosts resigned from their posts. These departures were widely viewed by the public and media observers as a result of external political pressure, though the parties involved at the time offered varying explanations regarding the nature of this influence.

## Significance
This era signaled a shift in the role of media in Hong Kong, moving from a role of information dissemination to that of a public forum for political agitation. The 'Commercial Radio incident' of **2004** remains a critical case study in the history of local press freedom. It highlighted the tensions inherent in the 'One Country, Two Systems' (一國兩制) framework, particularly concerning the limits of free speech and the influence of political entities on private media corporations. The events of this time fundamentally shaped subsequent discussions about the autonomy of Hong Kong’s media landscape and the power of public opinion in an era of political sensitivity.

## Perspectives

- **Pro-democracy advocates and media critics**: Many viewed the 2004 resignations as a blatant act of political censorship orchestrated by the central or local government to silence critical voices. They argued that the pressure applied to the station owners undermined the editorial independence of Hong Kong’s private media.
- **The HKSAR Government and pro-establishment figures**: Government officials generally denied direct interference, maintaining that the resignations were internal management matters. Some pro-establishment voices argued that the radio hosts had crossed the line from balanced journalism to inciting social instability and personal vilification.
