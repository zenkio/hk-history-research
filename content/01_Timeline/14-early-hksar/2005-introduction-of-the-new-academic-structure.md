## Background
For decades, Hong Kong's education system followed the British model, characterized by a seven-year secondary school program and a three-year undergraduate degree. By the late 1990s, calls for comprehensive education reform emerged, driven by concerns that the existing system was overly specialized and failed to equip students with a sufficiently broad foundation for a globalized economy. Critics often pointed to the high-stakes nature of the Hong Kong Certificate of Education Examination (HKCEE) and the Hong Kong Advanced Level Examination (HKALE) as sources of immense pressure on students.

## What happened
On **May 11, 2005**, the Education and Manpower Bureau, led by Secretary for Education and Manpower **Arthur Li (李國章)**, officially announced the decision to implement the New Academic Structure (NAS). The reform sought to replace the '5+2' secondary school system with a '3+3' model, consisting of a three-year junior secondary and a three-year senior secondary program. This would be followed by a four-year university curriculum, moving away from the traditional three-year tertiary model. The policy aimed to promote a more holistic education, emphasizing Liberal Studies as a core component to foster critical thinking and social awareness among students.

## Significance
This reform represented one of the most substantial structural changes to the HKSAR education system since the 1997 handover. The transition intended to align Hong Kong's academic qualifications with international standards, particularly those in the United States and mainland China. However, the implementation phase faced significant challenges, including the massive logistical burden on schools to adapt their curriculum and the pressure placed on universities to overhaul their degree programs within a constrained timeframe. The transition process, which concluded with the first cohort graduating under the new system in 2012, remains a benchmark for the HKSAR government's effort to distance itself from colonial-era administrative structures in favor of a homegrown educational identity.

## Perspectives

- **Government and Policy Reformers**: Proponents argued that the '3+3+4' system was essential for human capital development, allowing students more time for whole-person development and reducing the extreme pressure of the colonial 'bottleneck' examination system.
- **Educators and Student Groups**: Many teachers and students expressed apprehension regarding the implementation, citing the rushed nature of the reform and the lack of sufficient training for the new Liberal Studies curriculum, which some feared might be politicized or lacked clear pedagogical standards.
