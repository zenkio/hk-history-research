## Background

By the early 2000s, the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region (HKSAR) government pursued extensive urban renewal and land reclamation projects along Victoria Harbour (維多利亞港) to expand the central business district. Plans for the Central and Wan Chai Reclamation (中環及灣仔填海計劃) necessitated the removal of the third-generation Central Star Ferry Pier (中環天星碼頭), an Edinburgh Place (愛丁堡廣場) landmark designed in the modernist Streamline Moderne style by architect **Architectural Services Department** (建築署) officials and opened in **1957**. While government planners viewed the demolition as essential for infrastructural connectivity and modern transport integration, a growing segment of civil society began to question the continuous erasure of collective memory in the name of economic development.

## What happened

On **November 12, 2006**, the historic pier operated its final cross-harbour sailings amid large public gatherings. Following its closure, a coalition of activists, heritage advocates, and ordinary citizens launched demonstrations and sit-ins to prevent the demolition. Protesters occupied the clock tower and surrounding areas, demanding that the structure be preserved on its original site. Tensions escalated over subsequent weeks as police cleared the site. Despite protracted resistance and hunger strikes, authorities proceeded with the demolition, and by early 2007, the iconic clock tower and pier structure were dismantled.

## Significance

The destruction of the Central Star Ferry Pier is widely regarded by historians and sociologists as a turning point in Hong Kong's social and political consciousness. The protests marked the transition of heritage conservation from an elite, bureaucratic concern into a grassroots mass movement, directly preceding the even larger preservation campaign over the Queen's Pier (皇后碼頭) in **2007**. Scholars suggest that this period fostered a distinct, place-based localist identity among younger generations, who increasingly mobilized around questions of urban space, cultural heritage, and community autonomy within the post-handover political landscape.

## Perspectives

- **HKSAR Government and Urban Planners**: Authorities emphasized that the demolition was necessary for critical transport infrastructure and land reclamation to maintain Hong Kong's status as a competitive international financial hub. They argued that public consultation had been duly conducted and that the pier's architectural and historical significance did not outweigh the demands of modern urban development.
- **Local Activists and Heritage Advocates**: Protesters and community preservationists viewed the demolition as an act of cultural vandalism that destroyed collective memory and public spaces to serve commercial and bureaucratic interests. For many younger participants, the struggle galvanized a broader questioning of government legitimacy and sparked a lasting commitment to protecting local heritage.
