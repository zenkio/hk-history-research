## Background
In the early years of the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region (HKSAR), the pro-democracy camp was largely dominated by pan-democratic parties that focused on legislative advocacy and institutional reform. Following the massive protests against Article 23 legislation in 2003, public demand for universal suffrage grew, yet many activists felt that the established democratic parties were too moderate or ineffective in challenging the HKSAR government and its policies. This dissatisfaction created a vacuum for a more militant, populist approach to advocacy that prioritized direct action and social justice issues.

## What happened
On **October 1, 2006**, during the National Day holiday, a group of outspoken activists, including **Leung Kwok-hung** (梁國雄)—widely known as 'Long Hair'—**Albert Chan** (陳偉業), and **Wong Yuk-man** (黃毓民), founded the **League of Social Democrats** (社會民主連線). The party distinguished itself by adopting a platform of 'radical' democracy, emphasizing street-level protests, filibustering in the Legislative Council (LegCo), and direct confrontation with government officials. Unlike the more diplomatic stance of traditional pan-democrats, the LSD sought to mobilize the working class and marginalized communities by articulating a clear, uncompromising opposition to the existing political order.

## Significance
The emergence of the LSD marked a distinct shift in Hong Kong’s political culture. By embracing confrontational tactics such as 'long-hair' style disruption and high-profile demonstrations, the party challenged the established norms of parliamentary decorum. Their rise forced other political groups to re-evaluate their strategies, contributing to a more polarized political environment. Whether this shift was a necessary development for democratic progress or an obstacle to functional governance remains a subject of intense debate among political analysts and observers.

## Perspectives

- **Pro-Democracy Activists**: Many activists viewed the LSD as a necessary corrective to the perceived complacency of moderate pan-democrats. They argued that confrontational tactics were the only way to pressure a government unresponsive to peaceful, conventional lobbying.
- **Pro-Establishment and Government Observers**: Critics from the pro-establishment camp often characterized the LSD’s activities as disruptive and populist. They argued that such tactics undermined legislative efficiency and damaged the social stability required for Hong Kong's economic growth.
