## Background
In **2006**, the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region (HKSAR) government conducted a mid-decade by-census. Following the transition of sovereignty in **1997**, Hong Kong experienced significant economic restructuring and demographic volatility. By the mid-2000s, policymakers sought updated data to better allocate resources, particularly as the city transitioned away from its post-war era of rapid manufacturing-led growth toward a more service-oriented, aging society.

## What happened
The **2006 Population By-census** was conducted between July 15 and July 31, 2006. It aimed to capture a snapshot of the living conditions and social composition of the territory. The results highlighted several key trends: the median age of the population had climbed, signaling a long-term trend of an aging society. Furthermore, the data reflected the ongoing influence of the One-way Permit scheme, which facilitated the arrival of individuals from Mainland China. The census recorded their residential patterns, employment sectors, and household integration, providing the most comprehensive evidence at the time regarding how these new residents were being absorbed into the local workforce and housing estates.

## Significance
The findings of the **2006** by-census became a cornerstone for urban planning and social welfare policy. It confirmed that the "dependency ratio"—the proportion of non-working-age individuals to the working-age population—was shifting in a way that placed pressure on healthcare and pension systems. Additionally, the census data sparked public discourse regarding social cohesion. While some analysts viewed the integration of Mainland migrants as a necessary response to a falling birth rate, others questioned the pace of assimilation and the resulting strain on public services. The data effectively ended the perception of Hong Kong as a youthful migrant society, replacing it with a picture of a more static, aging, and increasingly integrated demographic landscape.

## Perspectives

- **Governmental and Economic Planners**: Officials viewed the census as an essential tool for evidence-based governance, focusing on the fiscal sustainability of an aging population. They emphasized the necessity of planning for long-term demographic decline through infrastructure and social policy adjustments.
- **Community and Localist Advocates**: Some local commentators expressed concern that the rapid integration of immigrants from the Mainland would alter the unique cultural identity of Hong Kong (香港). They often pointed to the census data as evidence that social resources were being stretched thin, fueling debates over immigration quotas and social welfare access.
