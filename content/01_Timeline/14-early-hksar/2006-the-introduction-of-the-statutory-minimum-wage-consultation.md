## Background
For decades, Hong Kong’s economic policy was characterized by the principle of 'positive non-interventionism' (積極不干預主義). The government generally maintained that wage levels should be determined solely by market forces, arguing that mandated minimums would undermine competitiveness and lead to unemployment for low-skilled workers. However, following the 1997 Asian Financial Crisis and the subsequent economic downturn, income inequality became a prominent public concern. Grassroots advocacy groups and labor unions intensified their campaign for a wage floor, particularly for workers in the cleaning and security sectors who faced stagnant pay despite a recovering economy.

## What happened
Throughout **2006**, pressure mounted on the HKSAR government to address the plight of the working poor. While the administration initially resisted legislative intervention, public sentiment and the political influence of trade unions made the status quo increasingly difficult to maintain. On **January 1, 2006**, and throughout that year, the government began to pivot toward formal consultation processes. This marked the start of a multi-year policy evolution that would eventually transition from a voluntary 'Wage Protection Movement' (工資保障運動) to the eventual enactment of the Minimum Wage Ordinance in **2010**.

## Significance
The initiation of these consultations signaled a fundamental change in the relationship between the state, the market, and the workforce. Scholars note that the move represented a departure from the British colonial economic orthodoxy that had persisted well into the post-handover period. By considering statutory labor protections, the government acknowledged that the 'market-led' approach had left a significant portion of the population vulnerable. This shift remains a focal point in discussions regarding Hong Kong's transition from a purely free-market economy to one that incorporates elements of social welfare protection.

## Perspectives

- **Pro-business and free-market advocates**: Many business leaders and chambers of commerce expressed concern that a statutory minimum wage would increase operating costs for small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs). They argued that such policies could lead to inflation and discourage hiring, thereby harming the very workers the policy was intended to protect.
- **Labor unions and grassroots activists**: Labor advocates viewed the consultation as a necessary step to curb corporate exploitation and address the widening wealth gap. They argued that the 'market-led' model had failed to provide a living wage, necessitating government intervention to ensure basic human dignity and social stability.
