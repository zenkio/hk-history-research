## Background
Prior to 2008, Hong Kong lacked specific legislation prohibiting racial discrimination. While the Bill of Rights Ordinance (香港人權法案條例) and the Basic Law (基本法) provided broad protections regarding equality before the law, civil society organizations and international human rights bodies had long campaigned for a dedicated statutory framework. The debate intensified following the 1997 handover, as Hong Kong transitioned into the HKSAR. Discussions centered on whether existing common law remedies were sufficient or if a dedicated ordinance was necessary to align Hong Kong with international human rights standards.

## What happened
The **Race Discrimination Ordinance (RDO)** was passed by the Legislative Council (立法會) on **July 10, 2008**. The legislative process was lengthy, characterized by extensive consultations and debates regarding the scope of the bill. A significant point of contention during the drafting process was the inclusion of government functions and the treatment of immigrants, as well as the exemption of certain language and immigration policies. The ordinance eventually provided for the **Equal Opportunities Commission (平等機會委員會)** to handle complaints and investigate instances of discrimination, while also offering legal avenues for individuals to seek redress through civil courts.

## Significance
The passing of the RDO is widely viewed as a milestone in the evolution of Hong Kong’s human rights legal framework. For supporters, the law served as a vital tool to promote social harmony and address the needs of ethnic minority communities, which had grown more visible in the city's demographic landscape. Critics and observers, however, noted that the final version of the legislation was limited in scope, particularly due to the specific exemptions granted to the government regarding immigration and language-related policies. The enactment shifted the burden of proof in certain cases and mandated that public authorities take proactive steps to prevent discrimination, though the long-term efficacy of these mechanisms remains a subject of academic and public analysis.

## Perspectives

- **Human Rights Advocates**: Activists viewed the RDO as a long-overdue necessity to protect marginalized communities. They argued that while the final legislation was a compromise, it established an essential institutional mechanism for challenging systemic prejudice.
- **Governmental/Legislative Proponents**: The administration emphasized the need for a balanced approach that maintained executive autonomy in immigration and language policy. They argued that the ordinance provided sufficient protection while ensuring that the city's unique administrative needs were not compromised.
