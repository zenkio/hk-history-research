## Overview

The early years of the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region (HKSAR) from **1997** to **2008** marked a turbulent and transformative chapter in the city's modern history. Following the Transfer of Sovereignty over Hong Kong (**香港主權移交**) on **July 1, 1997**, the territory transitioned from British colonial administration to become a special administrative region of the People's Republic of China. Guided by the Sino-British Joint Declaration and the Basic Law (**香港基本法**), the formula of 'One Country, Two Systems' (**一國兩制**) was put into practice under the leadership of the territory's first Chief Executive, **Tung Chee-hwa** (**董建華**).

Almost immediately, the fledgling administration faced severe systemic challenges. Just as the government successfully defended the Hong Kong Dollar Peg (**港元聯繫匯率**) against intense speculative attacks during the **1997** Asian Financial Crisis, the city was struck by the H5N1 Bird Flu Outbreak (**H5N1禽流感疫情**) in **1997**, leading to a massive poultry cull to prevent a wider human epidemic. infrastructural modernization continued with the grand Opening of Chek Lap Kok International Airport (**香港國際機場啟用**) in **1998**, replacing the congested Kai Tak Airport. However, legal and political tensions quickly surfaced, notably with the First NPCSC Basic Law Interpretation on Right of Abode (**全國人大常委會第一次釋法**) in **1999**, which raised questions regarding judicial independence and the finality of the Court of Final Appeal.

## Key themes

The era was defined by institutional restructuring, economic restructuring, and unprecedented public mobilization. In **2000**, the government launched the Mandatory Provident Fund Scheme (**強制性公積金計劃**), establishing a formal, privately-managed retirement protection system for the workforce. The administrative apparatus underwent significant shifts with the resignation of Chief Secretary **Anson Chan** (**陳方安生**) in **2001**, followed by the Introduction of the Principal Officials Accountability System (**主要官員問責制**) in **2002**, which sought to align top bureaucrats more closely with the Chief Executive.

Public grievances intensified dramatically during public health and security crises. In **2003**, the SARS Outbreak Cripples the City (**沙士疫情**), severely impacting the local economy and claiming numerous lives, compounded by the tragic Death of Pop Icon Leslie Cheung (**張國榮逝世**). Economically, the central government and the HKSAR signed the Closer Economic Partnership Arrangement (**內地與香港關於建立更緊密經貿關係的安排**, CEPA) in **2003** and launched the Individual Visit Scheme (**個人遊**), which revitalized tourism by allowing mainland residents to travel independently to Hong Kong. Concurrently, the controversial push to enact national security legislation under Article 23 of the Basic Law sparked the Massive Protest Against Article 23 National Security Bill (**七一大遊行**) on **July 1, 2003**, drawing half a million citizens into the streets and leading to the shelving of the bill.

Political debates over democratization deepened when the NPCSC Rules Out 2007/2008 Universal Suffrage (**全國人大常委會關於二零零七年及二零零八年香港特別行政區行政長官和立法會產生辦法有關問題的決定**) in **2004**. Amid mounting political pressure and failing health, **Tung Chee-hwa** resigned in **2005**, paving the way for **Donald Tsang** (**曾蔭權**) to assume the role of Chief Executive. The latter half of the decade witnessed cultural and international milestones, including the Opening of Hong Kong Disneyland (**香港迪士尼樂園開幕**) in **2005**, the high-profile WTO Ministerial Conference Protests (**世貿 ministerial 會議示威**) in **2005**, the Demolition of Queen's Pier Sparks Heritage Movement (**皇后碼頭清拆事件**) in **2007** which galvanized local conservationists, and Hong Kong Hosts Olympic Equestrian Events (**香港奧運馬術比賽**) in **2008**.

## How it shaped Hong Kong

The events of **1997** to **2008** laid the structural, legal, and psychological groundwork for contemporary Hong Kong. The tension between rapid economic integration with mainland China and the preservation of distinct civil liberties became the defining paradox of the era. Public participation in governance shifted from passive acceptance under colonial rule to active civic engagement, permanently altering the political landscape and establishing a precedent for mass mobilization that would echo in subsequent decades.

## Perspectives

- **Beijing and Central Authorities**: Viewed the early HKSAR era as a successful restoration of national sovereignty and a period of vital economic rescue, emphasizing that integration with the mainland's booming economy through mechanisms like CEPA and the Individual Visit Scheme was essential for Hong Kong's long-term stability and prosperity.
- **Local Hong Kong Civil Society and Pro-Democracy Advocates**: Interpreted the era as a gradual erosion of local autonomy and delayed democratization, highlighting the massive 2003 protests and the fight over Article 23 and universal suffrage as critical moments in defending the rule of law, civil liberties, and the promise of 'Hong Kong people ruling Hong Kong'.
- **Later Historical Scholarship**: Characterizes the period as a complex transition phase where economic dependency on Beijing deepened alongside a growing divergence in political identity, setting up structural contradictions that would heavily influence future constitutional crises.
