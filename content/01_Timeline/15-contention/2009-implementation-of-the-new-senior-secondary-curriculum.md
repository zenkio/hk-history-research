## Background
Historically, Hong Kong’s education system followed the British model, characterized by a seven-year secondary course concluding in the Hong Kong Certificate of Education Examination (HKCEE) and the Hong Kong Advanced Level Examination (HKALE), followed by a three-year undergraduate program. By the early 2000s, calls for educational reform emerged, driven by a desire to align with international standards and to broaden the curriculum beyond the specialized stream structure.

## What happened
In **2009**, the Education Bureau officially launched the New Senior Secondary (NSS) curriculum, known as the '3-3-4' scheme. This structure condensed secondary schooling to six years (three years of junior secondary and three years of senior secondary) and transitioned local universities to a four-year degree program. A central component of this reform was the introduction of Liberal Studies (通識教育科) as a core subject, designed to encourage critical thinking and awareness of local and global issues. The reform necessitated the integration of the Hong Kong Diploma of Secondary Education (HKDSE) to replace the previous public examination system.

## Significance
The transition to the '3-3-4' system represents a major shift in the pedagogical landscape of Hong Kong. While proponents argued the change provided a more holistic education and removed the 'bottleneck' of the two-tier exam system, it also placed immense pressure on teachers and students to adapt to new assessment criteria. Critics have questioned whether the reform served to align Hong Kong’s educational objectives more closely with Mainland China’s national development goals, particularly regarding curriculum content and the emphasis on standardized civic awareness. The legacy of the curriculum, especially Liberal Studies, remains a subject of intense debate regarding its role in shaping the political identity of Hong Kong youth in the years leading up to 2020.

## Perspectives

- **Education Bureau and proponents**: The reform was viewed as a necessary modernization to improve accessibility to higher education and to foster well-rounded students who could compete in a global economy. It aimed to provide a broader knowledge base compared to the previous highly specialized subject structure.
- **Critics and localist observers**: Some scholars and activists argued that the curriculum changes were designed to emphasize national identity and streamline the education system with that of the People's Republic of China. Concerns were raised that the implementation process disregarded the autonomy of local educators and marginalized traditional critical inquiry.
