## Background
For decades, Hong Kong operated under a largely free-market, non-interventionist economic model with minimal government regulation regarding wages. However, rapid economic development alongside globalization contributed to widening wealth inequality and the stagnation of wages for grassroots laborers. As living costs rose significantly through the 2000s, labor unions and advocacy groups increasingly pressured the government to intervene. Critics argued that the absence of a wage floor left vulnerable workers susceptible to extreme exploitation, prompting prolonged debates over social justice versus market competitiveness.

## What happened
Following extensive legislative deliberation and fierce debate within the Legislative Council, the Minimum Wage Ordinance (最低工資條例) was passed. On **May 1, 2011**, the law officially took effect, establishing a baseline hourly pay rate of **HK$28**. This policy covered nearly all employees in the territory, with specific exemptions for certain categories such as live-in domestic helpers, student interns, and persons with disabilities under prescribed conditions. The Provisional Minimum Wage Commission was tasked with reviewing the rate periodically to account for inflation and economic shifts.

## Significance
The introduction of the statutory minimum wage marked a historic turning point for labor rights in Hong Kong. It signaled a departure from strict non-interventionism towards regulated worker protection. While proponents hailed the move as a long-overdue victory for social equity and poverty alleviation, it also generated ongoing discussions concerning potential inflationary pressures, increased operational costs for small businesses, and the broader implications for Hong Kong's economic competitiveness.

## Perspectives

- **Labor unions and grassroots advocates**: Viewed the minimum wage as a vital and overdue step toward basic dignity, wealth redistribution, and the protection of vulnerable workers from exploitative employment practices.
- **Business owners and free-market economists**: Expressed concern that mandatory wage floors would increase operational costs, stoke inflation, burden small-and-medium enterprises, and potentially diminish Hong Kong's overall economic competitiveness.
