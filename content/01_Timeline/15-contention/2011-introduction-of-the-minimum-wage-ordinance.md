## Background
For decades, Hong Kong operated under a largely unregulated capitalist model emphasizing free markets and minimal government intervention. While this fostered rapid economic growth, it also contributed to a widening wealth gap and extreme working poverty for low-skilled laborers. Calls for statutory wage protections grew louder through the 2000s, driven by labor unions and concern over stagnating wages amid rising living costs.

## What happened
On **May 1, 2011** (二零一一年五月一日), the **Minimum Wage Ordinance** (《最低工資條例》) officially took effect, setting an initial hourly wage floor at **HK$28**. The implementation followed extensive legislative deliberation and the establishment of the **Provisional Minimum Wage Commission** (臨時最低工資委員會) to review and recommend rate adjustments. Retail, catering, and security sectors experienced the most immediate adjustments as employers recalculated payrolls to comply with the new legal standard.

## Significance
This legislative milestone fundamentally altered the relationship between labor and capital in Hong Kong, challenging the territory's long-standing adherence to positive non-interventionism. It established a precedent for regular government intervention in wage structures, leading to ongoing biennial reviews of the rate in subsequent years.

## Perspectives

- **Labor advocates and grassroots organizations**: Hailed the policy as a long-overdue victory for social justice and worker dignity, arguing it provided a crucial safety net against exploitation in a high-cost city.
- **Business owners and corporate representatives**: Expressed deep concern that a mandatory wage floor would stoke inflation, drive up operating costs for small and medium enterprises, and reduce overall economic competitiveness.
