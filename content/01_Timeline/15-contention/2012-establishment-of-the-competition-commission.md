## Background
For decades, Hong Kong's economic policy was defined by the doctrine of 'positive non-interventionism' (積極不干預), a term popularized by Financial Secretary **John Cowperthwaite**. While this approach facilitated rapid growth and a low-tax environment, it also led to a market structure dominated by a small number of large conglomerates, particularly in sectors such as telecommunications, property, and supermarkets. By the early 2000s, calls for a cross-sector competition law grew louder, as critics argued that the lack of regulation stifled innovation and empowered cartels, ultimately harming consumer welfare.

## What happened
Following years of consultation and debate, the **Competition Ordinance** (競爭條例) was passed by the Legislative Council on **June 14, 2012**. The legislation was designed to prohibit anti-competitive conduct, such as price-fixing, market sharing, and the abuse of market power. Following the enactment, the **Competition Commission** (競爭事務委員會) was established as an independent statutory body tasked with enforcing the ordinance. While the law was officially passed in 2012, it took several years of preparation before it fully came into effect across all sectors in December 2015, signaling a measured approach to implementing market oversight.

## Significance
The establishment of the Competition Commission represented a fundamental shift in Hong Kong’s regulatory framework. It marked a transition from a system characterized by almost complete market freedom to one that acknowledges the necessity of regulatory intervention to ensure fair competition. Proponents argue this was a necessary modernization to align Hong Kong with international standards and protect the interests of smaller businesses and the general public. Conversely, skeptics have raised concerns about the potential for regulatory overreach and the burden imposed on businesses in a city that historically thrived on minimal government interference. The ongoing balance between these two philosophies remains a central theme in the city's modern economic governance.

## Perspectives

- **Pro-reform advocates**: Advocates argue that the law was long overdue to dismantle deep-seated oligopolies that hampered the city's economic vitality. They maintain that a fair playing field is essential for sustainable long-term growth and protecting consumer rights.
- **Pro-business skeptics**: Some business leaders and traditionalists expressed concern that the commission might introduce unnecessary bureaucracy. They worried that the shift away from 'positive non-interventionism' could undermine the efficiency and competitive edge that previously defined the Hong Kong economy.
