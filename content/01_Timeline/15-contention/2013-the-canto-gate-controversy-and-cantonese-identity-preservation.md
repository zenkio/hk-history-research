## Background
Following the 1997 handover, the Hong Kong government adopted a policy termed 'biliterate and trilingual' (兩文三語), emphasizing proficiency in written English and Chinese, alongside spoken Cantonese, English, and Putonghua (Mandarin). While Mandarin was gradually integrated into the school curriculum, the status of Cantonese as the primary medium of instruction remained largely unchallenged for over a decade. By the early 2010s, however, shifts in educational policy began to emphasize the importance of Mandarin proficiency for regional integration, leading to increased government funding and promotion of Putonghua-led education.

## What happened
In **2013**, the Education Bureau (教育局) published a controversial article on its website titled 'The Relationship Between Mainland China and Hong Kong' in its 'Deepening Understanding of Mainland China' series. The text suggested that Cantonese was not the 'mother tongue' of Hong Kong people, arguing that Mandarin was the standard dialect for Chinese people. This sparked immediate outrage among parents, educators, and activists, who labeled the incident 'Canto-gate' (粵語保衛戰). The public response was characterized by widespread protests on social media and organized gatherings, where demonstrators accused the government of attempting to marginalize Cantonese in favor of a monolithic national identity. Critics argued that the policy aimed to slowly phase out Cantonese as the primary language of instruction in favor of Mandarin, a process they termed 'Mandarinization' (推普).

## Significance
The controversy marked a pivotal moment in the articulation of a distinct 'Hong Kong identity.' For many, the defense of Cantonese was not merely a linguistic preference but a symbolic stand against political encroachment. Later scholarship notes that this event catalyzed a broader social movement focused on localism and the preservation of native heritage. While the government eventually clarified that its stance on Cantonese was not intended to discourage its use in daily life, the lasting impact of the controversy was a heightened sensitivity among the public toward any policy perceived to be diluting Hong Kong's local cultural uniqueness. The event remains a key case study in how language policy can become a surrogate for deeper political anxieties regarding autonomy and national integration.

## Perspectives

- **Government and Pro-Establishment perspective**: The government maintained that promoting Mandarin was a pragmatic educational goal, essential for Hong Kong's economic integration with the mainland and the professional advancement of its youth. From this viewpoint, the backlash was seen as a misunderstanding of educational objectives and an over-politicization of linguistic policy.
- **Localist and Civil Society perspective**: Critics and localist groups viewed the incident as a deliberate, top-down attempt to erase Hong Kong's unique cultural identity and replace it with a homogenized national identity. They argued that Cantonese is the bedrock of Hong Kong's distinct social cohesion and must be protected as a foundational element of local heritage.
