## Background
In the years following the 1997 handover, the discourse surrounding the implementation of "universal suffrage" as outlined in the Basic Law (基本法) remained a central point of contention. While the central government in Beijing asserted that political reform must follow the framework established by the National People's Congress Standing Committee, many pro-democracy advocates argued that progress toward electing the Chief Executive (行政長官) and the Legislative Council (立法會) had stalled. By early 2013, a sense of urgency grew among activists who believed that conventional lobbying and legislative pressure were insufficient to secure a democratic model that met international standards.

## What happened
On **March 27, 2013**, **Benny Tai Yiu-ting** (戴耀廷), **Chan Kin-man** (陳健民), and **Chu Yiu-ming** (朱耀明) published an article in the *Hong Kong Economic Journal* (信報) titled "Civil Disobedience is the Most Powerful Weapon" (公民抗命是最大的武器). In this manifesto, they formally introduced the concept of "Occupy Central with Love and Peace" (讓愛與和平佔領中環), often abbreviated as "Occupy Central" (佔中). 

The trio proposed a campaign of non-violent civil disobedience, suggesting that thousands of people should peacefully occupy the streets of the Central (中環) financial district to pressure the government into implementing universal suffrage. They emphasized the principles of non-violence, legality, and public deliberation, intending for the movement to act as a last resort should official political reform consultations fail to offer a "genuine" democratic choice. Throughout 2013, the movement conducted several "deliberation days" to refine their proposals and gauge public sentiment, effectively setting the stage for the political confrontations that would follow in subsequent years.

## Significance
The launch of the Occupy Central concept marked a shift in Hong Kong’s protest culture, transitioning from petitioning and mass marches toward the threat of direct action. It polarized public opinion, drawing both support from those frustrated by the perceived delay in democratic reform and criticism from those who feared the economic and social stability of the city would be compromised. The discourse initiated by the proposal established a framework for future mass mobilizations, including the 2014 protests, and remains a defining point in the study of post-colonial Hong Kong's constitutional development.

## Perspectives

- **Pro-democracy activists**: Advocates viewed the movement as a moral and necessary act of civil disobedience to achieve the promise of universal suffrage granted under the Basic Law. They maintained that peaceful occupation was a legitimate democratic tool to challenge what they perceived as the central government's restrictive interpretation of electoral reform.
- **Beijing and Pro-establishment camp**: The central government and local pro-establishment figures condemned the movement as an illegal attempt to disrupt Hong Kong's economic hub and undermine the rule of law. They argued that such actions were provocative and incompatible with the principle of 'One Country, Two Systems' (一國兩制) and the constitutional framework set by the National People's Congress.
