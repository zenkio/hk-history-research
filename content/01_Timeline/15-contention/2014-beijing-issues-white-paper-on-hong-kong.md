## Background

Since the handover of sovereignty in 1997, the governance of Hong Kong has operated under the principle of "One Country, Two Systems" (一國兩制), rooted in the **Sino-British Joint Declaration** (中英聯合聲明) and the **Hong Kong Basic Law** (香港基本法). While Beijing held ultimate sovereignty, the degree of autonomy and the definition of "Hong Kong people governing Hong Kong" (港人治港) remained subjects of ongoing interpretation and political debate between mainland authorities, local democrats, and international observers.

## What happened

On **June 10, 2014**, the State Council Information Office of the People's Republic of China published a white paper titled "The Practice of the 'One Country, Two Systems' Policy in the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region" (「一國兩制」在香港特別行政區的實踐). For the first time in an official policy document of this scale, Beijing explicitly emphasized its "comprehensive jurisdiction" (全面管治權) over the territory, asserting that the high degree of autonomy granted to Hong Kong was not full autonomy, but was entirely derived from and subordinate to the central government. The document also noted that judges and judicial personnel in Hong Kong were part of the "administrators" required to love the country, raising questions locally about the traditional independence of the judiciary.

## Significance

The release of the white paper sparked immediate and widespread controversy in Hong Kong. Pro-democracy politicians, legal scholars, and civil society groups viewed the document as a direct challenge to the promised high degree of autonomy and judicial independence. Conversely, Beijing loyalists and pro-establishment figures defended the white paper as a necessary clarification of the constitutional order and a reinforcement of national security and sovereignty. The document is widely seen by historians as a crucial milestone that heightened political polarization in the city, preceding the mass protests of the **Umbrella Movement** (雨傘運動) later that year.

## Perspectives

- **Beijing and Pro-Establishment View**: The central government and its supporters maintained that the white paper did not alter existing policies but merely clarified the correct constitutional relationship between the central authorities and the Special Administrative Region. They argued that asserting comprehensive jurisdiction was necessary to prevent foreign interference and ensure stability.
- **Pro-Democracy and Local Legal Community View**: Local pro-democracy groups and members of the legal profession perceived the document as a significant erosion of the "Two Systems" framework. They contended that redefining judges as political administrators threatened the independence of Hong Kong's judiciary and violated assurances given in the Basic Law.
