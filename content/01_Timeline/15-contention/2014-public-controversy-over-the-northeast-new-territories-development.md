## Background
The **Northeast New Territories New Development Areas (東北新界新發展區)** planning project was proposed to address Hong Kong's long-term housing shortage and land demand. The plan aimed to transform rural areas, primarily in **Kwu Tung (古洞)**, **Fanling North (粉嶺北)**, and **Ping Che/Ta Kwu Ling (坪輋/打鼓嶺)**, into high-density urban residential and commercial zones. While the government emphasized the necessity of these projects for urban development and economic expansion, local residents and activists expressed significant concerns regarding the displacement of long-term farming communities and the destruction of rural ways of life.

## What happened
On **2014-06-13**, the Legislative Council’s Finance Committee held a meeting to discuss funding for the pre-construction works of the project. Thousands of protesters, including residents from the affected villages and various activist groups, gathered outside the Legislative Council Complex in **Admiralty (金鐘)**. Protesters argued that the government failed to conduct sufficient public consultation and disregarded the rights of indigenous and non-indigenous villagers facing eviction. The protest escalated when demonstrators attempted to storm the Legislative Council building. Police were deployed to clear the area, resulting in clashes that led to several arrests. The incident marked one of the most visible instances of public resistance to development policies during this era.

## Significance
The controversy underscored a deepening divide in Hong Kong society regarding the prioritization of development versus conservation. To some, the project represented a vital step in solving the city's housing crisis; to others, it was an example of 'executive-led' governance that bypassed the concerns of marginalized rural communities. The events of **2014-06-13** served as a catalyst for broader discussions on land justice and the 'right to the city,' influencing subsequent social movements throughout the decade. The tension remains a study in how Hong Kong's complex land tenure system, shaped by both colonial-era legacy and modern urban planning, continues to create friction between state-led urban growth and local identity preservation.

## Perspectives

- **HKSAR Government and Pro-Development Advocates**: The government maintained that the development was essential to provide necessary housing for Hong Kong’s growing population. They argued that the planning process was conducted through official channels and that relocation packages were designed to provide fair compensation to affected residents.
- **Villagers and Local Activists**: Affected communities viewed the development as an act of 'forced eviction' that destroyed established social networks and sustainable agricultural practices. They contended that the project favored property developers and commercial interests over the traditional rural heritage and the livelihoods of long-term residents.
