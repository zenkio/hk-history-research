## Background
In the years leading up to **2015**, Hong Kong's public housing program, managed by the **Housing Authority (香港房屋委員會)**, faced immense pressure to complete estates quickly to meet housing demands. Plumbing subcontractors utilized soldering materials containing lead, a practice that drew intense scrutiny once public health alarms were raised.

## What happened
On **July 10, 2015**, the Democratic Party publicly revealed that tests conducted at **Kai Ching Estate (啟晴邨)** in **Kowloon City (九龍城)** showed lead levels in drinking water exceeding World Health Organization guidelines. As testing expanded to other recently completed public housing developments, including **Kwai Luen Estate (葵聯邨)** in **Kwai Chung (葵涌)**, excessive lead was detected across multiple sites. Residents experienced deep anxiety, leading to the distribution of bottled water, blood tests for affected children and pregnant women, and the installation of temporary water filters. The scandal prompted the government to establish a Commission of Inquiry to investigate the root causes and fix accountability.

## Significance
The lead water scandal severely damaged public trust in government oversight, housing construction quality control, and public utilities management. It highlighted systemic vulnerabilities within outsourced government projects and subcontracting systems, sparking ongoing debates over public accountability, regulatory enforcement, and bureaucratic transparency in Hong Kong.

## Perspectives

- **Pro-establishment and Government View**: Authorities emphasized that the issue stemmed from localized plumbing practices by individual contractors rather than a systemic regulatory collapse. Officials acted swiftly to provide alternative water supplies, conduct blood tests, and set up an independent commission of inquiry to address the failures.
- **Pan-democrat and Local Resident View**: Critics and affected residents argued that the scandal exposed deep-seated corruption, systemic negligence, and a lack of transparency within the Housing Authority and government departments. They maintained that relying on independent inquiries was insufficient to restore public faith without structural bureaucratic reforms.
