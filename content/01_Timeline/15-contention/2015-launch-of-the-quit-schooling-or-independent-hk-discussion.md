## Background
Following the conclusion of the **2014** Umbrella Movement (雨傘運動), which sought universal suffrage for the Chief Executive election, a sense of political fatigue and disillusionment emerged among segments of the student population. While previous pro-democracy efforts focused on reform within the framework of the Basic Law (基本法), a growing faction began to question the efficacy of these methods, leading to an interest in more radical political ideologies.

## What happened
In **2015**, student union publications, most notably at the **University of Hong Kong** (香港大學), began to feature editorial content discussing the prospect of Hong Kong independence. The publication *Undergrad* (學苑) released a series of articles examining the city's self-determination and sovereignty. These discussions were initially framed as academic inquiries into the city's historical and political status, but they quickly evolved into a broader public debate. The term "quit schooling" (罷課) had previously been utilized during the **2014** protests as a tactic of civil disobedience, and this mobilization experience provided the infrastructure for these new debates. As university students became the primary catalysts, the discourse moved away from the moderate reformist goals of traditional pan-democratic groups and toward the idea that Hong Kong needed to consider its existential status independent of the mainland.

## Significance
This shift marked a profound transition in Hong Kong politics. Prior to **2015**, the consensus among major political factions generally assumed the legitimacy of the "One Country, Two Systems" (一國兩制) framework. The emergence of open discussion regarding independence challenged the established political boundaries. While the long-term impact of these specific publications is still debated by historians, it is clear that they forced the Central People's Government and the Hong Kong SAR government to re-evaluate local political stability. These debates laid the intellectual groundwork for later political developments, including the rise of localist candidates in the **2016** Legislative Council elections. By moving the discourse toward the question of sovereignty, these student movements fundamentally altered the relationship between Hong Kong's civil society and the state.

## Perspectives

- **Pro-Beijing and Government view**: The central government and pro-establishment figures viewed these discussions as a direct violation of the Basic Law and a threat to national security. They argued that such discourse undermined the sovereignty of the People's Republic of China and ignored the historical reality that Hong Kong has been part of China since antiquity.
- **Localist and Student Activist view**: Proponents of these discussions argued that the failure of democratic reform efforts demonstrated that existing constitutional frameworks were insufficient to protect Hong Kong's autonomy. They viewed the debates as a legitimate exercise of freedom of speech and a necessary response to perceived political erosion.
