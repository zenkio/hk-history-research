## Background
In **2015**, the University of Hong Kong (香港大學), frequently referred to as HKU, became the focal point of a significant debate regarding institutional autonomy. The central issue involved the long-delayed appointment of a Pro-Vice-Chancellor (academic staffing and resources). The search committee had recommended **Johannes Chan** (陳文敏), a former Dean of Law and a well-known liberal scholar, for the position. However, the university's governing Council (校委會) repeatedly deferred the appointment, citing the need for the candidate to hold a full professorship, a move critics argued was a departure from established university protocol.

## What happened
On **July 28, 2015**, following months of mounting frustration, members of the Hong Kong University Students' Union (香港大學學生會) and other protesters surrounded the venue of the Council meeting at the Meng Wah Complex (孟華科技樓). Students demanded an immediate response regarding the status of the appointment. As the meeting concluded, Council members were physically blocked from leaving the building. The protest escalated into a tense standoff between the students and Council members, including Chairman **Leung Chun-ying** (梁振英), who, in his capacity as Chief Executive, appointed a significant portion of the Council members, and Council Chairman **Leong Che-hung** (梁智鴻).

## Significance
By September 2015, the Council officially rejected **Johannes Chan**'s appointment. The incident became a defining moment for Hong Kong's civil society, symbolizing broader anxieties regarding the encroachment of political influence on tertiary education. Supporters of the students viewed the protest as a necessary defense of academic freedom and institutional integrity. Conversely, others expressed concern regarding the methods used by the protesters, arguing that the disruption of university governance undermined the rule of law and the autonomy of the Council itself. This event is often cited by scholars as a precursor to the heightening tensions between student activists and the administration that characterized the years leading up to 2020.

## Perspectives

- **Student Activists and Academic Staff**: Many students and faculty argued that the delay and subsequent rejection were politically motivated retaliation for Johannes Chan's past advocacy for democratic reform. They viewed the Council's actions as a dangerous erosion of institutional independence under pressure from the Beijing-backed government.
- **Council Members and Pro-Establishment Supporters**: Council members and supporters of the administration maintained that they followed internal procedures and acted in the best interest of the university's stability. They criticized the student blockade as an undemocratic act that intimidated university officials and threatened the sanctity of academic governance.
