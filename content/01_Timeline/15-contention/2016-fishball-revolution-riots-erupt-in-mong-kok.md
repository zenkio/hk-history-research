## Background

Street hawking during the Lunar New Year (農曆新年) has long been a traditional part of Hong Kong's festive culture, providing both affordable seasonal foods like fishballs and livelihood opportunities for grassroots vendors. However, the Food and Environmental Hygiene Department (食物環境衛生署), backed by the Hong Kong Police Force (香港警務處), traditionally maintained a strict enforcement policy against unlicensed hawkers during the holidays to manage crowd control and public hygiene. In the years following the 2014 Umbrella Movement (雨傘運動), political tensions in Hong Kong were exceptionally high. Localist groups, which emphasized the preservation of local culture and autonomy from mainland influence, began actively intervening in hawker management operations to challenge government authority and protect traditional vendors.

## What happened

On the night of **February 8, 2016** (Lunar New Year's Day), tensions boiled over in the densely populated commercial district of Mong Kok (旺角). Government inspectors attempted to clear unlicensed food hawkers operating on Portland Street (砵蘭街). As crowds gathered to support the vendors, localist activists, notably members of Indigenous Hong Kong (本土民主前線), arrived at the scene to confront the authorities. The confrontation quickly escalated into violent clashes as protesters threw bricks torn from pavements, glass bottles, and wooden pallets at police officers, while also setting fires in the streets. In response, police deployed pepper spray, batons, and fired two warning shots into the air—a rare and controversial use of live ammunition by the Hong Kong police force. The unrest continued into the early hours of **February 9, 2016**, resulting in extensive property damage, injuries to dozens of police officers, journalists, and participants, and numerous arrests.

## Significance

The events of **February 2016**, widely dubbed the "Fishball Revolution" by participants and local media, marked a profound and radical shift in Hong Kong's political landscape. It signaled to observers that some factions of the opposition movement had moved beyond the largely peaceful civil disobedience of the Umbrella Movement toward direct, physical confrontation with the state. The clashes intensified debates over the limits of local culture, government enforcement policies, and the rising tide of localism. In the aftermath, several prominent localist figures faced severe legal consequences, leading to convictions and prison sentences for rioting and assault, which further reshaped the trajectory of Hong Kong's political opposition.

## Perspectives

- **Government and Police**: Authorities viewed the unrest as a premeditated, violent riot that threatened public safety and law and order, necessitating a firm police response to protect officers and restore public peace.
- **Localists and Protesters**: Participants and sympathetic localists framed their actions as a defensive community response against heavy-handed governance, cultural erasure, and the erosion of local traditions during the festive season.
