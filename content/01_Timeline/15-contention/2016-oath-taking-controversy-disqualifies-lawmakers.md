## Background

On **October 12, 2016**, the Legislative Council (立法會) of the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region held its swearing-in ceremony for the newly elected sixth-term legislators. Article 104 of the Basic Law (基本法) stipulates that when assuming office, members of the Legislative Council must swear to uphold the Basic Law and swear allegiance to the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region of the People's Republic of China. This procedural requirement soon became a focal point for intense political contention amid growing localist sentiment following the 2014 Umbrella Movement.

## What happened

During the ceremony, several newly elected lawmakers—notably **Yau Wai-ching** (游蕙禎) and **Baggio Leung** (梁頌恆) of Youngspiration (青年新政)—intentionally altered the prescribed wording of the oath. They displayed a banner reading "Hong Kong is not China," used derogatory language to refer to China, and mispronounced the country's name. Other pan-democratic lawmakers, such as **Lau Siu-lai** (劉小麗), added extraneous phrases or read the oath at an unusually slow pace. 

The clerk to the Legislative Council refused to administer the oaths to Yau and Leung, leading to chaotic scenes and subsequent meetings where pro-establishment lawmakers staged walkouts to prevent a quorum. In November 2016, the Standing Committee of the National People's Congress (全国人民代表大会常务委员会) exercised its power under Article 158 of the Basic Law to issue an interpretation of Article 104, stating that oath-taking must be sincere and solemn, and that failure to do so legally precludes a person from holding office. Subsequently, Hong Kong courts ruled that Yau, Leung, and several other legislators—including Lau Siu-lai, **Nathan Law** (羅冠聰), **Yiu Chung-yim** (姚松炎), and **Kwok Ka-ki** (though Kwok was not disqualified at this initial stage, four others joined the unseated group later)—had declined or failed to take their oaths properly, effectively unseating them.

## Significance

The oath-taking controversy marked a major turning point in Hong Kong's political history. It demonstrated Beijing's willingness to intervene directly in local legislative matters via Basic Law interpretations before local judicial exhaustion. For the pro-democracy and localist camps, the disqualifications were viewed as a severe blow to the separation of powers and the representation of voters' choices. For Beijing and the Hong Kong government, the episode was framed as a necessary defense of national sovereignty, constitutional order, and the rule of law.

## Perspectives

- **Beijing and Hong Kong Governments**: Authorities viewed the altered oaths as a direct challenge to national sovereignty and China's territorial integrity, arguing that individuals who refuse to pledge allegiance to the state cannot legally serve in its government.
- **Localists and Pro-Democracy Camp**: Critics of the disqualifications argued that the actions were a legitimate form of political expression and protest, and that retroactively applying an NPC standing committee interpretation violated judicial independence and disenfranchised hundreds of thousands of voters.
