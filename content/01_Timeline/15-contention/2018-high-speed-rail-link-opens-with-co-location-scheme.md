## Background
Planning for the Guangzhou-Shenzhen-Hong Kong Express Rail Link (廣深港高速鐵路) began in the 2000s, aiming to integrate Hong Kong's transportation infrastructure with the rapidly expanding mainland Chinese high-speed railway network. The central component of the project was the massive West Kowloon Station (西九龍站), designed to handle millions of cross-border passengers annually. 

## What happened
On **September 23, 2018**, the rail link officially opened to the public. To maximize travel efficiency, the Hong Kong government and Beijing authorities agreed to implement a 'co-location' arrangement (一地兩檢). Under this scheme, a designated 'Mainland Port Area' was established within the West Kowloon terminus. Passengers clearing customs and immigration for mainland China would do so inside Hong Kong territory, where mainland Chinese laws—including criminal jurisdiction and law enforcement powers—would apply.

## Significance
The implementation of the co-location arrangement marked a major milestone in physical and logistical integration between Hong Kong and mainland China, drastically reducing travel times to Guangzhou, Shenzhen, and major mainland hubs. However, the project was also a watershed moment in Hong Kong's political contention. Critics and pan-democratic politicians argued that ceding jurisdiction within Hong Kong to mainland law enforcement violated the Basic Law (香港特別行政區基本法), while supporters maintained it was legally sound, necessary for the economic viability of the railway, and authorized under the legal framework established by the National People's Congress Standing Committee.

## Perspectives

- **Pro-establishment and Beijing Authorities**: Proponents argued that the co-location arrangement was a practical necessity that maximized the convenience and economic benefits of high-speed rail travel. They maintained that the legal basis was fully supported by decisions of the National People's Congress Standing Committee and did not undermine the 'One Country, Two Systems' framework.
- **Pan-democrats and Local Critics**: Critics contended that the scheme constituted a dangerous precedent and a direct violation of Article 18 of the Basic Law, which states that national laws shall not be applied in Hong Kong except for those listed in Annex III. They argued it eroded the boundaries of Hong Kong's legal autonomy.
