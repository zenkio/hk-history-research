## Background

Planning for the Guangzhou-Shenzhen-Hong Kong Express Rail Link (廣深港高速鐵路) began in the 2000s to integrate Hong Kong into China's rapidly expanding national high-speed rail network. The project designated the **West Kowloon Station** (香港西九龍站) as its sole terminus in the territory. To maximize travel efficiency, the Hong Kong and Beijing governments proposed an 'co-location' or 'one-check, two-in-one' (一地兩檢) arrangement. This scheme required establishing a **Mainland Port Area** inside the Hong Kong terminus where mainland Chinese laws and border control would apply, departing from the historical practice where Hong Kong law applied throughout the entire territory.

## What happened

On **September 23, 2018**, the West Kowloon Station officially opened, connecting Hong Kong directly to mainland cities such as Shenzhen and Guangzhou, as well as the broader national rail grid. Prior to the opening, the Legislative Council passed the local enabling legislation amid chaotic scenes and procedural disputes. Critics and pro-democracy lawmakers argued that ceding jurisdiction over a portion of Hong Kong soil to mainland authorities violated the Basic Law (香港特別行政區基本法), specifically Article 18, which generally prohibits the application of national mainland laws in the Special Administrative Region except for a specified list of national laws related to defense and foreign affairs.

## Significance

The controversy surrounding the West Kowloon Station boundary crossing highlighted deep societal divisions over constitutional interpretations of the Basic Law. It served as a major flashpoint in Hong Kong's political contention era, preceding later and more expansive debates over legal integration with mainland China. The station's operation established a novel legal precedent regarding jurisdiction and territorial governance under the 'One Country, Two Systems' (一國兩制) principle, the long-term implications of which continue to be analyzed by legal scholars and political commentators.

## Perspectives

- **Pro-Beijing and Hong Kong Government**: Proponents argued that co-location was essential for maximizing the economic and transport benefits of the high-speed rail, allowing passengers to save time by completing immigration and customs procedures in a single location. They maintained that the legal arrangements fully complied with the Basic Law and had been endorsed by the Standing Committee of the National People's Congress.
- **Local Pro-Democracy and Legal Critics**: Critics and independent legal scholars contended that the establishment of the Mainland Port Area unconstitutionally carved out a portion of Hong Kong territory from local jurisdiction, breaching the promises of the 'One Country, Two Systems' framework. They viewed the decision as a dangerous precedent that undermined the rule of law and the autonomy guaranteed to the Special Administrative Region.
