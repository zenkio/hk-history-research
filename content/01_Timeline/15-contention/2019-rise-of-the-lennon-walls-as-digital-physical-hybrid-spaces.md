## Background
During the **2019** anti-extradition bill protests, public spaces throughout Hong Kong (香港) were transformed by the rapid appearance of 'Lennon Walls' (連儂牆). These were designated areas where protesters posted colorful sticky notes, posters, and art to express political demands. The concept originated from the original **Lennon Wall** in Prague, which featured imagery of John Lennon, and had previously appeared in Hong Kong during the **2014** Umbrella Movement (雨傘運動).

## What happened
By mid-**2019**, these displays moved beyond specific protest sites to permeate residential neighborhoods, footbridges, and MTR station exits. The movement was characterized by its spontaneous, leaderless nature. Participants utilized digital platforms like **Telegram** and **LIHKG** to organize the creation, maintenance, and defense of these walls. This synergy between digital mobilization and physical manifestations allowed for a rapidly evolving visual discourse that could be updated in real-time, effectively blurring the lines between online discourse and urban geography. As tensions escalated, the walls became flashpoints for conflict between groups with opposing political views, leading to frequent vandalism and protective vigils.

## Significance
These installations are often cited by scholars as a hallmark of 21st-century protest, representing a shift toward 'networked' dissent. By decentralizing political messaging, the Lennon Walls made it difficult for authorities to suppress the movement through traditional means. The walls served not only as bulletin boards for demands—such as the 'Five Demands' (五大訴求)—but also as a shared urban canvas for artistic expression. The proliferation of these walls fundamentally altered the visual landscape of the city, turning mundane infrastructure into sites of intense political contestation and collective identity formation.

## Perspectives

- **Pro-Democracy Supporters**: Viewed the walls as an essential exercise of free speech and a means to maintain community morale. They emphasized the collaborative and non-violent nature of the postings as a symbol of unity.
- **Pro-Beijing Supporters and Government Authorities**: Characterized the walls as a public nuisance and a form of vandalism that disrupted social order. They argued that the messages contained divisive or illegal rhetoric that contributed to the radicalization of youth.
