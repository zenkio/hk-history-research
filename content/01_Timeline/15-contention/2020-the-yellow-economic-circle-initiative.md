## Background
Following the outbreak of large-scale protests in **2019** related to the proposed extradition bill, Hong Kong experienced deep social polarization. As institutional political channels faced gridlock and public distrust toward government-aligned entities grew, activists began seeking alternative methods to exert influence. By **early 2020**, this led to the formalization of the 'Yellow Economic Circle' (黃色經濟圈), a grassroots mobilization aimed at creating a distinct economic ecosystem to support the movement's objectives.

## What happened
Participants in the movement categorized businesses using a color-coding system: 'yellow' shops were those perceived to support or sympathize with the pro-democracy movement, while 'blue' shops were identified as supporting the government or the police. Supporters utilized mobile applications, social media platforms, and online maps to identify and patronize yellow-listed establishments. The movement encompassed various sectors, including restaurants, retail stores, and service providers. Proponents framed this as a form of 'spending with a conscience,' arguing that economic power could be used to sustain the livelihoods of activists and protect the 'yellow' economy from what they described as political suppression.

## Significance
The initiative marked a departure from traditional street protests, shifting the focus toward decentralized, consumer-driven mobilization. Supporters viewed it as a vital tool for long-term resistance and community building, intended to insulate the movement from state economic pressure. Conversely, critics and government officials argued that the practice was discriminatory and divisive, contending that it harmed the local economy by politicizing commercial transactions and creating artificial market barriers. The initiative remains a subject of intense debate, with some economists questioning the feasibility and scalability of such boycotts, while political scholars study it as an unconventional method of civil resistance in a restrictive political environment.

## Perspectives

- **Pro-democracy activists**: Advocates viewed the movement as a legitimate exercise of consumer choice to foster social solidarity and economic independence. They argued it was a necessary defensive mechanism to prevent the marginalization of their values in a shrinking civil space.
- **The Hong Kong Government and pro-establishment figures**: Government officials and critics categorized the movement as 'black-clad' economic coercion. They claimed the initiative promoted discrimination, fragmented society, and undermined the free-market principles that underpin Hong Kong's economic success.
