## Background
Following the implementation of the **Law of the People's Republic of China on Safeguarding National Security in the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region** (中華人民共和國香港特別行政區維護國家安全法) in June **2020**, the administration of **Chief Executive Carrie Lam** (林鄭月娥) began structural reforms to the public sector. The government cited Article 6 of the National Security Law, which requires the region to take measures to strengthen supervision and regulation of public servants, as the legal foundation for the new policy. Historically, the civil service in Hong Kong was expected to remain politically neutral, maintaining the same administrative standards regardless of the political climate.

## What happened
On **October 21, 2020**, the **Civil Service Bureau** (公務員事務局) announced that all civil servants joining the government on or after July 1, 2020, were required to sign a declaration to uphold the **Basic Law** (基本法) and swear allegiance to the **Hong Kong Special Administrative Region** (香港特別行政區). Shortly thereafter, this requirement was extended to all serving civil servants. Employees were asked to sign and return a form confirming their commitment. The government stated that failing to sign the declaration or refusing to take the oath would be regarded as a failure to meet the requirements for employment, potentially leading to dismissal. By early 2021, the vast majority of the civil service had complied, with a small number of employees resigning or facing termination for non-compliance.

## Significance
This event marked a definitive shift in the governance of Hong Kong. For the government, the requirement was an essential step in ensuring that those who implement public policy are loyal to the constitutional order and the state. Critics and various international observers argue that this policy effectively ended the long-standing tradition of political neutrality within the Hong Kong civil service, transforming it into an instrument for ensuring ideological alignment. The mandate set a precedent that was subsequently expanded to include district councilors, school teachers, and other public sector entities, fundamentally altering the relationship between the state and those employed to serve it.

## Perspectives

- **The Hong Kong Government and Beijing Authorities**: They maintain that the oath-taking requirement is a fundamental duty of public servants globally. They argue that safeguarding national security and upholding the Basic Law are prerequisite conditions for anyone entrusted with public administration.
- **Democratic Advocates and Pro-Democracy Civil Society**: They express concern that the oath requirement serves as a tool to purge dissent and curtail free speech within the bureaucracy. They believe this shift undermines the professional independence of the civil service and creates a climate of political surveillance.
