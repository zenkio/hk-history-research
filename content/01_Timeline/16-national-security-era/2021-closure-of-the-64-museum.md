## Background
The June 4th Museum (**六四紀念館**) was operated by the **Hong Kong Alliance in Support of Patriotic Democratic Movements of China** (**支聯會**). Since its inception, the museum served as a repository for historical materials, artifacts, and information related to the **1989 Tiananmen Square** protests in Beijing. For decades, the **Alliance** organized annual vigils in **Victoria Park** (**維多利亞公園**), which were the only large-scale public commemorations of the event on Chinese soil. Following the implementation of the **National Security Law** in **2020**, the legal environment regarding the exhibition of such materials changed significantly.

## What happened
On **May 30, 2021**, the museum reopened its doors to the public after a period of closure. However, shortly thereafter, officials from the **Food and Environmental Hygiene Department** (**食物環境衞生署**) visited the premises, alleging that the museum was operating without a valid place of public entertainment license. On **June 2, 2021**, the **Alliance** issued a formal statement announcing the temporary closure of the museum, citing the need to ensure the safety of its staff and visitors. The authorities subsequently conducted investigations into the facility’s compliance with local building and fire safety regulations. By the following months, the museum remained closed, and its physical contents were subjected to further scrutiny by law enforcement agencies.

## Significance
The closure of the museum is viewed by observers as a pivotal moment in the shifting landscape of civil society in Hong Kong. It represented the physical disappearance of spaces dedicated to the organized public memory of the **1989 Tiananmen crackdown**. For supporters of the museum, the closure signaled a broader crackdown on political dissent and the erosion of historical remembrance. Conversely, representatives of the government and certain pro-establishment commentators emphasized that the action was purely a regulatory matter involving administrative compliance and public safety, maintaining that all organizations must operate strictly within the legal framework established by local statutes and the **National Security Law**.

## Perspectives

- **Pro-democracy activists and organizers**: The closure is interpreted as a direct result of political pressure designed to silence memory of the Tiananmen crackdown. They argue that the licensing requirements were used as a pretext to dismantle institutional structures that preserved history.
- **Hong Kong government authorities**: Officials state that the museum's closure was the result of a standard inspection process regarding licensing and fire safety. They maintain that the enforcement actions were non-political and conducted in accordance with existing administrative law.
