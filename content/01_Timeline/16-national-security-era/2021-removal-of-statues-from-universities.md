## Background

Following the implementation of the **National Security Law** in June 2020, the political and social climate in Hong Kong underwent profound changes. Universities, which had traditionally served as centers for student activism and the maintenance of diverse historical narratives, faced increasing scrutiny regarding campus decorum and national security compliance. Throughout the latter half of 2021, university administrations began reassessing the presence of various public monuments on their grounds.

## What happened

On **December 23, 2021**, the **University of Hong Kong (香港大學)** removed the 'Pillar of Shame' (國殤之柱), a sculpture created by Danish artist **Jens Galschiøt** that had stood on the campus since 1998 to commemorate the 1989 Tiananmen Square protests. In the days surrounding this event, other institutions took similar actions. The **Chinese University of Hong Kong (香港中文大學)** removed the 'Goddess of Democracy' (民主女神像) statue, while **Lingnan University (嶺南大學)** dismantled a relief carving also related to the 1989 protests. These removals were typically conducted overnight or under restricted access, citing safety concerns or the absence of appropriate permits for the structures. The sculptures were subsequently placed into storage by the respective institutions.

## Significance

These removals are viewed by observers as a pivotal moment in the physical and symbolic restructuring of Hong Kong’s public space. Supporters of the move, including various university councils and government-aligned figures, argued that the sculptures had become unauthorized structures that potentially posed legal risks under the new security framework or interfered with the university's primary educational mission. Conversely, critics and student groups perceived the removals as an erasure of collective memory and a suppression of academic freedom and historical inquiry. The events highlighted the tension between the institutional authority of university administrations and the long-standing tradition of student-led memorialization. Whether these actions represent a necessary normalization of campus space or an irreversible loss of critical historical markers remains a subject of ongoing historical and social debate.

## Perspectives

- **University Administrations and Pro-Establishment Figures**: These entities emphasized that the removals were administrative decisions made to ensure campus safety and compliance with the law. They maintained that the statues were not sanctioned by the university and that clearing them helped refocus the institutions on academic standards and stability.
- **Student Activists and Civil Society Groups**: These groups viewed the removals as an attempt to erase historical consciousness and discourage dissent. They argued that the statues were symbolic pillars of the university's role as a space for free expression and historical reflection.
