## Background

The British National (Overseas) status, commonly known as **BNO**, was created in the late colonial era ahead of the 1997 transfer of sovereignty of Hong Kong to the People's Republic of China. For decades, the status granted holders travel documentation and consular assistance abroad without conferring the right of abode in the United Kingdom. Following the enactment of the **Law of the People's Republic of China on Safeguarding National Security in the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region** (中華人民共和國香港特別行政區維護國家安全法) in June **2020**, the British government argued that Beijing's actions constituted a direct breach of the 1984 Sino-British Joint Declaration.

## What happened

On **January 31, 2021**, the UK Home Office officially opened the **BNO visa** application pathway. The scheme allowed eligible BNO status holders and their close family members to apply for residence in the UK, with a pathway to apply for settled status after five years, and subsequently British citizenship. Unlike previous immigration routes, the scheme did not require a job offer or specific capital investment upon entry, though applicants were required to demonstrate financial self-sufficiency for a designated period and pay the UK's immigration health surcharge.

## Significance

The launch of the visa scheme precipitated a significant demographic shift in Hong Kong, triggering the largest wave of emigration since the period preceding the 1997 handover. Tens of thousands of residents applied within the first year, including professionals, educators, and families, leading to departures that affected various sectors of the local workforce. The policy also deepened diplomatic tensions between London and Beijing, with the Chinese central government and the Hong Kong government criticizing the UK's move as a violation of international law and bilateral understandings regarding nationality status.

## Perspectives

- **British Government**: London framed the visa scheme as a moral and historical obligation to the people of Hong Kong, responding to what it viewed as a severe erosion of rights and autonomies guaranteed under the Sino-British Joint Declaration.
- **Beijing and Hong Kong Governments**: Chinese and local authorities condemned the policy as a violation of China's sovereignty and international norms, subsequently announcing that the BNO passport would no longer be recognized as a valid travel document or proof of identity in Hong Kong.
