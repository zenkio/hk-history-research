## Background

In late **November 2022**, widespread public anger over mainland China's strict zero-COVID policies culminated in demonstrations across several major Chinese cities, often referred to as the 'White Paper protests' or 'A4 Protests'. These demonstrations were catalyzed by a deadly fire in Ürümqi, Xinjiang, which sparked nationwide grief and frustration with prolonged lockdowns and mass testing.

## What happened

On or around **28 November 2022**, echoes of the mainland demonstrations appeared in Hong Kong. University students, including individuals at the Chinese University of Hong Kong (香港中文大學) and the University of Hong Kong (香港大學), as well as residents in commercial districts like Central (中環), gathered to hold blank sheets of A4 paper. The blank paper served as a potent symbol of defiance against censorship, implying everything that could not be legally or safely said aloud. Participants also lit candles and displayed slogans expressing solidarity with victims of the Ürümqi fire and mainland protesters. Law enforcement officers maintained a heavy presence, stopping and searching individuals, checking identification, and recording participants.

## Significance

Operating under the strictures of the **National Security Law** enacted in June 2020, public demonstrations in Hong Kong had become exceedingly rare. The A4 paper demonstrations represented a significant moment of cross-border solidarity and a rare display of public dissent in post-2020 Hong Kong. While smaller in scale than the massive anti-extradition bill protests of **2019**, the event demonstrated that forms of political expression adapted to survive heavy legal and physical surveillance.

## Perspectives

- **Protesters and civil rights advocates**: Viewed the holding of blank paper as a fundamental exercise of free expression and a necessary act of human solidarity with compatriots facing severe restrictions on the mainland.
- **Hong Kong authorities and Beijing officials**: Regarded unauthorized gatherings and potential subversion with strict scrutiny under the National Security Law, viewing public dissent as a threat to public order and national security.
