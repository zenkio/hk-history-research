## Background

In March 2021, the Hong Kong government proposed the implementation of a real-name registration system for subscriber identity module (SIM) cards. The legislative framework aimed to combat telephone deception and enhance law enforcement's ability to investigate serious crimes involving telecommunications devices. Following public consultation and the passage of relevant subsidiary legislation, the policy was rolled out in phases starting in March 2022, culminating in a strict deadline of **February 23, 2023**, after which unregistered pre-paid SIM cards would be deactivated.

## What happened

Leading up to the **February 23, 2023** deadline, telecommunications operators established online portals, retail outlets, and service booths to assist users with registering their personal information—including legal name, identity document number, and a copy of an identification document—against their existing phone numbers. Users were permitted to register a limited number of pre-paid cards per operator. By the cutoff date, millions of cards had been processed, while unverified numbers were systematically deactivated by mobile network providers.

## Significance

The full implementation marked a significant structural shift in Hong Kong's telecommunications landscape, ending decades of anonymous mobile connectivity. While authorities emphasized the utility of the measure in preventing fraud and maintaining public order, the policy drew scrutiny from privacy advocates and international observers regarding data security, government surveillance, and the broader implications for civil liberties under the evolving legal framework.

## Perspectives

- **Hong Kong Government**: Authorities maintained that the real-name registration is a crucial anti-crime measure necessary to combat rising telephone scams, protect public safety, and align Hong Kong with international regulatory standards.
- **Privacy Advocates and Civil Society**: Critics and digital rights groups expressed concern over the centralization of personal data, arguing that the policy increases the risk of data breaches and exerts a chilling effect on anonymous whistleblowing and freedom of expression.
