During the **1940** and **1941** period preceding the Japanese invasion, British authorities in Hong Kong undertook significant defensive preparations. According to the research of **David Bellis**, these efforts included the construction of over **100** concrete pillboxes at strategic intersections and the excavation of hillside air raid shelters for the civilian population. Photographic documentation by **Harrison Forman**, currently held at the **University of Wisconsin**, provides visual evidence of these defense works and daily life prior to the conflict.

Following the Japanese invasion in **1941**, the territory suffered extensive damage from both Japanese bombardment and subsequent American airstrikes targeting shipping and logistics. During the occupation, Japanese forces utilized local labor and prisoners of war to construct the Kai Tak airfield and a storm drain system known as the *nolla*, which has since been transformed into the Kai Tak River. They also established a war memorial on the Peak, a structure of reinforced concrete that required until **1947** to demolish. Post-war housing reports from **1946** indicated the destruction or severe damage of **160,000** tenement-type and **7,000** European-type housing units, a situation exacerbated by looting during the American blockade.

Historical records from the **Gwulo** website document various wartime milestones. Key events include the August **1942** departure of the repatriation ship *MS Gripsholm* to New York and the September **1942** release of Chinese, Malay, and Annamite members of the **HKVDC** from the **Sham Shui Po** POW camp. By August **1945**, the Allied fleet arrived in Victoria Harbour, leading to the formal surrender of Japanese officials at Government House on September **16**, **1945**, an event marked by celebratory fireworks in the harbour.

## Historical context

> [!note] General background (AI, not from the source)

The Japanese occupation of Hong Kong lasted from December 1941 until August 1945, a period often referred to locally as the 'three years and eight months'. This era saw the total collapse of British colonial administration and profound hardships for the local population due to food shortages, forced labor, and air raids. The city's post-war recovery was defined by the massive task of reconstructing housing and infrastructure destroyed during the Pacific War.

David Bellis gave a talk to the Royal Geographical Society (RGS) on changes in Hong Kong brought by war, illustrating the changes with photographs taken by American photographer and journalist **Harrison Forman** in **1940** and **1941**. Forman's photos, now preserved at the **University of Wisconsin**, show street scenes, children, and notably British preparations for war.

Preparations included building over **100** concrete pillboxes at intersections and tunneling hillside air raid shelters for civilians. The speaker notes that surviving pillboxes can still be found, such as one at Repulse Bay Beach, though slowly sinking into the sand, and another clearer one at Turtle Cove. Hillside tunnel entrances also remain, including ventilation shafts hidden in bamboo stands.

During the Japanese occupation from **1941**, the Japanese built the Kai Tak airfield using local labor and POWs, and dug a storm drain known as the *nolla*, now the beautified Kai Tak River. They also chose the Peak for a war memorial, built on a granite platform with stone buttresses. Due to the reinforced concrete, demolition took until **1947**, and the speaker notes a local legend that a Japanese sword was buried in the foundations.

While the war lasted less than three weeks from invasion to surrender, damage occurred due to both Japanese bombardment and American airstrikes targeting Japanese shipping and material transport. A housing report in **1946** stated that **160,000** tenement-type and **7,000** European-type housing units were destroyed or severely damaged, worsened by looters stripping wood for firewood during the American blockade.

Finally, Bellis mentions his website, **Gwulo**, which features a map of Hong Kong pillboxes (red for demolished, yellow for surviving ruins) and promotes his books, *Old Hong Kong Photos and the Tales They Tell*, for further exploration.
