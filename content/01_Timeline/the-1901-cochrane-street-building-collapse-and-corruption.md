In **1901**, the **Cochrane Street** tragedy in **Hong Kong** resulted in the collapse of two tenement buildings, leading to the deaths of 43 people. According to **Professor Cecilia Chu** of the **Chinese University of Hong Kong**, these structures were originally built as three-story tenements during an **1870s** land boom. A **Chinese** investor named **Chan** subsequently purchased the properties and sought to add additional stories, a common but dangerous practice at the time. To facilitate these modifications, **Chan** hired former employees of the **Public Works Department** as architects, leveraging their insider knowledge to bypass building codes and regulations.

**Chu** argues that property ownership in this period was defined by a complex alliance between **European** and **Chinese** landlords. Data analyzed by **Chu** from property listings following a **plague** outbreak suggests that the largest landlords were often entities involved in the building trade or government land departments. She contends this indicates significant collusion, where elites prioritized financial interests over social welfare and building safety. A government commission launched after the **Cochrane Street** disaster confirmed that systemic bribery and the circumvention of building regulations were widespread. Although the government attempted to reform the **Public Works Department** and recruit better-trained professionals, **Chu** asserts that these institutional efforts largely failed. Deeply entrenched interests in property development persisted, resulting in even more frequent irregular building practices following the introduction of public health regulations in **1903**.

## Historical context

> [!note] General background (AI, not from the source)

During the late 19th and early 20th centuries, rapid urbanization and population growth in colonial Hong Kong placed immense pressure on existing housing stock. The resulting 'tenement' housing often suffered from poor ventilation, overcrowding, and inadequate structural standards, prompting periodic but often ineffective government legislative interventions.
