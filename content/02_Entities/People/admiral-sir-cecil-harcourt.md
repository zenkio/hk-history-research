## Role in the Liberation of Hong Kong
On **August 30, 1945**, Rear Admiral **Sir Cecil Harcourt** (夏慤) arrived in Hong Kong to re-establish British authority following the Japanese occupation. Commanding a task force of the British Pacific Fleet, he accepted the formal surrender of Japanese forces aboard the HMS *Indomitable* on **September 16, 1945**. For the following seven months, he served as the Commander-in-Chief and head of the British Military Administration, tasked with restoring order, repatriating prisoners of war, and restarting essential public services.

## Governance and the Young Plan
**Harcourt** oversaw the transition from military to civil government. His administration faced the difficult task of reviving a war-torn economy and addressing the infrastructure destroyed during the Pacific War. During his tenure, the colonial government began discussing the **Young Plan**, a proposal initiated by Governor **Mark Young** (楊慕琦) to introduce limited democratic representation to the Legislative Council. 

## Historical Perspectives
Interpretations of **Harcourt**'s administration vary depending on the historical lens. British colonial narratives often emphasize his role as a restorer of stability and the rule of law after a period of chaos. Conversely, some later scholars and local activists have critiqued the military administration for its exclusionary nature and the missed opportunity to implement more inclusive democratic reforms during the immediate post-war period. The **Young Plan** itself remains a subject of academic debate; while some view it as a genuine attempt at liberalization, others characterize it as a late-stage colonial strategy intended to maintain British influence rather than grant significant self-determination. **Harcourt** departed Hong Kong in **May 1946** when **Mark Young** returned to resume his duties as Governor, marking the official end of the military administration period.
