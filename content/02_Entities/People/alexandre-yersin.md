## Role in the 1894 Plague
In **1894**, Hong Kong was struck by a severe bubonic plague outbreak centered in the crowded district of **Tai Ping Shan** (太平山). Colonial authorities invited **Alexandre Yersin**, a former student of Louis Pasteur, to investigate the pathogen. Arriving in June, **Yersin** worked under challenging conditions in a makeshift laboratory near the **Kennedy Town Hospital** (堅尼地城傳染病院).

## Scientific Discovery and Controversy
**Yersin** successfully isolated the bacillus responsible for the plague, later named *Yersinia pestis* in his honor. His discovery occurred almost simultaneously with that of **Kitasato Shibasaburo**, a bacteriologist sent by the Japanese government. While **Yersin** is widely credited by later scholarship for identifying the correct pathogen, early historical accounts often reflected rivalry between the two scientists. Some colonial-era narratives emphasized the superiority of Western scientific methods, while later historiography has examined the tension between international researchers and the **British colonial government**, which was preoccupied with sanitation measures and public order.

## Impact on Hong Kong
**Yersin's** discovery provided the scientific foundation for modern plague control, though the implementation of these measures—such as house-to-house inspections and the forced cleansing of **Tai Ping Shan**—faced significant resistance from the local Chinese community. Local residents and leaders often viewed these interventions as intrusive and insensitive to cultural practices, leading to widespread distrust. While **Yersin** himself soon departed for Indochina, his identification of the bacillus transformed global understanding of the disease, though debates remain regarding the extent to which his findings influenced the colonial government's immediate shift from repressive quarantine measures to more systemic sanitation reforms.
