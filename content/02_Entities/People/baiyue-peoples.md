## Overview and Archaeological Context
The **Baiyue peoples** (百越) refer to a heterogeneous collection of non-Sinitic tribal groups that inhabited the coastal and river valley regions of southern China and northern Vietnam from the late Neolithic period through the Han dynasty. In the context of Hong Kong history, the Baiyue are significant for establishing the earliest regional cultures, characterized by distinct pottery traditions, stone tool industries, and subsistence patterns adapted to the coastal environment.

## Historiographical Perspectives
Interpretations of the Baiyue and their relationship to Hong Kong's heritage vary significantly across different historical and political frameworks:

* **British Colonial Scholarship:** Early 20th-century British colonial researchers often viewed the early inhabitants of Hong Kong through an isolated, primitive lens, categorizing archaeological findings as distinct from the dynastic history of China proper.
* **Qing and Mainland Chinese Historiography:** Traditional imperial texts often generalized southern populations under the broad umbrella of 'Yue' (越) or 'Nam Yue' (南越), framing their eventual incorporation during the **Qin dynasty** (秦朝) expansion as a civilizing process and the southern extension of imperial territory.
* **Local New Territories and Indigenous Perspectives:** Modern local communities and indigenous scholars emphasize the deep-rooted continuity of coastal livelihoods, viewing the ancient material culture not merely as an extinguished tribe, but as part of an enduring maritime heritage.
* **Modern Archaeological Consensus:** Contemporary scholarship highlights complex regional networks, noting that the **Integration into the Baiyue Cultural Sphere** involved advanced maritime mobility, sophisticated metallurgy, and the **Widespread Adoption of Stilt-House Architecture** suited to the estuarine environment.

## Material Culture in Hong Kong
Archaeological sites across Hong Kong—such as those associated with the **Flourishing Quartz Ring and Ornament Workshops** and the **Rise of Hierarchical Tribal Societies**—demonstrate that the Baiyue peoples engaged in specialized craft production, long-distance exchange, and increasingly stratified social structures long before formal imperial administration was established in the region.
