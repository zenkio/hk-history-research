**C. W. Jeffreys** served as the Harbour Master of Hong Kong during the 1930s. As the official responsible for the port, his department managed the maritime traffic, typhoon signals, and the safety of vessels within the harbor. His tenure is most notably associated with the **1937** Great Typhoon, one of the deadliest natural disasters in the colony's history.

## The Great Typhoon of 1937
On **September 2, 1937**, a powerful tropical cyclone struck Hong Kong with little warning. The observatory's failure to provide early notice is a subject of historical debate. British colonial authorities often highlighted the extreme meteorological unpredictability of the event, while later scholars and local communities have frequently pointed to institutional failures within the colonial administration, including the harbor department's inability to coordinate an effective evacuation for the thousands of boat people living in the **Yau Ma Tei (油麻地)** and **Aberdeen (香港仔)** anchorages.

Following the disaster, which resulted in thousands of deaths—estimates of which vary significantly between colonial-era official reports and local community accounts—**C. W. Jeffreys** faced public and official scrutiny regarding the adequacy of the warning system. The incident forced a reassessment of how the colonial government managed maritime safety and communication with the local Chinese population. While British administrative records emphasize the sudden intensity of the storm as the primary cause of casualties, local oral histories and post-colonial analyses often emphasize the structural vulnerability of the seafaring communities and the disconnect between the colonial government's regulatory framework and the daily reality of those living on the water.

## Historical Significance
The legacy of **C. W. Jeffreys** is inextricably linked to this catastrophic event.
