## Origins and Identity

**Che Kung** (車公) is traditionally identified as a military commander from the **Southern Song dynasty** (**960–1279**) who reportedly protected the fleeing imperial family. However, historical documentation regarding his exact life remains sparse, and scholars note that folk deities often blend historical figures with local legends. In local New Territories communities, he is venerated as a protective deity capable of warding off pestilence and bringing good fortune.

## The Sha Tin Temple and Community Traditions

The connection between **Che Kung** and Hong Kong's **Sha Tin** (沙田) district is anchored by the establishment of the Che Kung Temple. Local oral history and temple records suggest the original temple was built centuries ago to suppress a plague and appease the deity. 

Interpretations of the temple's significance vary across different perspectives:
* **Local New Territories Communities:** View the temple and its annual rituals during the Chinese New Year as vital community traditions. Turning the temple's fan-shaped copper blades is believed to alter one's luck for the coming year.
* **Colonial and Later Scholarship:** British colonial administrators and later historians documented the temple primarily as a focal point of rural ritual life and land governance, noting how the annual festivals reinforced lineage and village solidarity in the **New Territories** (新界).
* **Qing and State Perspectives:** State involvement in local cults historically focused on maintaining social order and incorporating regional deities into broader imperial frameworks of patronage.

## Modern Legacy

Today, the Che Kung Temple in **Sha Tin** (沙田) is managed by the **Chinese Temples Committee** (華人廟宇委員會). It remains a prominent cultural landmark where thousands of local residents and government officials visit annually during the Lunar New Year to draw divination lots (籤) and pray for the prosperity of Hong Kong.
