## Historical Background
**Cheng I** (鄭一) was a pivotal figure in the maritime history of the South China Sea. Active during the late **Qing dynasty**, he is primarily recognized for consolidating disparate pirate groups into a structured coalition, known as the Red Flag Fleet. His influence centered on the coastal regions of Guangdong, including the strategic waters and islands that now constitute the Hong Kong territory, such as Lantau Island and the Pearl River Delta.

## Interpretations of His Role
Historical perspectives on **Cheng I** vary significantly. British colonial historiography often framed him and his successors as lawless insurgents disrupting trade, emphasizing the necessity of intervention to restore maritime order. Conversely, some mainland Chinese scholarship characterizes his activities as a localized response to socioeconomic pressures and ineffective Qing governance, viewing the piracy as a form of maritime resistance. Within local New Territories oral histories, he is sometimes remembered through the lens of community survival, where pirate syndicates operated as complex social entities that both extorted and provided a rudimentary protection system for coastal villages.

## Legacy and Impact
Following his death in **1807**, leadership of his confederacy passed to his wife, **Cheng I Sao** (鄭一嫂), who is often credited with formalizing the legal code of the organization. The activities of the confederacy necessitated frequent military responses from the Qing imperial navy, occasionally supported by colonial Portuguese and British interests. This period of instability played a documented role in shaping the geopolitical conditions of the coastal regions that were later ceded to or leased by Great Britain. While the exact details of his early life remain historically obscure, his impact on the centralization of regional maritime power is widely acknowledged by contemporary scholars as a foundational chapter in Hong Kong's pre-colonial maritime history.
