## Role in Colonial Hong Kong
The **Church Missionary Society (CMS)** (英國聖公會差會) was a mission agency of the Church of England that arrived in Hong Kong shortly after the territory became a British colony. Its presence was pivotal in the institutional development of the colonial administration's social infrastructure, often operating in collaboration with the colonial government to provide services that the state initially lacked. The society focused heavily on evangelism, but their legacy is most enduring through the schools and social welfare homes they established.

## Victoria Home and Orphanage
A primary example of the **CMS** influence was the establishment of the **Victoria Home and Orphanage** (維多利亞寄宿女校及孤兒院). Founded in the late 19th century, the institution aimed to provide vocational training and Christian education to Chinese girls. Historical perspectives on this institution vary significantly: colonial records often frame it as a benevolent effort to protect vulnerable youth and elevate social standards; conversely, some modern scholars and local historians critique such institutions as instruments of cultural assimilation, questioning the extent to which they suppressed indigenous practices and family structures in favor of Anglican values.

## Historiographical Perspectives
The impact of the **CMS** remains a subject of multifaceted interpretation. Traditional colonial historiography frequently highlights the society’s role in modernizing Hong Kong and introducing Western educational systems. In contrast, scholars examining the interaction between mission work and local communities, particularly in the **New Territories**, often point to tensions between foreign religious mandates and established local traditions. While the **CMS** succeeded in integrating its educational and medical institutions into the fabric of Hong Kong society, the degree of genuine cross-cultural synthesis versus the imposition of colonial norms remains a point of academic debate. The society’s archives are often used to reconstruct the daily lives of mission workers, though gaps exist regarding the internal experiences of the Chinese converts and students who lived within these institutional frameworks.
