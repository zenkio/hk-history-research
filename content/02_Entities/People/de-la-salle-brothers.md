## Introduction to the Order

The **De La Salle Brothers** (喇沙修士會), formally known as the Institute of the Brothers of the Christian Schools, is an international religious congregation of men dedicated to the Christian education of youth, particularly the poor. Founded in France by **St. John Baptist de La Salle** in **1680**, the order expanded globally during the 19th and 20th centuries, establishing a prominent presence in the British colony of Hong Kong.

## Connection to St. Joseph's College

The arrival of the Brothers in Hong Kong is directly tied to the history of **St. Joseph's College** (聖若瑟書院). Originally established as St. Saviour's College by the **Catholic Diocese of Hong Kong** in **1864**, the institution faced severe administrative and financial challenges. To secure the school's viability, the leadership invited the De La Salle Brothers to take over its operation.

In **1875**, six Brothers arrived in Hong Kong under the leadership of **Brother Aloysius** to assume control of the school, which was subsequently renamed St. Joseph's College. Under their stewardship, the school grew rapidly, relocating to a prominent site on Caine Road before moving to Robinson Road and eventually to Kennedy Road.

## Educational Impact and Historiography

Historians generally credit the De La Salle Brothers with raising educational standards in colonial Hong Kong through structured curricula, discipline, and a strong emphasis on English-medium instruction. Their institutions served as key social mobility vehicles for generations of local students, bridging communities within the colonial social hierarchy.

While colonial-era narratives often emphasized the philanthropic and civilizing missions of such religious orders, later scholarship frequently places the Brothers' work within the broader framework of British imperial education policies, examining how missionary schools navigated the delicate balance between religious objectives, colonial governance, and the aspirations of the local Chinese elite.
