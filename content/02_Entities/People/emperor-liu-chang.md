## Role in the Southern Han Dynasty
**Liu Chang** (r. **958–971**) was the fourth and last emperor of the Southern Han, a dynasty that controlled much of modern-day Guangdong and Guangxi. Historical records indicate that during his reign, the strategic importance of the coastal regions near present-day Hong Kong became increasingly apparent, necessitating firmer military and administrative control.

## Institutionalization of the Pearl Garrison
Under the reign of **Liu Chang**, the Tuen Mun (屯門) area was officially designated as a site for the 'Pearl Garrison' (屯門寨). This development is often cited by historians as evidence of a shift from informal coastal activity to structured government oversight. The garrison was primarily established to secure the lucrative pearl-diving industry and to facilitate maritime trade, which was vital to the Southern Han economy.

## Historical Interpretations
Interpretations of **Liu Chang**’s impact on the region vary significantly among scholars and local perspectives:

*   **State-Centered Narratives:** Traditional Qing (清) dynasty and Beijing-centric accounts often emphasize the garrison as an early assertion of central Chinese sovereignty over the southern maritime frontiers, framing the Southern Han as a legitimate predecessor to later imperial administrative systems.
*   **Colonial and Local Perspectives:** British colonial-era historians sometimes portrayed these early outposts as sporadic military attempts to curb piracy rather than stable administrative centers. Conversely, many local Tuen Mun and New Territories communities view the era of the Southern Han as a foundational period, often linking local lineage histories and cultural markers to the influx of people and trade necessitated by the garrison's establishment.

Because contemporary primary sources are limited, the exact extent of the garrison's control over the surrounding islands remains a subject of academic debate. While the existence of the garrison is widely accepted, the degree to which it influenced local settlement patterns remains uncertain and continues to be studied by archeologists investigating Tang and Five Dynasties sites in the region.
