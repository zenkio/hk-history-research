## Expansion into the South

During the reign of **Emperor Wu of Han** (**漢武帝**), the Han dynasty launched major military campaigns to expand its territory southward. In **111 BCE**, Han forces conquered the southern kingdom of **Nanyue** (**南越**), which included parts of modern-day Guangdong, Guangxi, and northern Vietnam. This conquest integrated the Pearl River Delta region, including the area of present-day Hong Kong, into the administrative sphere of the Chinese empire.

## Economic and Administrative Impact

The incorporation of the south under Emperor Wu led to the establishment of new commanderies and counties. Historians note that this period marked the beginning of formal imperial governance and economic development in the region, notably including the emergence of the coastal salt industry (**鹽業**), which relied on the region's coastal geography for salt production. Archaeological finds in Hong Kong, such as Han-dynasty tombs and coins, provide physical evidence of increasing Chinese influence and trade during this era.

## Historiographical Interpretations

Interpretations of Emperor Wu's southern expansion vary across different traditions. Traditional Chinese historiography, largely shaped by Qing-era and later Beijing perspectives, portrays the campaigns as a civilizing unification that brought the benefits of imperial administration and culture to the southern borderlands. In contrast, modern scholarship and local Hong Kong perspectives often examine the conquest through the lens of colonization, emphasizing the subjugation of indigenous Baiyue (**百越**) populations and the transformation of local societies to serve imperial economic and military interests. British colonial-era historians frequently viewed these early dynasties through an antiquarian lens, cataloging Han artifacts to establish the antiquity of Chinese settlement in the territory without deeply engaging in the political narratives of the imperial center.
