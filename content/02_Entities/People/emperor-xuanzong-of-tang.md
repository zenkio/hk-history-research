## Administrative Integration and Tuen Mun
During the reign of **Emperor Xuanzong** (r. **712–756**), the Tang Dynasty expanded its maritime oversight to secure trade routes along the South China Sea. Historians generally agree that the establishment of a military garrison at **Tuen Mun** (屯門) served as a strategic outpost for coastal defense and the regulation of foreign maritime commerce. This administrative action is cited as one of the earliest instances of formal central government presence in the territory now known as Hong Kong.

## Historical Interpretations
The significance of the **Tuen Mun** garrison remains a subject of varying historical perspectives. Early colonial-era scholarship often treated the site as a marginal outpost, emphasizing the area's isolation from the northern heartlands. Conversely, contemporary mainland Chinese scholarship and local Hong Kong historians often emphasize the site as evidence of long-standing, continuous administrative inclusion within the Chinese imperial system, pointing to the deployment of troops as proof of state governance.

## Archaeological Evidence
While textual evidence from the *Old Book of Tang* confirms the existence of military structures in the region under the **Tang Dynasty**, the exact physical layout and duration of the **Tuen Mun** base remain uncertain due to the lack of exhaustive archaeological data. Some local community researchers suggest that maritime trade during the **Kaiyuan era** (開元) brought significant cultural and economic exchange to the Pearl River Delta, though the specific impact on the daily lives of local coastal inhabitants during the **8th century** is not fully documented in the surviving records. The legacy of this period is primarily acknowledged through the historical legitimization of Hong Kong as an integral part of regional maritime history.
