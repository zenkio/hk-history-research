## Role in the Colonial Administration
**Franklin Gimson** (1890–1969) arrived in Hong Kong to serve as the Colonial Secretary in **1941**, just days before the Japanese invasion. Following the fall of the colony on **December 25, 1941**, he was interned alongside other British officials and civilians at the **Stanley Internment Camp** (赤柱拘留營). During his captivity, he was noted for maintaining morale among the internees and overseeing the organizational structure of the camp’s internal governance.

## The Provisional Administration
Upon the Japanese surrender in **August 1945**, **Gimson** took a proactive stance. Asserting his authority as the highest-ranking British official, he established a **Provisional British Administration** (臨時英國行政機構) at the Flagstaff House before the arrival of the British Pacific Fleet. 

## Differing Historical Interpretations
Historians offer varying perspectives on **Gimson**'s role during the transition of power in **August 1945**. From a traditional British colonial perspective, his actions were seen as a vital effort to preserve sovereignty and public order during the power vacuum. However, some local Hong Kong scholars and commentators have noted that his provisional administration reflected a continuation of pre-war colonial structures that did not account for the experiences of the local Chinese population during the three years and eight months of occupation. Conversely, interpretations influenced by mainland historiography have occasionally framed the period as a complex intersection of anti-colonial sentiment and the reassertion of imperial influence. The extent to which his administration addressed the social and economic devastation faced by the local community remains a subject of ongoing academic discussion, as the British military administration quickly superseded his authority shortly thereafter.
