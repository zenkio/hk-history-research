## Administrative Tenure
**Sir George Bonham** (文咸) served as the third Governor of Hong Kong from **1848** to **1854**. Arriving after the turbulent governorship of Sir John Davis, Bonham inherited a colony suffering from economic stagnation and high rates of crime. He is widely credited by historians for his focus on retrenchment and fiscal responsibility, which helped stabilize the colonial administration during a period of uncertainty regarding the colony's long-term viability.

## The Registration Ordinance
The most controversial aspect of his tenure was the **Registration Ordinance** of **1844** (later modified and enforced during his term), which required all residents of Chinese descent to register with the authorities. This measure was designed to help the colonial government control the criminal elements then prevalent in the colony. 

Interpretations of this policy vary significantly. British colonial perspectives often frame the ordinance as a necessary step for public safety and administrative control in a nascent settlement. Conversely, many in the local Chinese community perceived the ordinance as a discriminatory tool that subjected them to collective suspicion and harassment. Later scholarship often highlights how the ordinance served to institutionalize the social stratification between the British colonial authorities and the indigenous population, setting a precedent for subsequent interactions between the government and the Chinese residents of Hong Kong.

## Historical Legacy
While Bonham was instrumental in managing the day-to-day governance of the colony and improving its basic infrastructure, his reliance on ordinances like the registration scheme left a complex legacy. He resigned his position in **1854** and returned to Britain. His term is generally viewed by historians as a pragmatic, if sometimes divisive, period that saw Hong Kong transition from an unstable military outpost into a more formal colonial administrative entity.
