## Background and Role in China

**George Elliot** served as a British naval officer and, alongside his cousin **Charles Elliot** (楚麥利ǎ), was appointed as joint Plenipotentiary and Superintendent of Trade in China by the British government in **1840**. Tasked with enforcing British demands on the Qing dynasty (清朝) during the opening phases of the First Opium War (第一次鴻片戰爭), **Elliot** held nominal military and diplomatic authority during the initial naval expeditions along the Chinese coast.

## Connection to Hong Kong Fever

While **Charles Elliot** is historically remembered for negotiating the Convention of Chuenpi and taking formal possession of Hong Kong Island (香港島) in **1841**, **George Elliot** shared in the broader command decisions that brought British forces to the island. Shortly after occupation, British troops and naval personnel were struck by the **First Major Outbreak of Hong Kong Fever** (香港熱), a severe epidemic later identified primarily as malaria.

Historiographical interpretations of this early period differ. British colonial accounts often framed the high mortality rates among soldiers and sailors as an unavoidable tragedy resulting from an unforgiving tropical environment and the primitive state of local sanitation. Conversely, later medical and social historians emphasize that the sickness was exacerbated by poor administrative planning, the choice of damp encampments near paddy fields, and the general lack of understanding regarding vector-borne diseases at the time. Qing and mainland Chinese scholarship typically view these events through the lens of imperial overreach, noting that the health catastrophe befell an occupying force engaged in an unjust war of aggression.

**George Elliot** resigned his commission and returned to Britain in late **1840** due to failing health, missing the worst phases of the epidemic on Hong Kong Island itself, though his tenure remains linked to the foundational administrative and military missteps of the colony's earliest days.
