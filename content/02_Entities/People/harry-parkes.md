## Role in the Second Opium War
**Harry Parkes** (1828–1885) served as the British consul in Canton during the mid-19th century. He is best known in the context of Hong Kong for his role in the **Arrow Incident** of **1856**. Parkes demanded an apology and the release of crew members from a vessel registered in Hong Kong, an act that escalated into the Second Opium War. From a British colonial perspective, Parkes was seen as a resolute protector of treaty rights; conversely, Qing officials and many modern scholars view his actions as provocative, deliberately staged to provide a pretext for military intervention and the further expansion of British influence.

## Territorial Expansion and the Kowloon Peninsula
Following the Second Opium War, **Parkes** played a critical role in the negotiations that led to the **Convention of Peking** in **1860**. As a primary negotiator, he secured the perpetual lease of the **Kowloon Peninsula** (九龍半島) for the British Crown. 

Interpretations of this acquisition vary significantly. British colonial records of the era characterized the lease as a strategic necessity to secure the safety of the Hong Kong harbor. However, indigenous communities in the **New Territories** (新界) and later historical scholarship often emphasize that this territorial expansion was achieved through coercion. The local population frequently experienced the encroachment as an alien imposition, leading to periods of tension and resistance that lasted well beyond the initial handover. While colonial officials viewed the annexation as a triumph of diplomacy, regional historiography often highlights the disruptive impact this had on local governance and land tenure systems. **Parkes** remains a polarizing figure, representing to some the efficiency of the British Foreign Office and to others the hallmarks of gunboat diplomacy.
