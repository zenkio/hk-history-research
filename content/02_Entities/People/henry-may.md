## Early Colonial Service
**Henry May** (梅含理) arrived in Hong Kong in **1881** as a cadet officer. Over the following decades, he rose through the colonial administration, notably serving as the Captain Superintendent of Police, a role in which he was responsible for the enforcement of colonial law and order during the late 19th century. His administrative experience was extensive, involving him in the management of the New Territories after the **1898** lease.

## Governorship (1912–1919)
Appointed as Governor in **1912**, **May** governed during a volatile period. His tenure saw the outbreak of the First World War, which tested the stability of the British administration in the Far East. He is remembered for his role in the development of public infrastructure and institutions, including his support for the **Opening of the Hong Kong Government Technical Institute** in **1907** (while acting as Colonial Secretary), which laid foundational work for technical education in the territory.

## Differing Historical Interpretations
Historians offer varying assessments of his legacy. In traditional British colonial narratives, **May** is often portrayed as a pragmatic and steadfast administrator who navigated the tensions between the Chinese republican movement and British colonial interests. Conversely, some local Hong Kong and contemporary scholars examine his governance through the lens of strict colonial control, particularly regarding his efforts to contain influence from the **1911** Chinese Revolution within the colony. Within the New Territories, his earlier involvement in land registration and administrative demarcation is viewed by some communities as an assertion of central colonial authority that often disregarded existing indigenous land rights. These interpretations remain debated, reflecting the conflicting priorities of colonial preservation versus the evolving identity of the local populace during the early 20th century.
