## Administrative Consolidation

The **Hongwu Emperor** (born **Zhu Yuanzhang**, 朱元璋) reigned from **1368** to **1398**. His establishment of the Ming dynasty marked a period of intensive re-organisation for the coastal regions of Guangdong, including the area now known as Hong Kong. Under his rule, the Ming government sought to consolidate control over maritime borders and local populations, a move that shifted the region from a loosely administered frontier to a more structured part of the empire.

## Historical Perspectives

Interpretations of the **Hongwu Emperor**'s impact on the region vary significantly among historians. Traditional accounts from the **Qing** (清) period often emphasise the imperial government's role in establishing the *li-jia* (里甲) system, a form of collective responsibility and tax registration that integrated local settlements into the central state. 

In contrast, some local scholars from the **New Territories** (新界) suggest that while administrative records indicate Ming authority, the daily governance of many coastal villages remained largely autonomous. These local narratives often highlight the resilience of ancestral clan structures that existed independently of, or even in tension with, Ming state mandates. 

Modern scholarship remains divided on the extent to which Ming policies actually penetrated the daily lives of coastal inhabitants. While official records from **1368** onwards demonstrate a clear intent to impose imperial order and suppress piracy, the physical evidence of Ming-era administration in the Hong Kong region is limited. Some historians argue that the region served primarily as a buffer zone rather than a core administrative territory, though the formalisation of land registration during this era is widely accepted as a transformative step in local governance.
