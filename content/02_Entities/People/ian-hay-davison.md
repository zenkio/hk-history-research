## Background and Appointment
**Ian Hay Davison** was a senior partner at the accounting firm Arthur Andersen who gained significant international reputation for his work in the London financial sector. Following the collapse of the Hong Kong stock market during the **1987 Black Monday** crash—which forced the four-day closure of the Hong Kong Stock Exchange—the colonial government sought an external expert to evaluate the territory's regulatory failures.

## The Securities Review Committee
In late **1987**, **Davison** was appointed to lead the Securities Review Committee. The committee's mandate was to investigate the systemic weaknesses that had led to the suspension of trading. The resulting report, published in **1988**, is considered a foundational document in Hong Kong's financial history. **Davison** proposed a radical overhaul of the regulatory environment, most notably recommending the establishment of an independent statutory body to replace the ineffective regulatory structures of the time. This eventually led to the creation of the Securities and Futures Commission (SFC) in **1989**.

## Interpretations and Legacy
Historians and financial analysts offer differing perspectives on the significance of the **Davison Report**. From the perspective of the **British colonial government** and institutional investors, the report was a necessary modernization effort that professionalized the market and restored international confidence, aligning Hong Kong with global standards of financial conduct. Conversely, some local market participants of the era perceived the reforms as an aggressive imposition of external bureaucratic oversight, which curtailed the previously informal and rapid trading practices that had characterized the local exchange. Later scholarship often emphasizes that while the reforms were essential for Hong Kong's evolution into a premier global financial hub, they also marked the beginning of a shift away from the "laissez-faire" administrative style favored by earlier colonial administrations toward a more interventionist, compliance-heavy regulatory regime.
