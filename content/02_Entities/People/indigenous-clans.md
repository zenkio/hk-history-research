## Social Structure and Land
The indigenous clans (原居民宗族) of the **New Territories (新界)** are organized around patrilineal descent and ancestral worship. The most influential are the **Five Great Clans (五大宗族)**: the **Tang (鄧)**, **Hau (侯)**, **Pang (彭)**, **Liu (廖)**, and **Man (文)**. These groups established fortified lineage villages, known as **walled villages (圍村)**, to protect their agricultural interests and maintain social order through a strict hierarchy based on seniority and genealogy.

## The Colonial Transition
Following the **Convention of Peking (北京條約)** in **1898**, which leased the New Territories to Great Britain, the clans' relationship with the state shifted. In **1899**, several **Punti (本地人)** and **Hakka (客家)** clans engaged in armed resistance against British forces. This conflict arose from disputes over land boundaries and the perceived illegitimacy of the British administration's claim to ancestral lands.

## Divergent Interpretations
Historians and contemporary actors interpret the status of the indigenous clans through different lenses:

* **British colonial** administration viewed the clans as local elites to be co-opted. By recognizing traditional land rights, the British sought to minimize unrest and ensure a stable transition of power.
* **Local community** perspectives emphasize an inherent, ancestral right to the land that exists independently of colonial legislation or imperial grants.
* **Qing/Beijing** administrative records historically viewed these clans as subjects under the jurisdiction of the local counties, managing their affairs through imperial law.
* **Modern scholarship** often analyzes the clan system as a socio-economic strategy for resource management and survival in a frontier environment, rather than purely a cultural or familial arrangement.
