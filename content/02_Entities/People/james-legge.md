## Life and Career in Hong Kong
**James Legge** (1815–1897) was a prominent Scottish Congregationalist missionary who moved to **Hong Kong** in **1843**. As the head of the London Missionary Society’s **Anglo-Chinese College**, originally founded by **Robert Morrison** (馬禮遜) in Malacca and relocated to Hong Kong, Legge played a pivotal role in the early development of education in the colony. His work is most notably associated with his massive project to translate the Chinese Classics into English, a bilingual endeavor that provided a bridge between Chinese philosophical thought and the Western world.

## Interpretations of His Work
Legge’s legacy is viewed through multiple lenses. British colonial historians often portray him as a foundational figure in the intellectual infrastructure of 19th-century Hong Kong, emphasizing his contribution to the professionalization of Sinology. Conversely, later scholars and historians of the post-colonial era have analyzed his work as part of the broader missionary efforts during the mid-Qing dynasty. While some credit him with a sincere, respectful engagement with Confucianism, others point out that his scholarly output occurred alongside the expansion of British imperial influence in East Asia. Local historians in Hong Kong often highlight his relationship with Chinese scholars, particularly his collaboration with **Wang Tao** (王韜), who assisted him in the translation work. This partnership is frequently cited as an early example of East-West intellectual synergy, though the extent of Wang’s influence on the final English translations remains a subject of continued academic debate.

## Scholarly Impact
Legge’s dedication to translating the *Four Books* (四書) and *Five Classics* (五經) brought the nuances of Confucian philosophy to the English-speaking world. His influence persists in both theological and academic spheres; while he sought to convert the Chinese population to Christianity, he simultaneously gained a reputation for his deep, empathetic reading of Chinese texts. Whether viewed as an agent of cultural imperialism or a pioneer of intercultural dialogue, his impact on the academic reputation of Hong Kong as a center for Chinese studies is widely acknowledged.
