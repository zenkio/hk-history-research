## Role in the Hong Kong Transition
**John Major** (馬卓安) served as the Prime Minister of the United Kingdom during a critical period of the Sino-British negotiations regarding the future of Hong Kong. His administration followed that of Margaret Thatcher and coincided with the final phase of the colonial era. **Major** is perhaps best remembered in Hong Kong for his role in appointing **Chris Patten** (彭定康) as the last Governor of Hong Kong in **1992**.

## Differing Interpretations
Historical assessments of **Major**’s policy toward Hong Kong vary significantly. From the perspective of the British government at the time, the appointment of **Patten** and the subsequent introduction of democratic reforms were presented as a commitment to ensuring a stable transition that prioritized the interests of Hong Kong's citizens. Conversely, the government of the People's Republic of China viewed these political reforms as a violation of the spirit of the **1984** Sino-British Joint Declaration and an attempt to leave behind a legacy that would destabilize the territory post-1997. 

Within Hong Kong, local reactions were similarly polarized. Pro-democracy advocates generally welcomed the reforms initiated under the **Major** administration as a necessary step toward greater representative government. Critics, including certain members of the business community and pro-Beijing factions, argued that these unilateral changes caused unnecessary friction with Beijing, potentially undermining the long-term prosperity and stability of the Special Administrative Region. Later scholarship often debates whether **Major**’s approach represented a genuine moral commitment to Hong Kong's political development or if it was a final assertion of British geopolitical relevance before the inevitable withdrawal.
