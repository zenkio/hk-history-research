## Missionary and Interpreter
**Karl Gützlaff** (1803–1851) arrived in East Asia in the early 19th century, eventually settling in Macau. He is best known for his role as a translator and diplomat during the First Opium War, serving on the HMS *Imogene* and later assisting in the negotiations of the **Treaty of Nanking** in 1842. His command of the Chinese language made him indispensable to British officials, though his reliance on Chinese assistants to gather intelligence has been a subject of scholarly scrutiny regarding the accuracy and ethics of his reports.

## Connection to Hong Kong
**Gützlaff** was an early advocate for the development of Hong Kong as a base for missionary and commercial expansion. Following the British acquisition of the territory, he served as the **Chinese Secretary** to the colonial government from **1843** until his death. In this capacity, he played a role in the early administrative development of the colony. He was also a prolific writer, producing numerous religious and secular tracts in Chinese that circulated throughout the region.

## Differing Interpretations
Historical perspectives on **Gützlaff** remain polarized. British colonial records often celebrate his linguistic achievements and his efforts to bridge the gap between East and West. Conversely, some mainland Chinese and local historiography have characterized him as an agent of imperialism, noting that his intelligence-gathering and translation work directly facilitated the British occupation. Modern scholarship often takes a more nuanced view, acknowledging both his genuine religious dedication and his complicity in colonial expansion. His legacy remains contentious, particularly regarding the reliability of the reports he provided to colonial administrators about the local population and the inland territories of the Qing Empire.
