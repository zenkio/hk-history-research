## Role in the 1894 Plague Outbreak
In **June 1894**, the Japanese government dispatched **Kitasato Shibasaburō** (北里柴三郎) to Hong Kong to investigate the bubonic plague epidemic that had recently emerged. Arriving shortly after the French-Swiss scientist **Alexandre Yersin**, **Kitasato** was provided access to the **Alice Memorial Hospital** and the **Government Civil Hospital** by colonial authorities. Within days of his arrival, he announced the discovery of a bacillus he identified as the causative agent of the plague.

## Scientific Controversy and Interpretation
**Kitasato**'s initial findings were published in the *Lancet* and appeared to confirm the existence of the bacterium. However, his work was soon mired in debate. **Kitasato** described a motile, short-rod organism, whereas **Yersin** described a non-motile, polar-staining bacterium. Subsequent analysis by international scientists led to the conclusion that **Yersin** had isolated the true pathogen, now known as *Yersinia pestis*.

Historians offer varying interpretations of this discrepancy. Some early accounts attributed the confusion to the contamination of **Kitasato**'s samples, possibly by pneumococcus. Others suggest that the limitations of contemporary laboratory conditions in the **Tai Ping Shan** district made isolation difficult for both parties. While modern medical consensus largely credits **Yersin**, Japanese scholarship often highlights the pioneering role **Kitasato** played in establishing bacteriology in Asia and notes that he was working under extreme pressure during an ongoing public health crisis. The rivalry remains a foundational case study in the history of microbiology and the evolution of international scientific peer review.
