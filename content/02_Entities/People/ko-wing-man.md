## Early Career and Medical Background

**Ko Wing-man** (高永文) trained as an orthopedic surgeon and spent much of his early career working within the public hospital system under the Hospital Authority in Hong Kong. He gained public recognition for his crisis management skills and dedication, particularly during the **2003** SARS outbreak when he served as the Hospital Authority's Director of Professional Services and Public Relations.

## Secretary for Food and Health

In **July 2012**, Ko was appointed as the Secretary for Food and Health by Chief Executive **Leung Chun-ying** (梁振英). His appointment was generally welcomed by the medical community and the broader public due to his extensive experience in public healthcare administration and his approachable public persona.

## The 2015 Public Housing Drinking Water Scandal

In **mid-2015**, public concern escalated dramatically after elevated levels of lead were discovered in the drinking water of newly constructed public housing estates, most notably at Kai Ching Estate (啟晴邨). As Secretary for Food and Health, Ko played a central role in the government's response to the crisis.

### Government Response and Public Perception

Critics and local residents raised concerns regarding the potential health impacts on vulnerable populations, especially children and pregnant women, demanding swift action and comprehensive blood testing. Ko coordinated with the Department of Health to offer free blood tests for affected residents and manage public health communications.

While some community groups and pan-democratic legislators criticized the government for a slow initial response and inadequate accountability from housing contractors, Ko's personal handling of the medical fallout earned praise from supporters who viewed him as empathetic and pragmatic under pressure. Evaluations of his overall handling vary; supporters emphasize his efforts to restore public confidence through transparent health screenings, whereas detractors argue the administration downplayed systemic regulatory failures.
