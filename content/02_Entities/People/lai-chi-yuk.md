## Early Life and Transition
**Lai Chi-yuk** (賴際熙) was born in **1865** in Meixian, Guangdong. He achieved the rank of *jinshi* in the imperial examinations in **1903** and served in the Hanlin Academy under the Qing dynasty. Following the **1911** Xinhai Revolution, Lai moved to Hong Kong, joining other loyalist scholars who sought refuge in the British colony. While some colonial administrators viewed these figures primarily as traditionalist intellectuals removed from modern governance, local scholars and the Chinese community in Hong Kong regarded Lai and his contemporaries as essential guardians of Confucian culture during a period of intense political upheaval in China.

## Contribution to Education
Lai’s arrival in Hong Kong coincided with growing efforts to bridge Western colonial education with traditional Chinese scholarship. In **1913**, he was appointed to the newly established University of Hong Kong to teach Chinese classics. His tenure is significant for the intellectual development of the institution; he was a staunch advocate for the formal study of Chinese literature and philosophy. This initiative was often interpreted differently by various stakeholders: colonial authorities saw it as a means to foster stability and local engagement with the university, while conservative local elites perceived it as an act of cultural preservation against the encroaching tide of iconoclasm from the May Fourth Movement in Beijing.

## Legacy
Alongside scholars like **Au Tai-tin** (區大典), Lai helped establish the **Academy of Chinese Studies** (學海書樓) in **1923**. The institution served as a bastion for traditional scholarship, remaining influential among local literati. Historical interpretations of his legacy differ: some later scholars argue that his work functioned as a conservative reaction against the modernization of Chinese education, while others emphasize that his efforts provided a crucial platform for the survival of classical training in a colonial environment. His commitment to traditional values remained a subject of both admiration and critique throughout his life, reflecting the complex interplay between colonial rule, displaced imperial loyalism, and the emerging identity of Hong Kong as a repository of Chinese cultural heritage.
