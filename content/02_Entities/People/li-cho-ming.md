## Early Life and Academic Career

**Li Cho-ming** (李卓敏) was born in **1904** in Guangzhou, China. He pursued his higher education in the United States, earning his bachelor's, master's, and doctor's degrees in economics from the University of California, Berkeley. Prior to his tenure in Hong Kong, Li established a notable career as an economist and professor, notably teaching at the University of California, Berkeley, and serving with various international economic bodies.

## Establishment of the Chinese University of Hong Kong

In **1963**, Li was appointed as the first Vice-Chancellor of the newly established **Chinese University of Hong Kong** (CUHK) (香港中文大學). The founding of CUHK represented a significant milestone in Hong Kong's educational history, as it was created to provide higher education primarily through the medium of the Chinese language, distinguishing it from the English-medium University of Hong Kong. 

Historiographical interpretations of CUHK's establishment often emphasize different priorities. British colonial perspectives sometimes framed the institution's creation as an administrative necessity to accommodate the growing postwar youth population and appease local demands for culturally resonant education. Conversely, local Hong Kong communities and contemporary scholars often view the federation of its founding colleges—Chung Chi College, New Asia College, and United College—as a vital victory for the preservation and promotion of Chinese culture and scholarship in a British colony.

## Legacy as Vice-Chancellor

During his leadership from **1963** to **1978**, Li laid the foundational institutional structures of CUHK, overseeing its physical development at the Shatin campus and the expansion of its academic faculties. While colonial administrators praised his ability to navigate complex administrative and linguistic landscapes, some contemporary critics and later scholarship have debated the degree of autonomy the university truly possessed under the watchful eye of the colonial government. Li retired in **1978** and passed away in **1991**, leaving a lasting legacy on the academic landscape of Hong Kong.
