## Background and Appointment

**Lord Napier** (律勞卑), officially **William John Napier, 9th Lord Napier**, was appointed in **1833** by the British government as the first Chief Superintendent of Trade in China. This appointment followed the **Abolition of the East India Company Monopoly** in **1834**, which ended the company's exclusive control over British trade with China and necessitated direct crown representation.

## The Canton Mission and the Napier Affair

Upon arriving in Macau and subsequently traveling to Canton (广州) in **1834**, Napier attempted to establish direct diplomatic communication with the Viceroy of Canton on terms of sovereign equality. This approach directly violated the strict Qing tributary protocols, which restricted foreign diplomats to dealing exclusively through the licensed monopoly merchants, known as the Cohong (公行). 

### Historiographical Interpretations

Interpretations of the ensuing confrontation, known as **The Napier Affair in Canton**, vary significantly across different historical perspectives:

*   **British Colonial Historiography:** Traditionally framed Napier's mission as a righteous endeavor for free trade and diplomatic recognition, characterizing Qing officials as arrogant, obstinate, and ignorant of international law.
*   **Qing and Mainland Scholarship:** Viewed Napier's actions as a deliberate violation of Chinese sovereignty and established legal frameworks, interpreting his direct approach as gunboat diplomacy and an early manifestation of Western imperialist aggression.
*   **Modern Scholarship:** Tends to view the episode through a structural lens, emphasizing a profound cultural and political misunderstanding between two incompatible systems of international relations—the British Westphalian nation-state model versus the Qing tribute system.

## Legacy and Impact on Hong Kong

Napier's mission ended in failure; isolated by Chinese authorities, cut off from trade, and suffering from a fatal bout of malaria, he was forced to withdraw to Macau, where he died in **1834**. While Napier never set foot on Hong Kong Island, his mission's failure convinced London that peaceful, regulated commerce within the Qing empire was impossible without territorial leverage. Later scholarship widely regards the impasse he encountered as a crucial catalyst that paved the way for the First Opium War and the eventual establishment of Hong Kong as a British colony.
