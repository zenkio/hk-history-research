## Background and Official Role

**Lu Kun** (盧坤) served as the Viceroy of Liangguang (Guangdong and Guangxi) during a critical transitional period in the early **1830s**, a time marked by shifting trade dynamics and rising friction between the **Qing Empire** and British commercial interests.

## The Napier Affair and Trade Monopolies

As the abolition of the **East India Company** (EIC) monopoly in **1833** dismantled the traditional *Canton System* of regulated trade, the British government dispatched **Lord Napier** (William John Napier) to Canton (Guangzhou) as Chief Superintendent of Trade. Napier attempted to establish direct, equal diplomatic communication with Qing authorities, bypassing the traditional merchant intermediaries known as the *Cohong*.

**Lu Kun**, adhering strictly to imperial protocol and the defensive policies of the **Daoguang Emperor**, refused to treat Napier as an equal diplomat. **Lu** insisted that foreign officials must communicate through lower-ranking Chinese merchants and submit petitions rather than letters. This standoff escalated into a major diplomatic crisis, with **Lu** ordering the stoppage of British trade and the temporary confinement of the factory areas in **September 1834**.

## Historiographical Interpretations

Interpretations of **Lu Kun**’s actions vary significantly across different historical perspectives:

- **Qing and Traditional Chinese Accounts:** View **Lu** as a resolute defender of national sovereignty and imperial dignity who successfully upheld dynastic laws against unruly foreign incursions.
- **British Colonial Historiography:** Characterize **Lu** as obstinate and out of touch, arguing that his refusal to adapt to modern diplomatic norms contributed directly to the breakdown of relations and the subsequent armed conflict.
- **Modern Scholarship:** Often analyzes **Lu** as constrained by rigid institutional structures and incomplete intelligence regarding British military and political changes following the end of the EIC monopoly.
