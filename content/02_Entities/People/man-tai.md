## Introduction

**Man Tai** (文帝), literally translated as the Emperor of Literature, is a traditional Chinese deity often worshipped alongside **Mo Tai** (武帝, the Martial Emperor, commonly identified as Guan Yu). In the context of Hong Kong history, **Man Tai** is primarily known through his veneration in the **Man Mo Temple** (文武廟) in **Tai Po** (大埔), New Territories.

## Connection to the Tai Po Man Mo Temple

The construction of the Tai Po Man Mo Temple is a key event linking **Man Tai** to local Hong Kong history. While exact archival details regarding the temple's founding remain sparse, local tradition and historical surveys suggest the temple was established in the late 19th century, following the British lease of the New Territories in **1898**. The temple served not only as a place of worship but also as a vital administrative and judicial hub for the **Tai Po Tai Uk Hui** (大埔舊墟) and surrounding indigenous lineages.

## Historiographical Perspectives

Interpretations of the temple and its deities vary among different historical viewpoints:

* **Local Communities:** To the rural inhabitants and indigenous clans of the New Territories, **Man Tai** and the temple served as a mechanism for local governance, conflict resolution, and community cohesion during a period of rapid transition under British administration.
* **Colonial Administration:** British colonial records often viewed these rural religious institutions through the lens of local custom and land management, occasionally noting their role in community arbitration without emphasizing the theological significance of **Man Tai** himself.
* **Later Scholarship:** Modern historians emphasize that the establishment of temples dedicated to **Man Tai** in the New Territories highlights how rural communities sought to preserve traditional Qing-era social structures and cultural identity amid the encroachment of British colonial rule.
