## Background and Context

**Martim Afonso de Melo Coutinho** was appointed as an envoy and commander by the Portuguese Crown to establish official trade and diplomatic relations with Ming China. Following the earlier pioneering voyage of **Jorge Álvares** in **1513**, the Portuguese sought to formalize commercial access to the lucrative Pearl River Delta trade network.

## Military Conflicts in the Hong Kong Region

In **1521**, **Melo Coutinho** arrived in Chinese waters with a fleet, heading toward the Tamão (Tuen Mun 屯門) area. By the time of his arrival, the geopolitical situation had deteriorated significantly. Local Ming authorities, alarmed by Portuguese fortification building, tribute violations, and reports of piracy and the kidnapping of children, had reversed their previous tolerance.

This tension culminated in the **Battle of Tunmen** in **1521** and subsequent clashes such as the **Battle of Xicaowan** in **1522**. During these engagements, the technologically superior Ming naval forces—utilizing heavy artillery and fire ships—repelled the Portuguese fleet. **Melo Coutinho** found himself embroiled in these hostilities, which resulted in a heavy defeat for the Portuguese, the loss of several ships and commanders, and the expulsion of Portuguese traders from the immediate estuary region for a period.

## Historiographical Interpretations

Historians differ in their framing of these early Sino-Portuguese encounters. Later Portuguese colonial narratives often emphasized commercial ambition, exploration, and initial misunderstandings, portraying their figures as pioneers of global trade. In contrast, traditional Chinese scholarship and official Ming records viewed these events through the lens of coastal defense and maritime prohibition (*haijin* 海禁), characterizing the Portuguese as foreign intruders and violators of imperial law. Modern scholarship seeks to balance these views, examining the clashes as an inevitable friction between expanding European maritime empires and the established tribute-trade system of the Ming Dynasty in the waters that would centuries later become Hong Kong.
