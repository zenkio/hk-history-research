## Early Administration and Governance

**Murray MacLehose** (麥理浩) assumed office as the 25th Governor of Hong Kong in **1971**. His tenure marked a distinct departure from the traditional laissez-faire approach of his predecessors, initiating sweeping reforms in response to the social unrest of the **Star Ferry Riots** and the **1967 Leftist Riots**. Building upon foundations like the **City District Officer Scheme Launched** in the late 1960s and the cultural outreach of the **First Festival of Hong Kong**, MacLehose sought to bridge the gap between the colonial government and the local population.

## Anti-Corruption and Public Services

Facing systemic police corruption—typified when **Police Chief Peter Godber Flees** in **1973**—MacLehose decisively prioritized institutional integrity by spearheading the **Establishment of the ICAC** (Independent Commission Against Corruption) in **1974**. His administration also heavily invested in public infrastructure and social goods, introducing the **Introduction of Nine-Year Free Education** and expanding public housing. Leisure and recreation also grew under his watch, highlighted by the **Opening of Ocean Park** in **1977** and the creation of the MacLehose Trail.

## Historiographical Perspectives

Interpretations of MacLehose's legacy vary across different viewpoints. British colonial historiography often portrays him as an enlightened reformer who successfully transformed Hong Kong into a stable, modern society with a distinct local identity. Conversely, local Hong Kong and New Territories communities sometimes viewed sweeping land developments and administrative centralization with mixed feelings, as traditional rural autonomy was frequently challenged. Later scholarship frequently analyzes his era as the golden age of Hong Kong governance, though critics note it remained an unrepresentative administration that deferred substantive democratization due to geopolitical pressures from Beijing.
