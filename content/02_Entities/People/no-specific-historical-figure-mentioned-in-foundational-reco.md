## Origins and Context
In **1938**, the Chinese Manufacturers' Union of Hong Kong (香港中華廠商聯合會) organized the First Hong Kong Industrial Exhibition. Held at the **Hong Kong Chinese General Chamber of Commerce** premises, the event was conceived against the backdrop of the **Second Sino-Japanese War**. As international trade routes were disrupted and the influx of refugees from the mainland provided both a labor pool and consumer base, local entrepreneurs sought to promote goods 'made in Hong Kong' to replace previously imported products.

## Historical Interpretations
Historians offer differing perspectives on the significance of this exhibition. From a **British colonial** administrative viewpoint, the event is often framed as evidence of the resilience and entrepreneurial spirit of the local population under colonial governance. Conversely, some **later scholarship** and **local community** narratives emphasize the exhibition as a pragmatic response by Hong Kong's grassroots industrial class to the existential threats posed by the war, asserting an identity distinct from both the mainland and the colonial administration. While archival records confirm the event's logistical success, the degree to which it was a calculated political statement versus a purely economic survival strategy remains a subject of academic debate.

## Legacy
Though the exhibition was modest by later standards, it established a template for annual trade fairs that would define the city’s economic trajectory during the **1950s** and **1960s**. By showcasing items ranging from textiles to enamelware, the organizers successfully shifted the perception of Hong Kong from a transit point for goods to a center of domestic production. The success of this inaugural event is widely cited as the precursor to the rapid industrialization that became the hallmark of the territory's postwar economic miracle.
