## Legislative Background
The **Race Discrimination Ordinance** (Cap. 602) was passed by the Legislative Council of Hong Kong on **July 10, 2008**, and came into full effect on **July 10, 2009**. The enactment followed years of public debate and advocacy from civil society groups and international human rights bodies regarding the lack of specific legal protections against racial discrimination in Hong Kong.

## Differing Interpretations
Perspectives on the RDO vary significantly across different sectors of society. From the perspective of the Hong Kong government at the time, the ordinance represented a pragmatic balance between safeguarding individual rights and maintaining the social harmony and commercial flexibility of a diverse international city. Government officials emphasized that the law was designed to be "light-touch" to avoid excessive litigation.

Conversely, many civil society organizations and advocates for ethnic minorities argued that the ordinance was insufficient. Key criticisms included the exemption of the government's immigration and nationality-related functions from the scope of the law, which some scholars viewed as a continuation of colonial-era administrative discretion. Others argued that the law failed to adequately address institutional racism or provide sufficient remedies for marginalized communities. 

Later scholarship has often focused on the gap between the ordinance’s intent and its practical impact. While some legal scholars suggest it has established a necessary legal framework for redress, others point to the limited number of successful claims brought to the **Equal Opportunities Commission** (平等機會委員會) as evidence of the ordinance’s restricted accessibility. The debate continues to center on whether the legislation serves as a robust shield against discrimination or as a symbolic measure that leaves structural inequalities largely unaddressed.
