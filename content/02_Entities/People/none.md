## Technical Development
Agrarian terracing in the **New Territories (新界)** involved the modification of steep hillsides into flat steps to facilitate wet-rice cultivation. This process required a sophisticated understanding of hydrology to ensure that water from mountain streams was distributed evenly across different levels of paddies. Land management was typically organized at the village level, utilizing a system of irrigation channels and ponds to sustain crops throughout the growing season.

## Differing Interpretations
Historical perspectives on these systems differ significantly. **British colonial** officials and early administrators often characterized these agricultural practices as primitive or low-yield subsistence farming. Conversely, **local New Territories communities** view the terraces as an ancestral legacy of engineering ingenuity and communal cooperation. **Later scholarship** in environmental history interprets these systems as a highly efficient adaptation to the region's mountainous topography, emphasizing the sustainability of the traditional land-use model.

## Land Tenure and Social Structure
Land management was intrinsically linked to the **clan (宗族)** system. The allocation of terraced plots and the rights to water access often reflected the social hierarchy within a village. After the **1898** lease of the New Territories, the British administration sought to implement a formal system of land registration. This created a conflict between the colonial legal preference for documented title and the local reliance on customary land rights and ancestral agreements, a tension that persisted for decades.
