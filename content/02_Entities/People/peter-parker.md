## Introduction

**Peter Parker** (伯駕) was born in **1804** and became one of the earliest Protestant medical missionaries sent to China by the American Board of Commissioners for Foreign Missions. Arriving in **1834**, Parker initially established a medical practice in Canton (Guangzhou), where his ophthalmic work—particularly treating cataracts and tumors—gained widespread renown among local patients and foreign merchants alike. 

## Connection to Hong Kong and the Medical Missionary Society Hospital

While Parker's primary operations were centered in Canton and later Macau, his broader institutional efforts, including the establishment of the Medical Missionary Society in China (**1838**), directly influenced the trajectory of Western medicine in the region leading up to the British acquisition of Hong Kong in **1841**. The initiatives championed by Parker and his contemporaries helped conceptualize the role of hospitals as tools of both humanitarian aid and Western cultural diplomacy.

## Historiographical Interpretations

Interpretations of Parker's legacy vary across different historical perspectives. 

* **Western and Missionary Accounts:** Traditional British and American missionary records generally portray Parker as a benevolent pioneer who used medical science to open doors for Christianity and modern education in a closed Qing empire.
* **Qing and Critical Scholarship:** Conversely, Qing officials and later critical historians often view figures like Parker through the lens of nineteenth-century imperialism, noting how medical missions operated alongside gunboat diplomacy and commercial expansion, serving as advance scouts for Western encroachment.
* **Local Hong Kong and New Territories Perspectives:** Within local Hong Kong historiography, Parker is seen as part of the broader transnational network of practitioners who bridged Western clinical practices with the burgeoning colonial port city, though direct documentation of his physical presence in early Hong Kong remains secondary to his institutional impact through the Medical Missionary Society.

Parker later transitioned into full-time diplomacy, serving as the United States Commissioner to China during the **1850s** before returning to the United States, where he died in **1888**.
