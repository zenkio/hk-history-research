## Role in the Convention of Peking
**Prince Gong** (恭親王), born Aisin Gioro Yixin, served as the primary negotiator for the Qing government during the **1860** negotiations with British and French forces. Following the capture of Beijing, **Prince Gong** signed the **Convention of Peking** on **October 24, 1860**. This treaty formally ended the Second Opium War and included the transfer of the Kowloon Peninsula south of Boundary Street to the British Crown.

## Historical Interpretations
Historians offer varying perspectives on **Prince Gong**'s motivations and the significance of his actions regarding Hong Kong. From the perspective of late-Qing imperial administration, the cession of Kowloon was often framed as a strategic concession made under extreme military duress to preserve the ruling dynasty's legitimacy and territorial integrity elsewhere. 

Conversely, British colonial narratives historically emphasized the perceived necessity of the Kowloon Peninsula for the defense and expansion of Hong Kong’s harbor trade. Local Hong Kong and New Territories communities, as well as later nationalist scholarship, often view the **1860** agreement as an 'unequal treaty,' highlighting the lack of sovereign consent and the permanent displacement of local residents' traditional land rights. While British records characterize the acquisition as a formal diplomatic settlement, contemporary Chinese historiography frequently underscores the role of the Convention in the 'century of humiliation.' There remains ongoing academic debate regarding whether **Prince Gong** truly believed the cession would be temporary or if he viewed it as an unavoidable finality given the military disparity at the time.
