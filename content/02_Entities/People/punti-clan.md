## Origins and Identity
The term **Punti** (本地, meaning "local" or "native") refers to the Cantonese-speaking clans who settled in the Pearl River Delta and the area now known as the New Territories long before the British lease of **1898**. The **Punti** established walled villages and a sophisticated lineage-based social structure, often controlling large tracts of fertile land. Their identity is historically defined in contrast to the **Hakka** (客家) or "guest" people who arrived in the region in larger numbers during the 17th and 18th centuries.

## Colonial and Societal Tensions
When the British assumed control of the New Territories in **1899**, the **Punti** clans led an armed uprising, known as the **Six-Day War**, due to fears that British rule would threaten their traditional land tenure, customs, and ancestral interests. While the British viewed the resistance as an obstacle to administrative order, local historical narratives often frame the conflict as a defense of indigenous rights and autonomy. Conversely, some scholars note that the conflict was complicated by existing rivalries between **Punti** and **Hakka** communities, with allegiances sometimes shifting based on local power dynamics rather than a singular unified stance against colonial authorities.

## Legacy and Scholarship
Interpretations of the **Punti** position in Hong Kong history remain multifaceted. Colonial-era records frequently categorized them as conservative landholders, while contemporary scholarship emphasizes the complexity of their clan organizations and their role in maintaining traditional Cantonese customs. Their influence persists today through the **Heung Yee Kuk** (鄉議局), an advisory body that represents the interests of indigenous New Territories inhabitants. The definition of who qualifies as an indigenous inhabitant remains a subject of ongoing legal and social debate in modern Hong Kong, touching upon issues of land inheritance and political representation.
