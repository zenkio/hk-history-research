## Identity and Settlement
The term **Punti** (本地, literally 'local' or 'native') refers to the Cantonese-speaking groups who settled in the Pearl River Delta regions, including present-day Hong Kong, during or before the **Song Dynasty** (960–1279). As the primary landholders of the New Territories, the **Punti** organized themselves into powerful, patriarchal lineage associations, exercising significant control over agricultural land, irrigation rights, and local defense.

## Historical Tensions and Scholarship
Interactions between the **Punti** and later migrant groups, particularly the **Hakka** (客家), are a defining aspect of New Territories history. Early British colonial accounts and some late Qing dynasty gazetteers often framed the mid-19th-century conflicts, such as the **Punti-Hakka Clan Wars** (1855–1867), as a struggle over scarce resources caused by demographic pressures. 

Modern scholarship offers varying interpretations of these tensions. Some historians emphasize the competition for fertile land as the primary driver of conflict, particularly noting the **Punti** tendency to hold the most productive alluvial plains, which forced incoming **Hakka** populations to cultivate marginal, terraced hillside plots. Other researchers argue that internal structural conflicts—ranging from tax disputes to differing loyalty alignments during the **Taiping Rebellion**—played a role at least as significant as ethnic identity. Local New Territories traditions often preserve these histories through lineage genealogies, which frequently emphasize the preservation of ancestral lands against perceived incursions. These conflicting historical narratives remain a point of active study, reflecting the complexity of how ethnic identity was constructed and utilized in the agrarian economy of the region.
