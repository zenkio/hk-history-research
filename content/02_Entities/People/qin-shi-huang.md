## Imperial Expansion and the Lingnan Campaign

**Qin Shi Huang** (秦始皇), who reigned as the First Emperor of the Qin dynasty from **221 BCE** to **210 BCE**, fundamentally altered the geopolitical landscape of East Asia by unifying China. Following centralization, he turned his military ambitions toward the southern frontier, known as Lingnan (嶺南), home to various indigenous communities often referred to collectively as the Baiyue (百越).

In **219 BCE**, Qin forces launched a major campaign southward. To supply these troops, the emperor ordered the construction of the Lingqu Canal (靈渠), connecting the Yangtze and Pearl River systems. These military actions eventually led to the establishment of commanderies in the south, notably Nanhai (南海), Guilin (桂林), and Xiang (象). 

## Historiographical Perspectives and Hong Kong Connections

Interpretations of Qin Shi Huang's legacy in relation to Hong Kong vary across different historiographical traditions:

* **Mainland Chinese Scholarship (Qing/Beijing Perspective):** Official and traditional mainland historiography generally views Qin Shi Huang's southern expansion as a progressive step in unifying the Chinese nation and integrating the Lingnan region—including territories corresponding to modern Guangdong and parts of Hong Kong—into the centralized imperial administration.
* **Local Hong Kong and New Territories Communities:** Indigenous lineages and local historical traditions in the New Territories often trace the origins of early Han settlement further back or emphasize local autonomy, viewing early imperial incursions through the lens of migration waves rather than direct, day-to-day administrative control during the Qin period itself.
* **British Colonial and Later Scholarship:** Western and modern academic analyses often treat the early Qin presence in the far south as tenuous and largely military in nature, questioning the degree of actual governance exerted over coastal areas like Hong Kong prior to the subsequent establishment of the Nanyue Kingdom (南越國) under **Zhao Tuo** (趙佗).

Due to the scarcity of direct archaeological evidence within the territory dating specifically to the short-lived Qin dynasty, the exact nature of early interactions between local tribes and the expanding Qin empire remains a subject of ongoing academic debate.
