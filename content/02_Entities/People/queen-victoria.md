## Reign and the Acquisition of Hong Kong

During the reign of **Queen Victoria** (維多利亞女王), the British Empire formally acquired Hong Kong Island following the First Opium War. On **January 26, 1841**, British forces landed at Possession Point, claiming the territory in her name. British colonial historiography traditionally framed this period as the dawn of free trade, rule of law, and modernization under the protection of the Crown. In contrast, Qing dynasty officials and modern mainland Chinese scholarship view these events through the lens of foreign imperialism, unequal treaties, and national humiliation.

## Colonial Society and Nomenclature

As the British administration expanded, the monarch's name became deeply embedded in the physical and institutional landscape of the colony. Major urban developments, such as the Victoria City district and Victoria Peak, were named in her honor. Elite colonial institutions established during this era, including the **Hong Kong Club** founded in **1846**, reflected the tastes and social structures of the British ruling class. Local Chinese communities, while operating largely under their own customary laws and traditional leadership structures—exemplified by the establishment of the **Tung Wah Hospital** (東華醫院) in **1872**—nevertheless navigated their civic affairs under the ultimate authority of the British Crown.

## Historiographical Perspectives

Interpretations of Queen Victoria's symbolic role in Hong Kong history vary widely. While early British accounts emphasized benevolent paternalism and the civilizing mission, subsequent scholarship offers more critical evaluations of racial stratification, colonial exploitation, and the imposition of foreign rule. Local Hong Kong historians often focus on how indigenous and immigrant Chinese communities forged their own identities and agency within the institutional framework established during her long reign.
