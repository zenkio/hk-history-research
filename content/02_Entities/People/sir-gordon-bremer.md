## Naval Command and Early Operations

**Sir Gordon Bremer** (伯麥) served as the Commander-in-Chief of the British naval forces in China during the early stages of the First Opium War. Working alongside **Captain Charles Elliot** (義律), the Joint Plenipotentiaries sought to secure British trade interests and territorial acquisitions from the Qing Empire. **Bremer** commanded the naval operations that pressured Chinese authorities along the southern coast, leading to strategic engagements in the Pearl River Delta.

## Possession of Hong Kong

Following the disputed and ultimately unratified Convention of Chuenpee in **January 1841**, **Bremer** participated in the initial occupation of Hong Kong Island. On **26 January 1841**, British forces formally raised the Union Flag, an event in which **Bremer** played a central role alongside **Elliot**. Shortly thereafter, **Elliot** issued a proclamation to the inhabitants of Hong Kong, assuring local fishermen and villagers that they would be governed according to local customs while under British administration.

## Historiographical Interpretations

Perspectives on **Bremer**'s actions and the seizure of Hong Kong vary significantly across historical traditions:

* **British Colonial Historiography:** Traditionally framed **Bremer** and **Elliot** as pioneering agents of free trade and empire, establishing a secure port that would grow into a global commercial hub.
* **Qing and Mainland Chinese Scholarship:** Views **Bremer** as an aggressive military aggressor representing imperialist encroachment, enforcing unequal treaties born from the opium trade.
* **Local Hong Kong Perspective:** Modern local historians often emphasize that the island's early development relied heavily on the indigenous Tanka and Hakka populations, whose lives were abruptly militarized and redirected by British commanders like **Bremer**.
