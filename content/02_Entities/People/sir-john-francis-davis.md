## Early Administration and Governance
**Sir John Francis Davis** (戴維斯) served as the second Governor of Hong Kong from **1844** to **1848**. A scholar of Chinese language and culture, he previously served with the East India Company and the Macartney Embassy. His tenure is marked by a transition from military to civil governance, though his period in office remained contentious.

## Fiscal Policy and Local Friction
**Davis** is frequently associated with the implementation of controversial taxation measures intended to make the colony self-sufficient. His efforts to impose property and police taxes were met with fierce resistance from the local merchant class and the European community. Scholars often highlight that **Davis**’s rigid fiscal policies contributed to the **First Mass Protest and Strike**, as the local population and merchants perceived the levies as both burdensome and inequitable. 

## Differing Historical Interpretations
Historians offer varying perspectives on his legacy. Colonial-era narratives often focused on his administrative reforms and his efforts to establish a structured legal system modeled on British law. Conversely, contemporary scholars and local community historians emphasize the cultural insensitivity of his policies, noting that his approach often alienated the Chinese population and ignored local social structures. While some records from the Qing-era view his tenure as an era of intrusive colonial expansion, modern local historical accounts focus on the resilience of the Hong Kong population in responding to his mandates. The extent to which his personal rigidity dictated the instability of early Hong Kong remains a subject of ongoing debate among academics, as some point to external economic pressures during the mid-1840s as an equally significant factor in the colony's volatile early years.
