## Command during the Second Opium War
**Sir Michael Seymour** (1802–1887) served as the Commander-in-Chief of the East Indies and China Station from **1856** to **1858**. He is best known for his direct naval involvement following the **Arrow Incident** in October **1856**. Following the seizure of the Chinese-registered lorcha *Arrow* by Qing officials, **Seymour** led the bombardment and capture of the Bogue forts and the city of Canton (Guangzhou).

## Impact on Hong Kong
For the young British colony of Hong Kong, **Seymour**’s actions were viewed by colonial officials as a necessary defense of British commercial interests and treaty rights. The local colonial administration relied heavily on **Seymour**’s naval strength to maintain maritime control in the Pearl River Delta. Conversely, Qing authorities and many local Chinese residents viewed these naval operations as unprovoked acts of imperial aggression that destabilized the region, led to significant loss of life, and disrupted traditional trade networks between the mainland and the island.

## Historical Interpretations
Later scholarship often frames **Seymour** as a key figure in the escalation of hostilities that led to the **Treaty of Tientsin** in **1858**. British imperial historians of the era typically lauded him for his decisive application of 'gunboat diplomacy' to protect British subjects. However, modern revisionist historians often emphasize the disproportionate force used against Qing targets, noting that the hostilities initiated under his command placed immense strain on the Hong Kong community, as the city became a focal point for military mobilization and a target for retaliatory measures from the mainland, including the poisoning of the E-Sing Bakery in **1857**. Whether **Seymour** was acting strictly within the bounds of his orders to protect colonial trade or was an agent of aggressive expansionism remains a subject of varying historical perspective.
