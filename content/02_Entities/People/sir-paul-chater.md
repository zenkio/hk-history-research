## Early Life and Business Career
Born **Paul Catchick Chater** in **1846** in Calcutta to an Armenian family, he arrived in Hong Kong in **1864**. He quickly established himself in the colonial mercantile sector, initially working in banking before transitioning into real estate, mining, and maritime infrastructure. He became a key figure in the colonial administration, serving as a member of the Legislative Council and the Executive Council for several decades.

## The Praya Reclamation Scheme
**Chater** is most famously associated with the **Praya Reclamation Scheme**, initiated in **1887**. This ambitious project involved reclaiming land from the harbour to extend the Central district. While contemporary colonial authorities viewed the project as an essential modernization of the city's infrastructure to facilitate global trade, some later historians have examined the scheme's role in entrenching the power of the British 'hongs' and wealthy expatriate elite. The resulting expansion significantly altered the geography of the harbour and created the foundation for Hong Kong’s modern financial hub.

## Historical Interpretations
Perspectives on **Chater** reflect the broader complexities of colonial history. Within the traditional British colonial narrative, he is celebrated as a 'founding father' who utilized his wealth and influence to transform a barren island into a bustling metropolis. Conversely, critical scholarship and local community perspectives often note that such large-scale developments were primarily designed to serve the interests of the colonial elite, sometimes at the expense of local fishing communities and without significant input from the broader Chinese population. 

## Philanthropy and Legacy
Beyond his business interests, **Chater** was a prolific philanthropist, donating extensively to institutions such as the University of Hong Kong and the Royal Hong Kong Yacht Club. Upon his death in **1926**, he bequeathed his significant collection of historical paintings and prints to the government, which formed the nucleus of what later became the Hong Kong Museum of Art. His influence remains physically manifested in landmarks such as Chater Road and Chater Garden.
