## The 1966 Protest
In **April 1966**, the Hong Kong government approved a fare increase for the Star Ferry, a vital transport link between Kowloon and Hong Kong Island. At the time, public dissatisfaction with colonial governance, economic disparity, and the rising cost of living was high. On **April 4, 1966**, **So Sau-chung** (蘇守忠) began a solitary hunger strike in front of the Star Ferry Pier in Central, holding a sign protesting the fare hike. His action drew significant public attention, and he was arrested shortly thereafter for obstruction.

## Impact and Interpretations
The arrest of **So Sau-chung** acted as a flashpoint for widespread public demonstrations. While his initial protest was focused on the specific issue of ferry fares, the subsequent unrest—now known as the **Star Ferry Riots**—evolved into a broader expression of anti-colonial sentiment. 

Interpretations of the event remain varied. Contemporary colonial authorities often viewed the riots as an outburst of lawlessness spurred by agitators. In contrast, local social historians and later scholars have frequently characterized the unrest as a significant turning point in Hong Kong’s social consciousness, signaling growing frustration with the lack of representation and the alienation felt by the working class under British rule. Some later accounts highlight the riots as a precursor to the more politically charged 1967 Leftist Riots, though others argue that the 1966 protests were primarily socio-economic in origin rather than ideological or linked to the Cultural Revolution in mainland China. **So Sau-chung** himself remained a figure of historical interest as an early symbol of non-violent, grassroots dissent in a city then governed by an unelected administration.
