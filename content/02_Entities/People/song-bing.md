## Historical Context
In the historiography of Hong Kong, the term **Song Bing** (宋兵)—literally 'Song soldiers'—often refers to the soldiers of the **Southern Song** (南宋) dynasty who retreated into the Guangdong coastal regions following the Mongol invasion. While individual historical records for specific low-ranking soldiers are scarce, the presence of these military groups is a foundational element in the local lore of many indigenous clans in the **New Territories** (新界).

## Local Narratives and Militia
Local traditions often credit the ancestors of various clans, such as the **Tang** (鄧) or **Pang** (彭) lineages, with providing refuge or integration to remnants of the Song imperial court and its accompanying troops. According to these narratives, the formalization of clan-based militias was partly influenced by the organizational structures left behind by these soldiers. Historians note that these stories serve as a vital marker of identity, emphasizing a long-standing tradition of self-defense and local autonomy.

## Interpretations and Scholarship
Interpretations regarding the extent of the **Song** military presence in Hong Kong vary significantly. **British colonial** historians often viewed these stories as folklore, suggesting that formal administrative records from the imperial era do not provide sufficient evidence to support large-scale military encampments in the region. Conversely, **local community** historians emphasize the authenticity of these oral traditions, arguing that they reflect the actual survival strategies and military coordination required by settlers in the region against coastal threats. **Modern scholarship** tends to occupy a middle ground, acknowledging that while the specific identities of individual 'Song soldiers' remain elusive, the survival of these narratives highlights the historical importance of the region as a frontier space for refugees and migrants escaping political turmoil during the **13th century**.
