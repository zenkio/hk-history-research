## The Imperial Flight
Following the Mongol conquest of the Song capital, the court of the Southern **Song** dynasty (宋朝) fled south, eventually arriving in the coastal areas of present-day Hong Kong. Primary historical sources, such as the *History of Song* (宋史), document that the child emperors **Duanzong** (端宗, Zhao Shi) and **Bing** (帝昺, Zhao Bing) resided at **Kowloon** (九龍) and **Lantau Island** (大嶼山) between **1277** and **1278**.

## Historical Interpretations
The significance of this period is viewed through varying lenses. Local traditions in the New Territories, particularly among the indigenous clans (such as the **Tang** family), often emphasize the presence of the imperial court as a foundational moment that elevated the status of the region. This narrative has historically served to solidify the local identity and legitimacy of long-standing lineage groups.

Conversely, some academic scholars and colonial-era historians have approached these accounts with caution, noting that while the presence of the fleeing court is verifiable, the precise locations—such as the legendary **Sung Wong Toi** (宋王臺) boulder—are subject to tradition rather than archaeological confirmation. Qing-era chroniclers focused heavily on the fall of the dynasty as a tragedy of loyalty, while 20th-century narratives sometimes utilized the Song refugees to argue for the historical continuity of the region with the Chinese mainland. Regardless of these varying perspectives, the Song presence underscores Tuen Mun's historical importance as a strategic maritime hub in the Pearl River Delta during the transition between the Southern Song and Yuan dynasties.
