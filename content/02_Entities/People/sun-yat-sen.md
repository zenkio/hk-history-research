## Early Education and the Hong Kong College of Medicine

In **1887**, **Sun Yat-sen** (孫中山) enrolled in the newly established Opening of Hong Kong College of Medicine for Chinese (香港華人西醫書院). Studying under British colonial administration, Sun absorbed Western political ideas, modern sciences, and administrative concepts. It was during these student years in Hong Kong that he began actively discussing the overthrow of the **Qing** (清) dynasty with like-minded associates, utilizing the relative safety and freedom of speech afforded by the British colonial setting.

## Revolutionary Base and Organizing

Hong Kong served as an indispensable sanctuary and operational hub for Sun's revolutionary ambitions. In **1894**, Sun founded the Sun Yat-sen Founds the Hong Kong Xingzhonghui (香港興中會), a revolutionary society dedicated to China's rejuvenation. While British authorities generally tolerated these early activities, they later placed restrictions on Sun and his followers to maintain diplomatic neutrality with the Qing government. The volatile environment led to significant political violence, notably the Assassination of Yeung Ku-wan (楊衢雲被刺事件) in **1901**. Despite such pressures, Hong Kong remained crucial for logistical coordination, including providing Support for the Huizhou Uprising from Hong Kong (香港支援惠州起義) in **1900**.

## Later Visits and Historical Interpretations

Following the Outbreak of the 1911 Xinhai Revolution (辛亥革命爆發), Sun returned to the colony as a prominent statesman. During the Inauguration of the University of Hong Kong (香港大學成立) in **1912**, which absorbed his alma mater, Sun delivered a famous Sun Yat-sen's Address at HKU (孫中山香港大學演講), famously attributing the inspiration for his revolutionary ideas directly to the city of Hong Kong itself.

Historiographical interpretations of Sun's relationship with Hong Kong vary. Nationalist and mainland historiography emphasizes Hong Kong as the cradle of the bourgeois-democratic revolution that liberated China from feudalism. In contrast, British colonial accounts often highlighted the colony's role as a benevolent sanctuary of Western rule of law that accidentally nurtured Asian reform. Meanwhile, local Hong Kong perspectives increasingly view Sun as part of a distinct, homegrown regional network that shaped the city's unique identity independent of both British and mainland Chinese political orthodoxies.
