## Origins and Settlement
The **Tang lineage** (鄧氏族人) traces its ancestral roots in the region back to the **Song dynasty** (宋朝), with members eventually settling in the fertile plains of **Kam Tin** (錦田) in the northwestern New Territories. As one of the prominent **Five Great Clans** (五大氏族) of Hong Kong, the lineage established a vast network of walled villages, ancestral halls, and study halls to consolidate local influence, manage agricultural land, and prepare lineage members for the imperial civil service examinations.

## Establishment of the Yee Kung Study Hall
A notable example of their local institution-building is the establishment of the **Yee Kung Study Hall** (二公書室) in Kam Tin. Traditionally, such study halls served a dual purpose: functioning as educational facilities to groom scholars for the imperial exams and acting as ancestral shrines to reinforce clan solidarity and prestige within the community.

## Historiographical Perspectives
Interpretations of the role and status of the Tang lineage vary across different historical perspectives:

*   **Local New Territories Communities:** Indigenous narratives often emphasize the Tangs as pioneering settlers who reclaimed land, established security through walled villages, and maintained traditional customary rights over centuries.
*   **British Colonial Administration:** Early colonial records frequently viewed the New Territories lineages through the lens of land tenure and customary law, sometimes characterizing local gentry structures as traditional impediments to modern administration while simultaneously relying on village leaders for local governance.
*   **Modern Scholarship:** Recent academic research examines the Tangs through socio-economic frameworks, analyzing how lineage wealth, ancestral trusts (tso), and educational institutions like study halls enabled them to negotiate power relations dynamically with imperial dynasties, British colonial authorities, and neighboring clans.
