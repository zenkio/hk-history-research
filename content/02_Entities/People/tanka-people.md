## Origins and Identity
The **Tanka people** (**蜑家人**) are historically boat-dwelling communities inhabiting the coastal areas and waterways of Guangdong and Hong Kong. Long marginalized by land-based populations, they were traditionally viewed as a distinct demographic group with their own customs and dialects. Their integration into the broader fabric of Hong Kong society is a central theme in the study of the region's maritime history.

## Historiographical Interpretations
Perspectives on the Tanka vary significantly across different traditions. **Qing dynasty** records and land-based local elites often marginalized the Tanka as outsiders or boat people (*danmin* 蛋民), restricting their social mobility and rights to land ownership. **British colonial** administrations initially viewed the Tanka through the lens of maritime taxation and population census, gradually incorporating them into the colonial economy as vital participants in the fishing industry. **Local New Territories communities** maintained complex social boundaries with the Tanka, characterized by traditional distinctions between land-dwellers (*punti* 本地) and boat-dwellers. Meanwhile, **modern scholarship** increasingly emphasizes the Tanka as indigenous maritime actors who shaped the economic networks of the Pearl River Delta long before British colonization in **1841**.

## Connection to Hong Kong
The Tanka are deeply connected to the establishment of early fishing communities, prominently featured in the history of the **Establishment of the Tanka and Hoklo Fishing Communities**. As modernization and urbanization accelerated in the mid-to-late **20th century**, government resettlement policies and the decline of traditional inshore fishing led many Tanka families to transition from boat life to public housing ashore.
