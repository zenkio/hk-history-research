## Early Career and Appointment

**Thomas Chisholm Anstey** (安思地) arrived in Hong Kong to assume the post of Attorney General in **1856**. Known for his combative legal background and reformer zeal, Anstey quickly turned his attention to the inner workings of the colonial administration. He grew convinced that the civil service was riddled with bribery, nepotism, and collusion with local criminal elements.

## The Caldwell-Anstey Scandal

Anstey's suspicions crystallized into a direct confrontation with **Daniel Richard Caldwell** (高緝愛), the Registrar General and holder of the crucial post of Protector of Chinese. Anstey accused Caldwell of deep complicity with Chinese secret societies and pirates, notably the notorious pirate leader **Ma Chow-tong** (馬週棠). This triggered the **Caldwell-Anstey scandal**, a bitter institutional feud that paralyzed the Hong Kong government.

Historians and contemporary observers viewed the conflict through different lenses. British colonial officialdom largely regarded Anstey as a disruptive, insubordinate gadfly whose relentless accusations threatened administrative stability. Conversely, later scholarship and local perspectives often portrayed him as a rare, principled crusader challenging a corrupt colonial establishment that prioritized expediency and commercial stability over the rule of law.

## Dismissal and Departure

Anstey's public accusations and refusal to relent led Governor **Sir John Bowring** (寶寧) and later Governor **Sir Hercules Robinson** (羅便臣) into fierce administrative battles with him. By **1858**, the Colonial Office suspended Anstey, and he was eventually dismissed from his post. Although an official inquiry largely cleared Caldwell of some specific charges, the scandal severely damaged the reputation of the early Hong Kong administration and prompted closer scrutiny from London.
