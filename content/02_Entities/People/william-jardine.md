## Early Career and the Opium Trade
Born in **1784**, **William Jardine** (渣甸) initially served as a surgeon with the British East India Company before transitioning into private trade. In **1832**, alongside **James Matheson**, he founded the firm **Jardine, Matheson & Co.**, which became the largest British trading house in East Asia. The firm was a major player in the illicit opium trade, utilizing floating storage hulks at **Lintin Island** (伶仃島) to distribute the drug along the Chinese coast, effectively bypassing Qing administration restrictions.

## Influence on Conflict
**Jardine** was a vocal proponent of "gunboat diplomacy." Following the destruction of opium stocks by Commissioner **Lin Zexu** (林則徐) in **1839**, he traveled to London to lobby the British government for a forceful response. His efforts were instrumental in securing the naval expedition that led to the First Opium War (**1839–1842**). While some British colonial historians traditionally viewed his actions as a necessary push to modernize trade and end monopolies, later scholars and Beijing-aligned narratives frequently characterize his legacy as that of an imperialist architect who profited from the forced opening of China.

## Legacy in Hong Kong
**Jardine**'s influence on the territory's founding is inseparable from his commercial interests. He was the primary proponent for acquiring a permanent base for British merchants, eventually leading to the ceding of Hong Kong Island under the Treaty of Nanking. His company’s land purchases at **East Point** (東角) shaped the early development of the waterfront. Locally, the legacy of **Jardine, Matheson & Co.** persists through various landmarks and place names, reflecting the firm's deep integration into the city's economic history. The interpretation of his impact remains divided: some emphasize his role in Hong Kong's development as a global entrepôt, while others highlight the human and social costs of the opium trade he orchestrated.
