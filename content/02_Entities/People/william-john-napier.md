## Background and Appointment

**William John Napier** (律勞卑), **9th Lord Napier of Merchistoun**, was appointed in **1834** as the first Chief Superintendent of Trade in China following the British Parliament's abolition of the **East India Company**'s trade monopoly. Operating under the **Establishment of the Select Committee of Superintendents of Trade**, Napier was dispatched to Guangzhou (Canton) to manage British commercial interests and attempt direct official communication with Qing imperial officials.

## The Canton Mission and Confrontation

Upon arriving in Macau and subsequently traveling to Guangzhou in **July 1834**, Napier bypassed traditional merchant intermediaries (the **Co-hong**) and attempted to present a letter directly to the Viceroy of Guangzhou. This breach of Qing protocol—which restricted foreign diplomats to indirect communication through approved mercantile channels—triggered an immediate diplomatic crisis. The Qing authorities, viewing foreign envoys through a tributary framework, halted trade and ordered Napier to leave.

## Historiographical Interpretations

Interpretations of Napier's mission vary significantly across historiographical traditions:

*   **British Colonial and Traditional Accounts:** Often portrayed Napier as a martyr to free trade, hindered by an arrogant, anti-commercial Qing bureaucracy that refused to recognize sovereign equality or modern diplomatic norms.
*   **Qing and Mainland Chinese Scholarship:** View Napier's actions as a deliberate, aggressive violation of established imperial sovereignty and maritime regulations, interpreting the mission as an early precursor to imperialist aggression designed to undermine China's legal and administrative systems.
*   **Modern Scholarship:** Balanced analyses suggest that Napier was given an impossible mandate with inadequate naval and military backing, reflecting a fundamental misunderstanding by the British Foreign Office of Qing tributary protocols and domestic political pressures.

## Death and Legacy

Under intense pressure and facing a trade embargo, Napier withdrew from Guangzhou to Macau, where he fell ill with malaria and died on **October 11, 1834**. His brief and turbulent tenure exposed the unworkability of the old Canton System, directly paving the way for more coercive British policies that ultimately culminated in the acquisition of Hong Kong.
