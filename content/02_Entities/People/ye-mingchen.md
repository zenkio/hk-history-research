## Role in the Arrow Incident
**Ye Mingchen** (葉名琛) served as the Viceroy of Liangguang, holding authority over Guangdong and Guangxi provinces during the 1850s. He is best known in Hong Kong history for his role during the **Arrow Incident** of **1856**, in which Chinese officials boarded the lorcha *Arrow*, a vessel registered in Hong Kong, suspecting its crew of piracy and smuggling. 

## Differing Historical Interpretations
Historians offer varying perspectives on Ye's motivations and legacy. British colonial accounts often portrayed Ye as an arrogant, obstructive official whose refusal to apologize or return the crew led directly to the escalation of hostilities. Conversely, some mainland Chinese scholarship views Ye as a staunch patriot who resisted foreign encroachment and the illicit opium trade. Local Hong Kong historical narratives often emphasize the vulnerability of the young colony during this period, as Ye’s retaliatory policies, including the offer of bounties for European heads, led to heightened tension and sporadic violence within Hong Kong territory, most notably the **Esquire incident** in **1857**.

## Captivity and Death
Following the British capture of Canton in **1858**, **Ye Mingchen** was taken prisoner by British forces. He was transported to Calcutta, India, where he lived in confinement until his death in **1859**. While some reports claimed he died of hunger due to his refusal to eat foreign food, contemporary accounts suggest he suffered from illness. His tenure remains a subject of study regarding the collapse of traditional Qing diplomatic practices when confronted with Western gunboat diplomacy. His failure to de-escalate the **Arrow Incident** directly solidified the transition toward the unequal treaties that defined the mid-19th century in the Pearl River Delta.
