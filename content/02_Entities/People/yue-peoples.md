## Origins and Early Presence
The **Yue peoples** (越族), often referred to in classical texts as the **Baiyue** (百越 or 'Hundred Yue'), occupied the southeastern coastal territories including present-day Hong Kong. Archaeological evidence, such as the discoveries at **Sham Wan** (深灣) and **Sha Ha** (沙下), indicates that these communities practiced sophisticated maritime lifestyles. They are widely recognized for their mastery of boat building and navigation, which allowed them to thrive in the complex island geography of the Lingnan region.

## Historical Interpretations
Perspectives on the Yue vary significantly across historical narratives. **Qing dynasty** (清朝) and later **Beijing-centric** historiography often classified the Yue as 'barbarian' tribes eventually assimilated into the expanding Chinese empire during the **Qin** (秦) and **Han** (漢) dynasties. In contrast, **British colonial** historians often viewed the region as a largely empty 'backwater' prior to European arrival, minimizing the continuity of indigenous maritime cultures. 

**Modern scholarship** and local **New Territories** (新界) traditions offer a different view, emphasizing the distinct cultural and linguistic identity of the Yue. Some historians argue that the Yue maintained long-standing maritime trade networks that predated and influenced later Sinitic influence. It is uncertain to what degree the modern inhabitants of Hong Kong share direct genetic or cultural lineages with these ancient tribes, as historical records from the period are sparse and often filtered through the lens of external dynastic powers. However, the legacy of their seafaring expertise is frequently cited as the foundation of the region's long-term maritime orientation.
