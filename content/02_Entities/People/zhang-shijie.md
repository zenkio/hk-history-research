## Role in the Southern Song Flight

**Zhang Shijie** (張世傑) was a loyalist general who played a central role during the dramatic retreat of the Southern Song imperial court as it fled south to escape advancing Mongol forces. Alongside **Wen Tianxiang** (文天祥) and **Lu Xiufu** (陸秀夫), Zhang took charge of protecting the young emperors and the remnants of the court as they sought refuge in the coastal regions of Guangdong, including the area of modern-day Hong Kong.

Historical records and local lore associate Zhang with several pivotal events during the court's presence in the territory. Following the **Southern Song Imperial Court Flees to Kowloon**, the court briefly established a foothold, with traditions linking the **Establishment of Imperial Residence at Sacred Hill** (聖山) in modern-day Ma Tau Kok to the presence of the royals and their commanders like Zhang. The precarious existence of the court was further tested when a **Devastating Typhoon Strikes the Song Imperial Fleet**, scattering the ships and inflicting heavy hardships on the already beleaguered survivors.

Following these disasters, the court moved to **Mui Wo** (梅窩) on Lantau Island, where the tragic **Death of Emperor Duanzong at Mui Wo** occurred in **1278**. Zhang Shijie subsequently elevated Duanzong's younger brother, **Emperor Huaizong** (趙昺), to the throne, continuing the hopeless resistance until the ultimate defeat of the Song at the Battle of Yamen in **1279**, where Zhang reportedly drowned rather than surrender.

## Historiographical Interpretations

Interpretations of Zhang Shijie's legacy vary across different historical perspectives. Traditional imperial historiography, particularly promoted during the **Qing dynasty** (清朝) and later endorsed by mainland Chinese scholarship, lionized Zhang as the ultimate paragon of loyalty (*zhongyi*, 忠義) and steadfast resistance against foreign conquest. In contrast, local **Hong Kong and New Territories communities** often remembered the era through oral traditions, local temple dedications, and geographical markers rather than grand political narratives. Meanwhile, **British colonial** historians tended to treat the Southern Song episode as a minor, fleeting prelude to the region's later development, often categorizing the surviving accounts as semi-legendary due to the scarcity of contemporary written records physically anchored to the territory.
