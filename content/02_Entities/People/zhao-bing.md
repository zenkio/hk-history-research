## Historical Significance
**Zhao Bing** (1271–1279), also known as the **Emperor Bing of Song** (宋帝昺), was the last monarch of the Southern Song dynasty. His brief reign is inextricably linked to the final stages of the Mongol conquest, leading the imperial court to retreat into the territory of modern-day Hong Kong.

## The Retreat to Hong Kong
In **1278**, under the protection of officials **Lu Xiufu** (陸秀夫) and **Zhang Shijie** (張世傑), the court established a temporary capital, often associated with locations in present-day Kowloon or Lantau Island. Local traditions and oral histories in the New Territories, particularly among established clans, suggest the court resided at locations such as **Sung Wong Toi** (宋皇臺). While scholarly consensus confirms the court's presence in the region during the transition, the exact physical sites remain a subject of historical debate.

## Differing Perspectives
Interpretations of **Zhao Bing**'s presence vary across historical eras. Traditional historiography, particularly within local Cantonese communities, has long viewed the court's refuge as a noble final stand, leading to the veneration of the Emperor through sites like **Sung Wong Toi**. In contrast, some colonial-era accounts treated these sites as secondary to the wider narrative of Chinese imperial decay. Modern scholarship often focuses on the archaeological scarcity of the period, noting that while the retreat is a well-documented historical event, the material evidence identifying specific imperial campsites remains speculative.

## Death at the Battle of Yamen
In **1279**, the court left Hong Kong waters to engage the Mongol-Yuan navy at the **Battle of Yamen**. Following the catastrophic defeat of the Song forces, **Lu Xiufu** reportedly carried the seven-year-old **Zhao Bing** into the sea to commit suicide, marking the formal end of the Southern Song dynasty. This event is frequently commemorated in local heritage circles as a defining moment of loyalty and tragic history in the Lingnan region.
