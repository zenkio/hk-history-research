## Background and Southern Expansion

**Zhao Tuo** (趙佗) originated from the state of Zhao during the Warring States period. Following the Qin unification, he served as a military commander under **Qin Shi Huang** (秦始皇) during the Qin dynasty's southern expansion into the Lingnan region, which began around **219 BC**. Historical records indicate his involvement in subjugating the Baiyue peoples. During this campaign, administrative divisions that encompassed parts of modern-day Guangdong and Hong Kong were established, notably Panyu (番禺) and the broader Nanhai commandery.

## Foundation of Nanyue

As the Qin dynasty collapsed amid civil war in **204 BC**, Zhao Tuo capitalized on the turmoil to consolidate the commanderies of Nanhai, Guilin, and Xiang, proclaiming himself King of Nanyue (南越王). This established the independent Nanyue Kingdom (南越國), bridging Chinese administrative traditions with indigenous Yue cultures.

## Historiographical Perspectives and Hong Kong Connection

While direct archaeological evidence in Hong Kong specifically naming Zhao Tuo remains scarce, artifacts from the late Qin and Han periods found locally—such as salt production sites, coins, and pottery—reflect the integration of the territory into the Nanyue economic and political sphere.

Interpretations of Zhao Tuo's legacy vary across historiographical traditions:

* **Qing and Mainland Scholarship:** Traditionally viewed Zhao Tuo as a dutiful imperial official who later sinicized the southern frontier, helping to peacefully unify and civilize the Lingnan region under Chinese culture.
* **Local Hong Kong and Regional Studies:** Modern scholars often emphasize Nanyue as an independent maritime trading state that fostered a distinct regional identity separate from the Central Plains.
* **British Colonial Historiography:** Typically marginalized indigenous pre-British dynasties, focusing instead on Hong Kong's development as a modern treaty port after **1841**.
