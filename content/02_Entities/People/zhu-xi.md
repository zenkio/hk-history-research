## Introduction and Thought

**Zhu Xi** (**朱熹**, **1130**–**1200**) was a leading philosopher of the Song dynasty (**宋朝**) whose synthesis of Confucian thought, known as Neo-Confucianism, became the orthodox state ideology of later imperial China. Although Zhu himself never visited the region that is now Hong Kong (**香港**), his writings—particularly the *Family Rituals* (*Jiali* / **家禮**)—had a profound and lasting impact on local society through their implementation by migrating lineages.

## Expansion of Ancestral Hall Rituals

In the New Territories (**新界**), powerful clans such as the Tang (**鄧**) and Pang (**彭**) families adopted Zhu Xi's ritual prescriptions to organize their ancestral halls (**祠堂**) and lineage trusts. Modern scholarship and local historiography indicate that the promotion of these standardized ritual codes helped southern clans consolidate land ownership, regulate social behavior, and establish local hegemony during the late imperial period.

## Historiographical Interpretations

Interpretations of Zhu Xi's influence in the region vary across different perspectives:

* **Local New Territories Communities:** Indigenous villagers often view these ritual traditions as authentic markers of ancestral heritage, continuity, and lineage identity that predate British colonial administration.
* **British Colonial Administration:** Early British officials frequently interpreted these structured lineage practices through an administrative lens, sometimes viewing them as immutable customary law that needed to be accommodated to maintain rural stability.
* **Later Scholarship:** Contemporary historians emphasize that the adoption of Zhu Xi's rituals was not a static tradition, but a dynamic strategy used by local elites to project cultural sophistication and negotiate political power within the imperial and regional framework.
