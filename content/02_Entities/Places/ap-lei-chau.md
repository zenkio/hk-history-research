## Geography and Early Settlement

**Ap Lei Chau** (鴨脷洲) is an island located off the southwestern coast of **Hong Kong Island** (香港島), separated by the narrow **Aberdeen Channel** (香港仔海峽). The island's name derives from its shape, which resembles a duck's tongue (*ap lei*). Long before British colonial rule, the area was inhabited primarily by fishermen and seafarers who utilized the sheltered waters of the harbor.

## The Hung Shing Temple Connection

The most notable historical landmark on the island is the **Hung Shing Temple** (洪聖古廟), constructed in **1773** during the Qing dynasty. Local tradition holds that the temple was built by the fishing community to honor **Hung Shing** (洪聖), the God of the South Sea, for protecting them from typhoons and perils at sea. 

Interpretations of the temple's establishment reflect broader narratives of local Hong Kong and New Territories communities. Local oral histories emphasize grassroots maritime piety and community self-organization, whereas later historical scholarship often views such temple constructions as evidence of the expanding administrative and cultural reach of the Qing state into coastal southern China, alongside the consolidation of local gentry influence.

## Colonial Administration and Modern Development

Following the British occupation of **Hong Kong Island** in **1841** and the subsequent **Convention of Peking** in **1860**, which ceded the **Kowloon Peninsula** (九龍半島), **Ap Lei Chau** was formally incorporated into the British colony. While British colonial records primarily viewed the island through the lens of maritime administration, customs enforcement, and later industrial potential, local communities maintained their traditional religious practices, centered around the annual **Hung Shing Festival**.

In the latter half of the 20th century, **Ap Lei Chau** underwent rapid transformation from a quiet fishing and boat-building community into a densely populated residential and industrial zone, eventually linked to **Hong Kong Island** by bridges and later the MTR South Island Line.
