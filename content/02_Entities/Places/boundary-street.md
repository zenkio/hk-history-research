## Historical Demarcation

**Boundary Street** (**界限街**) is a major thoroughfare traversing the Kowloon peninsula from west to tail in **Kowloon**. Historically, the street marks the geographical line established following the **Signing of the Convention of Peking** in **1860**, which ceded the southern portion of the Kowloon Peninsula to Great Britain. 

## Interpretations of Significance

From the perspective of **British colonial** administration, the line served as a clear customs and administrative border before the acquisition of the New Territories. In contrast, **Qing and Beijing** official records viewed the boundary as a poignant symbol of territorial fragmentation resulting from unequal treaties. For **local Hong Kong and New Territories communities**, the boundary carried practical implications for trade, taxation, and migration between the urban core and the rural hinterland.

## Later Scholarship

**Later scholarship** emphasizes that although the physical demarcation lost its formal administrative function after the **Lease of the Kowloon Peninsula** under the **Signing of the Second Convention of Peking** in **1898** (which pushed the British border further north to the Sham Chun River), Boundary Street persisted as a powerful cultural and psychological divider within the urban landscape of Hong Kong, often invoked to describe socio-economic differences between older urban districts and subsequent developments.

## Timeline

- **1860**: The Convention of Peking cedes southern Kowloon, establishing the territorial basis for the future boundary line.
- **1898**: The Second Convention of Peking leases the New Territories, rendering Boundary Street an internal administrative divide rather than an international frontier.
