## Colonial Administration and Legal Framework

**Britain** (英國) established colonial rule over Hong Kong following the First Opium War, transforming the region into a strategic trading port and Crown Colony. The British administration introduced English common law alongside local ordinances, gradually extending administrative control from Hong Kong Island to the Kowloon Peninsula and the New Territories (新界).

## The Female Domestic Service Ordinance

In the 20th **century**, growing humanitarian pressures from British activists and local reformers compelled the colonial government in London and Hong Kong to address the traditional practice of *mui tsai* (妹仔)—young girls transferred into households as domestic servants. This culminated in the enactment of the **Female Domestic Service Ordinance** in **1923** (effective **1930**).

## Historiographical Interpretations

Interpretations of Britain's role in this legislative process vary significantly among historians:

*   **British Colonial Narrative:** Official histories often framed the legislation and subsequent registration processes as a progressive humanitarian triumph, demonstrating the Empire's commitment to eradicating forced labor and protecting vulnerable children.
*   **Local Hong Kong and New Territories Perspectives:** Conversely, many local communities and patriarchal elites viewed the colonial intervention as an intrusive misunderstanding of traditional Chinese kinship, adoption customs, and economic survival strategies.
*   **Later Scholarship:** Modern revisionist historians argue that while the British administration was pressured into action by metropolitan abolitionist groups, enforcement was often slow, cautious, and politically constrained by the desire to appease local elites and maintain social stability.
