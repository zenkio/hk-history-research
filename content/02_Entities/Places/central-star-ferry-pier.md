## History and Architecture

The third-generation **Central Star Ferry Pier** (中環天星碼頭), completed in **2005**, was designed in the Edinburgh Place (愛丁堡廣場) area as part of the Central and Wan Chai Reclamation. Designed in a modernist style, the structure featured a distinctive clock tower housing a bell cast by Gillett & Johnson, echoing its predecessor from **1957**. For decades, the Star Ferry (天星小輪) piers served as vital transportation nodes connecting Hong Kong Island and Kowloon, deeply embedding themselves into the daily routines and collective memory of residents.

## The 2006 Demolition and Controversy

In **November 2006**, the government proceeded with the demolition of the pier to make way for land reclamation and road infrastructure. This sparked intense public outcry and protests led by local activists, conservationists, and students, marking a watershed moment in Hong Kong's civil society.

Perspectives on the event varied significantly. **Local activists and community groups** viewed the pier as an irreplaceable repository of collective memory, arguing that the demolition exemplified a government-led development model that rode roughshod over heritage, public sentiment, and urban quality of life. Conversely, **government officials and pro-establishment figures** maintained that the reclamation and new infrastructure were essential for long-term economic growth, traffic alleviation, and infrastructural modernization.

## Historiographical Significance

Later scholarship often frames the defense of the Central Star Ferry Pier—and the subsequent protests surrounding the Queen's Pier (皇后碼頭) in **2007**—as the birth of a new localism and heightened heritage consciousness in Hong Kong. While colonial-era administrations had historically prioritized commercial development over preservation, the 2006 protests signaled a shift where grassroots communities actively contested official narratives of urban space, laying the groundwork for contemporary debates on heritage conservation and urban planning.
