## Origins and Early Settlement
**Chek Chu** (赤柱) was one of the largest and most established settlements on Hong Kong Island before the arrival of the British. Historical records from the **Qing Dynasty** suggest the area was primarily inhabited by fishing communities and farmers. The origin of the name remains a subject of interpretation; some local traditions attribute it to the presence of large red-flowered trees (kapok), while other scholars argue it derives from distinct geological features of the coastal cliffs.

## British Colonial Incorporation
Following the **1841** occupation of Hong Kong, the British colonial administration conducted the first census of the territory. The report, which included **Chek Chu**, identified a diverse population consisting of Chinese settlers, soldiers, and laborers. For early British administrators, the location was strategically significant for maritime oversight of the southern approaches to the island. During the mid-**19th century**, the colonial government initially considered establishing the center of government in the Stanley area before shifting focus to the northern side of the island around the area known as **Victoria City** (維多利亞城).

## Historical Significance and Later Development
Throughout the late **19th and early 20th centuries**, Stanley evolved from a remote coastal village into a site of distinct colonial infrastructure, including the construction of the **Stanley Prison** (赤柱監獄) in **1937**. During the **Japanese occupation of Hong Kong** (1941–1945), the area gained notoriety for the **Stanley Internment Camp**, where foreign civilians were detained under harsh conditions. Post-war scholarship often highlights the tension between the British colonial narrative of 'developing' a quiet outpost and the local perspective, which emphasizes the resilience and continuity of the indigenous village communities that predated the arrival of the colonial state. Today, Stanley remains a prominent site for historical study, representing the intersection of indigenous maritime culture and the transformative effects of colonial urban planning.
