## Introduction and Early History

Cheung Chau (長洲), meaning "long island" in Cantonese, is an dumbbell-shaped island located approximately 10 kilometers southwest of Hong Kong Island. Archaeological evidence, notably the prehistoric rock carvings discovered near the island's coastline, indicates human activity in the area dating back thousands of years. While the precise origins and creators of the **Creation of Sacred Coastal Rock Carvings** remain debated among archaeologists, scholars generally categorize them alongside other Bronze Age coastal carvings found throughout the region as expressions of early maritime culture.

## Maritime Trade and the Pirate Era

During the Qing dynasty, Cheung Chau developed as a fishing settlement and trading stop. Its geography made it a strategic stronghold for maritime outlaws. During the late **18th** and early **19th centuries**, the island served as a prominent base within the **Rise of Cheng I's Pirate Confederacy** led by the notorious pirate **Cheng I** (鄭一) and later his wife **Cheung Po Tsai** (張保仔), whose legendary cave remains a notable local landmark. Local folklore and historical memory often emphasize the autonomy of these coastal communities, whereas Qing dynastic records typically frame them through the lens of imperial pacification and suppression of piracy.

## Cultural Traditions and Colonial Administration

Following the British colonial annexation of Hong Kong Island and subsequently the New Territories, Cheung Chau was integrated into the administrative framework of the colony while retaining a high degree of local custom. The island is best known today for the **Origins of the Cheung Chau Jiao Festival** (長洲太平清醮), commonly known as the Bun Festival. While local oral tradition traces the festival back to a **18th-century** plague and subsequent rituals appeasing local deities, historians and anthropologists note that the modern manifestation evolved as a complex communal ritual negotiated between displaced fisherfolk, local merchants, and colonial authorities. British colonial officials initially viewed such large-scale traditional gatherings with administrative caution, whereas contemporary local communities and the Hong Kong government celebrate the festival as a cornerstone of intangible cultural heritage.

## Timeline

- **1810**: The pirate Cheung Po Tsai surrenders to Qing imperial forces, ending the peak of the confederacy's presence around Cheung Chau.
- **1899**: Following the Convention for the Extension of Hong Kong Territory, Cheung Chau comes under British colonial administration.
- **1930s**: Cheung Chau experiences growth as a retreat and residential area for mainland and local urban migrants.
