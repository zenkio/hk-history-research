## Imperial Era and Early Encounters

Historically, the territory comprising modern Hong Kong was integrated into the Chinese imperial administration during the Qin dynasty (**221–206 BCE**). For centuries, the region functioned primarily through salt production, fishing, and agriculture, falling under the jurisdiction of Xin'an County (新安) in Guangdong province (廣東). European contact began in the 16th century, notably marked by **Jorge Álvares** landing at Tuen Mun (屯門) in **1513**, initiating maritime trade and conflicts between the Ming dynasty (明朝) and Portuguese traders.

## Colonial Separation and Sovereignty

Following the First Opium War, the Qing dynasty (清朝) ceded Hong Kong Island to Great Britain via the Treaty of Nanking in **1842**, a move formalized under British colonial rule. Subsequent cessions added the Kowloon Peninsula in **1860** and the New Territories (新界) via a 99-year lease in **1898**. British colonial historiography often emphasized the transformation of a 'barren rock' into a global trade entrepôt, whereas mainland Chinese and later critical scholarship viewed these treaties as unequal agreements resulting from imperialist aggression.

## Geopolitical Shifts and the Cold War

Following the establishment of the People's Republic of China (中華人民共和國) in **1949**, Hong Kong experienced profound demographic and economic transformations. The **1951** United Nations embargo on China—imposed during the Korean War—severed Hong Kong's traditional entrepôt trade with the mainland, compelling local industrialists and refugees to pivot toward manufacturing. This shift laid the foundation for Hong Kong's post-war economic boom. Concurrently, preparations for regional security became vital, as seen in the **1939** enactment of Air Raid Precautions ahead of World War II.

## Interpretations of History

Interpretations of China-Hong Kong relations remain multifaceted. While Beijing traditionally emphasizes historical irredentism and the eventual recovery of sovereignty culminating in the **1997** handover, local Hong Kong and New Territories communities often highlight a distinct regional identity forged through decades of relative separation, varying socio-political systems, and unique migration histories.
