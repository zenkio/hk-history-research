## Origins and Development

**Choi Hung Estate** (彩虹邨), located in **Wong Tai Sin** (黃大仙), was developed by the **Hong Kong Housing Authority** (香港房屋委員會) to address the acute housing shortage following the massive influx of refugees in the 1950s. Construction began in the late 1950s and was completed in **1962**–**1964**. The estate was designed as a "model" housing project, intended to provide modern living conditions for low-income residents, replacing the informal squatter settlements that were common across Kowloon at the time.

## Architectural and Social Significance

Architecturally, the estate is known for its distinctive rainbow-colored exterior, which gave the estate its name, *Choi Hung* (meaning "rainbow"). It received the **Silver Medal** from the **Hong Kong Institute of Architects** in **1965**. Sociologically, the estate is often analyzed as a centerpiece of the colonial government's effort to stabilize society through public housing. While some historians view this as a progressive advancement in social welfare and public health, others—drawing from local oral histories—emphasize the disruption caused to indigenous farming communities previously occupying the land, and the challenges of adapting to high-density living in colonial-managed blocks.

## Historical Connection

**Choi Hung Estate** holds a notable place in the history of British-Hong Kong relations. During the **1975** visit of **Queen Elizabeth II** to Hong Kong, she toured the estate and visited a resident's home. This event is frequently cited in historical analysis; to some, it represents a moment of successful colonial public diplomacy and the government's perceived commitment to citizen welfare. Conversely, later scholarship often critiques this moment as a carefully staged performance of colonial paternalism, designed to solidify loyalty during a period of transition in the late colonial era. Today, the estate remains a highly recognizable symbol of mid-century Hong Kong architecture and a focal point for debates regarding urban conservation versus modernization.
