## Historical Significance

**Chuenpee** (穿鼻), located near the Bogue (Humen 虎門) estuary in Guangdong, was a strategic defensive point for the Qing Empire and a key theater of operations during the early stages of the First Opium War. Its primary connection to Hong Kong's foundational history stems from the events of **early 1841**, when British naval and military forces pressured Qing imperial commissioner **Qishan** (琦善) in the area.

## The Convention of Chuenpee

Following British military successes against the fortifications at Chuenpee and neighboring Taikoktsui on **January 7, 1841**, negotiations led to the drafting of the **Convention of Chuenpee** (穿鼻草約) on **January 20, 1841**. Under the terms negotiated by **Captain Charles Elliot** and Qishan, the island of Hong Kong was to be ceded to the British Crown, alongside an indemnity and the opening of Canton to trade. 

## Interpretations

Historiographical views on the agreements and events at Chuenpee vary:

*   **British Colonial Perspective:** Traditionally framed the Convention of Chuenpee as a legitimate, albeit provisional, diplomatic agreement that justified the subsequent landing and occupation of Hong Kong on **January 26, 1841**, and the issuance of Captain Charles Elliot's proclamation to the inhabitants.
*   **Qing / Beijing Perspective:** Viewed the unauthorized concessions made by Qishan as acts of capitulation under duress. The Daoguang Emperor subsequently repudiated the convention, dismissed Qishan for treasonous concessions, and ordered the continuation of hostilities.
*   **Later Scholarship:** Modern historians often emphasize that the Convention of Chuenpee was never formally ratified by either sovereign government—neither Queen Victoria nor the Daoguang Emperor approved it—making it a stepping stone rather than the final legal instrument of cession, which was later formally settled by the Treaty of Nanking in 1842.
