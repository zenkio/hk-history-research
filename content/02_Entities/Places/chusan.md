## Strategic Significance
Chusan (定海), an island archipelago located off the coast of Zhejiang province, held significant strategic importance for the British Empire during the **1840s**. Following the outbreak of the First Opium War, British naval forces captured Chusan in **July 1840**. 

## British and Qing Perspectives
For the British colonial administration, Chusan represented a potential site for a permanent trading post and naval station. However, historical records indicate that the British military faced significant logistical challenges, including a high mortality rate among troops due to disease, which led some officials to reconsider its suitability compared to the more southern **Hong Kong (香港)**. From the Qing Dynasty (大清) perspective, the occupation of Chusan was viewed as an illegal encroachment and a violation of sovereignty, frequently serving as a bargaining chip in treaty negotiations.

## Connection to Hong Kong
When the plenipotentiary **Sir Henry Pottinger** arrived in the region, the debate over whether to retain Chusan or develop Hong Kong remained an active point of contention within the British government. While some military leaders argued for the central location of Chusan, the British eventually prioritized the deep-water harbor of Hong Kong. Many historians suggest that the eventual return of Chusan to Qing control under the **Treaty of Nanking (南京條約)** in **1842** solidified the focus of the British colonial project onto Hong Kong Island. Modern scholarship often notes that the shift from Chusan to Hong Kong was driven by a combination of sanitary concerns, geographic suitability for trade, and the relative distance from the center of Qing administrative power in Beijing.
