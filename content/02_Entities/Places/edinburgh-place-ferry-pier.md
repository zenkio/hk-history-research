## Architectural Significance
The **Edinburgh Place Ferry Pier** (愛丁堡廣場碼頭), opened in **1957**, was a quintessential example of **Streamline Moderne** architecture. Designed by **Eric Cumine**, the pier featured a clock tower housing a four-faced **Dent & Co.** clock, which had been moved from the previous **Pedder Street** clock tower. For decades, the pier served as the primary gateway for cross-harbour commuters traveling between Central and Tsim Sha Tsui.

## Heritage and Contestation
The pier's demolition in **2006** to make way for land reclamation projects sparked significant public debate. Pro-democracy activists, local historians, and community members organized protests, arguing that the pier represented the collective memory of **Hong Kong** and the city's unique colonial-modernist identity. Conversely, the **Hong Kong Government** maintained that the demolition was a necessary component of the **Central Reclamation Phase III** project, which was designed to improve infrastructure and alleviate traffic congestion in the rapidly modernizing financial district.

## Differing Perspectives
Interpretations of the site's value vary significantly. Many local residents viewed the pier and its iconic clock tower as a symbol of the city's post-war identity, distinct from either British colonial administration or mainland Chinese governance. British colonial historians have often cited the pier as a testament to the architectural and infrastructural development fostered during the mid-twentieth century. Meanwhile, later scholars suggest the protest movement surrounding the pier marked a watershed moment in the development of a distinct **Hong Kong** civic identity, signaling a shift in how residents engaged with urban planning and heritage preservation. While the physical structure is gone, the discourse surrounding the site remains a central case study in the struggle to define **Hong Kong's** heritage amid ongoing urban development.
