## The Incident of 1857

The **Esing Bakery** (裕盛辦餅店) was a prominent bakery located in Queen’s Road, Central, owned by a Chinese baker named **Cheong Ah-lum** (張亞林). It is primarily remembered for a mass poisoning incident on **January 15, 1857**, during the Second Opium War. Hundreds of European residents, including the wife of the colony's Attorney General, fell ill after consuming bread laced with arsenic.

## Perspectives on Responsibility

Interpretations of the event remain divided. British colonial authorities at the time asserted that the poisoning was a deliberate act of sabotage orchestrated by Chinese officials or secret societies to undermine the British presence in Hong Kong. This narrative led to the arrest and trial of **Cheong Ah-lum** and several other bakery staff. Despite the lack of definitive evidence, colonial officials used the event to justify heightened suspicion toward the Chinese merchant class.

Conversely, other historical viewpoints, including later scholarship, suggest alternative possibilities. Some historians propose that the contamination may have been accidental, potentially due to the misuse of chemicals in the baking process or a misunderstanding of local sanitation practices. Others argue that if the act was intentional, it may have been a localized protest by individuals within the Chinese community rather than a coordinated state-sponsored effort by the Qing government. **Cheong Ah-lum** was eventually acquitted of the charges due to insufficient evidence, though he was subsequently deported from Hong Kong. The incident serves as a significant case study in the atmosphere of deep distrust that characterized early colonial Hong Kong, highlighting the stark disconnect between the ruling British administration and the local Chinese population during a period of geopolitical volatility.
