## Strategic Significance
**Fat Tong Mun** (佛堂門), also known as the Joss House Bay entrance, has served as a vital maritime passage in Hong Kong since the **Song dynasty** (960–1279). Its location at the southeastern approaches to the Victoria Harbour area made it a critical juncture for vessels navigating the Pearl River Delta. The area is most noted for the **Fat Tong Mun Rock Inscription** (佛堂門石刻), which dates back to **1274**, documenting the repair of a temple dedicated to **Tin Hau** (天后), highlighting the early importance of the site to maritime communities.

## Coastal Defense and Interpretations
During the **Qing dynasty** (1644–1912), the strategic importance of the channel led to the **Construction of the Fat Tong Mun Coastal Watch**. Interpretations of this site vary depending on the historical lens applied:

* **Qing and Imperial Authorities:** The site was viewed as a necessary defensive perimeter to monitor piracy and smuggling in the southeastern approaches to the Canton maritime province.
* **Local New Territories Communities:** The coastal watch stations were often viewed by residents as intrusive impositions that regulated traditional fishing routes and local coastal commerce.
* **British Colonial Era:** After the **1898** Convention of Peking, British surveyors categorized the site as part of the strategic geography of the New Territories. Later scholarship often emphasizes how these fortifications marked the transition from imperial maritime surveillance to colonial territorial management.

While the physical remains of the **Fat Tong Mun** fortifications are minimal, the site remains a subject of archaeological interest regarding the evolution of maritime security. It remains uncertain to what extent these outposts successfully suppressed local piracy, as historical accounts from colonial records often differ significantly from local oral traditions regarding the efficacy and purpose of the coastal watches.
