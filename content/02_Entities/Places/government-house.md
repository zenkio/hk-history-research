## Architectural History and Colonial Administration

Completed in **1855**, Government House (港督府) was designed by Government Surveyor **Reginald Eustace Morley** in a neoclassical style, with subsequent expansions—most notably the ballroom added during the Japanese occupation—altering its original appearance. For over a century, it stood as the physical embodiment of British colonial rule in Hong Kong, hosting major state functions and serving as the center of executive power.

## Wartime Occupation and the Japanese Surrender

During the Japanese Occupation of Hong Kong (**1941**–**1945**), the building was occupied by the Japanese military administration, during which structural alterations were made. Following the Liberation of Hong Kong from Japanese Occupation in **August 1945**, Government House regained its status as the center of government. Interpretations of this transitional period often vary: British colonial accounts emphasized the restoration of democratic governance and the rule of law, whereas later critical scholarship and local perspectives sometimes contextualized the immediate postwar period as a continuation of unyielding colonial autocracy that delayed genuine local political representation.

## The 1967 Leftist Riots

During the **1967** Leftist Riots, Government House became a focal point of civil unrest and confrontation. Pro-communist demonstrators staged protests outside its gates, reflecting the heightened geopolitical tensions of the era driven by the Cultural Revolution in mainland China. Perspectives on these events differ significantly: Beijing-aligned and leftist narratives historically viewed the protests as a patriotic anti-colonial movement against British repression, while mainstream Hong Kong memory and contemporary historians largely characterize the riots as disruptive and dangerous civil unrest driven by external radicalism that threatened local stability.
