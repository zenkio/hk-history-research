## Establishment and Tuberculosis Crisis

**Grantham Hospital** (**葛量洪醫院**), located in **Wong Chuk Hang** (**黃竹坑**) on Hong Kong Island, was established to address the devastating tuberculosis (TB) epidemic that overwhelmed the territory following the Second World War. Named after the then-Governor of Hong Kong, **Sir Alexander Grantham** (**葛量洪**), the hospital officially opened on **January 6, 1957**. 

During the post-war decades, rapid population growth, overcrowding, and impoverished living conditions caused TB to become one of the leading causes of mortality in Hong Kong. The establishment of Grantham Hospital marked a crucial milestone in the colonial government's public health response, shifting from basic isolation to specialized institutional care.

## Historiographical Perspectives

Interpretations of the hospital's establishment and the broader anti-tuberculosis campaign vary among historians and communities:

* **British Colonial Narrative:** Official histories often emphasize the initiative as a humanitarian triumph of colonial administration and modern medical intervention, highlighting the successful collaboration between the government and organizations like the **Hong Kong Discharged Prisoners' Aid Society** and the **Hong Kong Anti-Tuberculosis Association** (**香港防癆勞動心臟協會**).
* **Local and Subaltern Perspectives:** Community memory and later scholarship sometimes frame the post-war healthcare expansion as a belated reaction to systemic neglect of the working-class population. Critics and local historians note that severe housing shortages and poor sanitation in squatter areas—exacerbated by colonial zoning and economic policies—were the root causes of the epidemic that institutions like Grantham Hospital were left to manage.
* **Medical Modernization:** Scholars of public health also view the hospital as a key site where Western biomedical practices, including early thoracic surgery and pharmacological treatments, were systematically deployed in a non-Western colonial setting.
