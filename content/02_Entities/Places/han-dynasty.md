## Administrative Integration
During the **Han Dynasty** (**漢代**), specifically following the expansion of the **Qin Dynasty**, the territory comprising modern-day Hong Kong was placed under the jurisdiction of **Nanhai Commandery** (**南海郡**). Historical records suggest that this region, then part of the **Panyu** (**番禺**) county, served as a peripheral area of the imperial frontier. 

## Archaeological Evidence
Archaeological excavations, most notably at **Lei Cheng Uk Han Tomb** (**李鄭屋漢墓**) in **Sham Shui Po** (**深水埗**), provide the most concrete evidence of Han influence. The tomb's construction style, including its vaulted ceiling and brickwork, suggests it dates to the **Eastern Han** (**東漢**) period. Scholars debate the social status of the occupant; some interpret the find as evidence of a burgeoning colonial administration, while others view it as proof of local elites adopting northern burial customs. 

## Economic and Social Life
Evidence of iron tools unearthed in the region suggests an introduction of iron agriculture (**鐵器農業**), which would have significantly enhanced local production capabilities. Interpretations of the region's historical significance vary: traditional **Qing** (**清**) era gazetteers often emphasized the spread of Confucian civilization into the south, while contemporary scholarship often focuses on the region as a maritime trade node connecting the **Pearl River Delta** (**珠江三角洲**) with wider **Southeast Asian** networks. Local communities in the **New Territories** (**新界**) often view these sites as ancestral heritage, emphasizing the long-standing continuity of human settlement. The exact nature of Han governance in this remote coastal area remains uncertain, as the region was likely sparsely populated and served primarily as a transit point for salt production and maritime trade rather than a core agricultural hub.

## Timeline

- **206 BC**: Establishment of the Han Dynasty.
- **111 BC**: The Han Empire formally asserts control over the Nanhai Commandery.
- **25 AD**: Beginning of the Eastern Han period, the likely era of the Lei Cheng Uk tomb.
