## Overview and Imperial Capital

Hangzhou (杭州), known historically as Lin'an (臨安), served as the capital of the Southern Song dynasty from **1138** to **1276**. As the economic and cultural heart of southern China, the city presided over a maritime empire deeply connected to trade routes stretching across the South China Sea. 

## Connection to Hong Kong's History

Hangzhou's direct connection to Hong Kong's historical narrative stems from the collapse of the Southern Song under the relentless advance of the Mongol Empire. In **1276**, Mongol forces captured Hangzhou, seizing the imperial family. However, loyalist officials managed to smuggle young imperial princes out of the capital. 

This desperate retreat forced the remnants of the Southern Song imperial court to flee southward by sea, eventually reaching the territory of modern-day Hong Kong. The court sought temporary refuge in locations such as Kowloon and Mui Wo on Lantau Island, turning the region into the final, fleeting stronghold of the dynasty before its ultimate extinction at the Battle of Yamen in **1279**.

## Historiographical Perspectives

Interpretations of this flight from Hangzhou vary across different traditions. Nationalist historiography, spanning both Qing dynasty and modern Beijing perspectives, generally portrays the court's desperate migration as a tragic heroic stand of loyal resistance against foreign conquest. Conversely, local Hong Kong and New Territories indigenous historiography often focuses on the tangible legacy left by these refugees, emphasizing how the presence of the imperial court and fleeing scholars shaped local oral traditions, placenames, and lineage origin myths. Later scholarship tends to view the retreat not as a planned strategic withdrawal, but as a chaotic, panicked diaspora driven by military desperation.
