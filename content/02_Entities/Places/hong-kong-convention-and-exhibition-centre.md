## Construction and Significance
The **Hong Kong Convention and Exhibition Centre** (HKCEC), often referred to as the **Convention Centre** (會展), was constructed in phases on reclaimed land in **Wan Chai** (灣仔). The first phase was completed in **1988**, followed by the iconic expansion for the second phase, which opened in **1997**. The building is architecturally notable for its large-scale glass curtain walls and its curved roof, intended to resemble a bird in flight. It has served as the primary venue for many of the territory's most significant political and commercial milestones, including the **1997 handover ceremony** marking the transfer of sovereignty from Britain to China.

## Role in Local History
Beyond its commercial function, the site has been a focal point for political expression. Most notably, the centre served as the primary venue for the **2005 WTO Ministerial Conference**. During this event, thousands of protesters, including international activists and local groups, gathered in **Wan Chai** to demonstrate against global trade policies. Perspectives on the protests vary: local authorities and some business sectors emphasized the disruption to order and the safety challenges posed to the diplomatic event, while activists and local community observers often frame the demonstrations as a significant moment of civil society engagement in Hong Kong, highlighting the tension between the city's role as a global platform and the internal dissent of its residents.

## Perspectives on Development
Historical analysis of the **HKCEC** often sits at the intersection of colonial planning and post-1997 integration. British colonial planners viewed the centre as a necessary upgrade to Hong Kong’s infrastructure to cement its status as an international business gateway. Later scholarly interpretations suggest that the building functions as a site of 'monumental architecture' designed to project stability and prosperity. Whether interpreted as a neutral space for global trade or a stage for official state narratives, the centre remains an essential component of the Victoria Harbour skyline and a barometer for Hong Kong’s socio-political environment.
