## Early Settlement and Cession
Prior to formal British occupation, Hong Kong Island (香港島) supported a mixed population of fishermen, agriculturalists, salt producers, and Hakka villagers distributed across various coastal settlements and stone-quarrying sites. Following the unauthorized **Convention of Chuenpi** in **January 1841**, the British Expeditionary Fleet gathered in the local waters, and **Formal British Possession of Hong Kong Island** was declared on **January 26, 1841**, when **Captain Charles Elliot** claimed the territory for Queen Victoria.

## Establishment of Colonial Administration
British authorities quickly moved to establish institutional control. **Elliot** issued a proclamation guaranteeing local Chinese residents the continuation of their customary laws and practices, provided they did not conflict with British jurisprudence—a foundational compromise in colonial governance. To secure revenue and organize the nascent colony, the administration conducted **Hong Kong's First Colonial Census** in **1841**, which recorded a diverse population of several thousand inhabitants. Shortly thereafter, the **First Public Land Auction in Hong Kong** took place on **June 14, 1841**, establishing property rights that laid the groundwork for the island's future real estate market.

## Historiographical Interpretations
Perspectives on this foundational period vary significantly across traditions. British colonial historiography traditionally emphasized the island as a barren rock transformed through free-market capitalism and British rule into a modern metropolis. Conversely, mainland Chinese scholarship often frames the cession under the **Signing of the Treaty of Nanking** in **1842** as a humiliating symbol of foreign imperialism and the unequal treaty system. Meanwhile, local Hong Kong social histories increasingly highlight the agency of the indigenous inhabitants and Chinese merchants, whose commercial networks and social structures were vital to the island's survival and growth.
