## Geological and Prehistoric Foundations

Long before written records, the geography of Hong Kong underwent profound transformations. **Rising Sea Levels Form Hong Kong's Coastline** shaped the archipelago and indented peninsulas that would later define local maritime activity. Early human presence is evidenced by archaeological finds such as the **Introduction of Coarse Cord-Marked Pottery**, pointing to early Neolithic communities engaging with the marine environment.

## Maritime Adaptations and Social Complexity

Over subsequent millennia, coastal dwellers experienced a **Rise of Specialized Coastal Maritime Culture**, relying heavily on fishing and coastal trade. This eventually led to a **Transition to Sedentary Coastal Villages**, marking a shift from mobile foraging to more permanent settlements along the sheltered bays of the territory.

As populations grew and interacted with broader southern Chinese networks, the region saw the **Introduction of Bronze Metallurgy**. Concurrently, communities left enduring cultural markers through the **Creation of Sacred Coastal Rock Carvings**, which local and academic interpreters view variously as ritual boundaries, ancestral totems, or maritime protections.

## Tribal Societies and Alliances

By the later prehistoric and early historic periods, archaeological and historical interpretations—though often debated between colonial-era researchers, mainland Chinese historians, and indigenous New Territories narratives—suggest the **Rise of Hierarchical Tribal Societies**. Faced with external pressures and regional integration, these groups participated in a **Defensive Tribal Alliances Form**, reflecting complex social organization prior to formal imperial incorporation during the Qin and Han dynasties.
