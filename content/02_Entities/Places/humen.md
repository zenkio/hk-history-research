## Introduction and Strategic Importance
Humen (虎門), historically known in English as the Bocca Tigris, is a narrow strait in the Pearl River (珠江) delta connecting the river's upper reaches to the South China Sea. Because it guards the maritime approaches to Guangzhou (廣州)—then the sole legal port for foreign trade—the Qing dynasty (清朝) constructed heavy coastal fortifications along its shores.

## The Opium Destruction
In **1839**, Imperial Commissioner **Lin Zexu** (林則徐) arrived in Guangdong to suppress the illicit opium trade driven primarily by British merchants. After compelling foreign traders to surrender their stocks, Lin ordered the public destruction of over 20,000 chests of confiscated opium at Humen over a period of several weeks. This event directly catalyzed the First Opium War (**1839**–**1842**).

## Interpretations and Historiography
Interpretations of the events at Humen diverge significantly across historiographical traditions:

* **Qing and Mainland Chinese Scholarship:** Traditional and modern Chinese historiography views Humen as a heroic site of anti-imperialist resistance against foreign capitalist aggression, framing **Lin Zexu** as a patriotic defender of national sovereignty.
* **British Colonial Perspective:** 19th-century British accounts frequently characterized the seizure of property and subsequent blockade at Humen as an infringement on free trade and an affront to British subjects, justifying military retaliation.
* **Local Hong Kong Context:** For the communities of Hong Kong and the New Territories, the closure of trade and the military defeat at Humen fundamentally altered regional geopolitics, leading directly to the cession of Hong Kong Island and the establishment of British colonial rule that would shape the region for over a century.
