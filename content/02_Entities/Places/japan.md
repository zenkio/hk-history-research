## Wartime Requisition of Bronze Statues

During the occupation of Hong Kong (**1941**–**1945**), the Japanese military administration targeted prominent British colonial symbols for destruction to meet severe wartime metal shortages. Among the most notable losses were the bronze statue of **Queen Victoria** located in Statue Square and the statue of **Sir Thomas Jackson**, chief manager of HSBC. These metal objects were dismantled and shipped to **Japan** (日本) to be melted down for industrial and military use.

## Historiographical Perspectives

Interpretations of these actions vary among historians and political perspectives:

* **British Colonial Narrative:** Traditional British accounts emphasize the destruction as an act of cultural vandalism and an attempt by the occupiers to erase British heritage and symbols of sovereignty from the urban landscape.
* **Japanese Wartime Policy:** Japanese records and wartime logistics perspectives frame the removal purely as a pragmatic economic measure to source critical raw materials for industrial production rather than an ideological campaign.
* **Local Hong Kong and Mainland Perspectives:** Later local scholarship and post-war memory often view the episode through the broader lens of wartime suffering, noting that the requisition extended beyond British monuments to include various public fittings and private metals across the territory.

## Post-War Recovery and Legacy

Following the Japanese surrender in **1945**, the colonial government attempted to trace the fate of the requisitioned statues. While some fragments were eventually recovered and returned—notably the statue of **Queen Victoria**, which was later relocated to **Victoria Park** (維多利亞公園)—many other metal artifacts were permanently lost or destroyed in the industrial heartlands of **Japan** before the end of the conflict.

- **1941**: Japanese forces occupy Hong Kong and begin identifying metal assets.
- **1942**: Bronze royal and commercial statues are dismantled and shipped to Japan for scrap.
- **1945**: Surrender of Japan; post-war authorities begin assessing the loss of public monuments.
