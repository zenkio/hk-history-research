## Historical Development
Jordan (佐敦), originally known as Kwun Chung (官涌), developed rapidly during the colonial period due to its proximity to the Kowloon peninsula's waterfront. The area was named after **Sir John Jordan (朱爾典)**, a British diplomat who served in China, reflecting the naming conventions often employed by the colonial administration to cement British presence. Throughout the early 20th century, the district evolved from a rural coastal area into a major residential and commercial center, significantly bolstered by the expansion of the Kowloon-Canton Railway (九廣鐵路).

## Social Unrest and Identity
Jordan serves as a critical site in the study of Hong Kong’s social history. During the **1966 Star Ferry Riots (天星小輪加價事件)**, protests that began in Central regarding ferry fare increases quickly spread to Kowloon. Jordan became a flashpoint for this unrest. Historians note that while colonial authorities often framed these events as opportunistic rioting, later scholarly perspectives—and testimonies from local residents—frequently interpret the actions as a genuine manifestation of deep-seated frustrations regarding colonial governance, economic inequality, and a lack of political representation for the local Chinese population. These differing interpretations highlight the complex relationship between the colonial government and the grassroots community during the transition toward modern Hong Kong identity.

## Modern Context
Today, Jordan is characterized by a dense mix of traditional tong lau (唐樓) architecture and modern high-rise developments. It remains a central node in the city's transport network. While the colonial-era architecture is gradually being replaced by urban redevelopment projects, the area retains significant cultural markers of its past. The interplay between historical commercial growth and the legacy of mid-century social mobilization continues to define the district's character within the broader context of Hong Kong’s evolution.
