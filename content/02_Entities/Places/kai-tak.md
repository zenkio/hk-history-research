## Origins and Early Development

Kai Tak originated from a land reclamation and real estate venture initiated by businessmen **Ho Kai** and **Au Tak** in the early 20th century. When the real estate project failed, the land was utilized by aviation pioneers and eventually acquired by the British colonial government. In **1925**, it officially opened as an aerodrome. Throughout the 1930s, the facility underwent development to accommodate flying boats and landplanes, aligning with British imperial defense strategies and commercial expansion in East Asia, though local New Territories communities and Qing/Beijing perspectives often viewed these developments through the lens of encroaching colonial infrastructure.

## Wartime Destruction and Military Use

During the Japanese occupation (1941–1945), Kai Tak was significantly expanded using forced labor, incorporating materials from demolished local landmarks such as the **Sung Wong Toi** (宋王臺) monument. The airfield became a primary target for **Allied Air Raids on Hong Kong Operations** by the United States Fourteenth Air Force, resulting in severe damage to runways and Japanese aircraft.

## Post-War Expansion and Relocation

Following the war, as detailed in accounts of Kai Tak Expands into Commercial Airfield, the site underwent massive reconstruction to meet the post-war aviation boom. Its single runway jutting into **Kowloon Bay** (九龍灣) became famous worldwide for the dramatic, low-altitude approaches pilots had to execute over densely populated urban areas.

By the late 20th century, capacity constraints and noise pollution led to the controversial decision to relocate the airport. The closure of Kai Tak in **1998** and the transfer of operations to **Chek Lap Kok** (赤鱲角) was a central component of the broader **Rose Garden Project Announcement**, fundamentally altering the urban geography of Kowloon and opening vast tracts of land for long-term redevelopment.
