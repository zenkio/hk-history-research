## Geographic and Strategic Importance

The Kowloon Peninsula (九龍半島) forms the northern shore of Victoria Harbour, directly facing Hong Kong Island (香港島). Its proximity to the island made its acquisition a strategic priority for British colonial authorities seeking to secure the harbor and protect their military and commercial interests.

## The Convention of Peking and British Annexation

Following the Second Opium War, the British Empire expanded its foothold in the region. Through the **Convention of Peking** (**北京條約**) signed in **1860**, the Qing dynasty (清朝) ceded the southern portion of the Kowloon Peninsula up to Boundary Street (界限街), along with Stonecutters Island (昂船洲), to Great Britain "in perpetuity."

Historical interpretations of this cession differ significantly. British colonial historiography traditionally framed the acquisition as a necessary defensive measure to safeguard Victoria Harbour against piracy and foreign encroachment. Conversely, mainland Chinese scholarship and Qing-era perspectives view the Convention of Peking as an unequal treaty forced upon a weakened empire under military duress, symbolizing the 'century of humiliation.'

## Subsequent Expansion and the Lease of 1898

The peninsula's administrative history shifted further with the **Convention for the Extension of Hong Kong Territory** in **1898**, which leased the area north of Boundary Street—known as the New Territories (新界)—to Britain for 99 years. Local communities and indigenous clans in the New Territories initially resisted the British takeover in 1899, reflecting complex local dynamics that complicated the neat narrative of smooth colonial administration.

## Legacy

The handover of sovereignty over Hong Kong in **1997** eventually extinguished the perpetual lease of the southern peninsula alongside the 99-year lease of the New Territories, ending over a century of British governance.

- **1898**: The Second Convention of Peking leases the northern Kowloon Peninsula and New Territories for 99 years.
