## Historical Significance
Originally a Qing Dynasty (清朝) military outpost, the site of the **Kowloon Walled City (九龍城寨)** evolved into a dense, unregulated urban settlement following the **1898** Convention for the Extension of Hong Kong Territory. While the British colonial administration excluded the enclave from the **1898** lease, interpretations of its sovereignty remained contested. The British viewed it as a pocket of Qing authority, whereas the Qing government—and later the Republic of China (中華民國) and the People’s Republic of China (中華人民共和國)—maintained claims of jurisdiction, often resulting in a power vacuum.

## The Enclave Years
Throughout the mid-20th century, the area became famous for its high-density architecture and lack of government services. Residents, often originating from local Hong Kong or mainland migrant communities, developed an independent internal governance structure. For many residents, the Walled City provided affordable housing and a sense of community, while colonial authorities periodically faced challenges regarding policing and public health. This duality remains a subject of debate among historians, with some focusing on the resilience of the residents versus others highlighting the dangers posed by the lack of structural and fire safety regulations.

## Demolition and Transformation
In **1987**, the British and Chinese governments reached a formal agreement for the clearance of the site. Following the relocation of thousands of residents between **1991** and **1992**, the structures were demolished. The **Kowloon Walled City Park (九龍城寨公園)** was officially opened in **1995**. The design of the park, utilizing the Jiangnan garden style, incorporates salvaged artifacts from the original site, including the remnants of the South Gate. Today, the park serves as a green space and a site of historical memory, reflecting the complex intersection of colonial history, sovereign dispute, and the legacy of Hong Kong’s urban evolution.
