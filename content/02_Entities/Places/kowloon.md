## Introduction and Early History

Kowloon (**九龍**) forms a major part of the urban landscape of Hong Kong, situated immediately north of Hong Kong Island across Victoria Harbour. Long before formal British annexation, the peninsula and its surrounding areas supported diverse fishing and agricultural communities. Archaeological evidence, most notably the **Lei Cheng Uk Han Tomb** (李鄭屋漢墓) discovered in **1955**, indicates that the area had connections to the broader Chinese administrative and cultural sphere during the Han dynasty, though scholarly interpretations regarding the exact nature of early settlement and regional control vary.

## Territorial Expansion and Colonial Administration

Under the **Convention of Beijing** in **1860**, the southern portion of the Kowloon Peninsula up to Boundary Street was ceded to the British Empire. This expansion provided critical geographical security and anchorage for the colony of Hong Kong. Early British administration treated the area largely as a military and residential buffer, while Qing authorities maintained a watchful administrative presence nearby, particularly around the **Kowloon Walled City** (九龍城寨). Tensions occasionally flared, such as during the **Lin Weixi Affair** in **1888** in Tsim Sha Tsui (**尖沙咀**), which highlighted jurisdictional friction between British colonial authorities and Qing officials.

## Infrastructure and Modernization

Kowloon soon became a focal point for institutional and scientific development. In **1883**, the **Hong Kong Observatory** (香港天文台) was established in Tsim Sha Tsui to monitor typhoons and provide meteorological services vital for maritime trade. During the **Battle of Kowloon** in **December 1941**, the peninsula served as a primary theater of defense against the advancing Japanese forces during the Battle of Hong Kong, resulting in heavy fighting before the eventual British surrender.

Historiographical perspectives on Kowloon's development often diverge. British colonial narratives traditionally emphasized administrative modernization, infrastructural expansion, and public health initiatives. Conversely, local and revisionist scholarship frequently highlights the displacement of indigenous villages, the assertion of colonial sovereignty over the Walled City, and the complex socio-economic realities faced by local Chinese communities living under foreign administration.
