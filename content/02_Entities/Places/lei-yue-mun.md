## Geography and Strategic Importance

Lei Yue Mun (鯉魚門) is a channel situated at the eastern entrance of Victoria Harbour, separating **Kowloon** from **Hong Kong Island**. Its narrow waters made it a vital maritime chokepoint historically.

## Early History and Piracy

During the Ming and Qing dynasties, the secluded coves and difficult navigation of the channel made Lei Yue Mun a known haven for pirates and smugglers. From the perspective of Qing (清朝) official records, the area was a troublesome frontier requiring naval patrols. Local oral histories from New Territories communities, however, often remember early inhabitants not merely as outlaws, but as independent fisherfolk and salt producers navigating weak imperial control.

## British Colonial Era and Defense

Following the British occupation of Hong Kong Island in **1841** and the subsequent acquisition of Kowloon and the New Territories, the strategic value of the channel was prioritized by the British military. In the late **19th century**, the British constructed the **Lei Yue Mun Fort** (now the Hong Kong Museum of Coastal Defense) to guard the eastern approaches to the harbour against foreign naval threats.

## Modern Transformation

By the mid-20th century, the fishing villages within Lei Yue Mun, such as Sam Ka Tsuen (三家村), transitioned into vibrant communities known for stone quarrying and later, aquaculture. In recent decades, the area has transformed into a major tourism and culinary hub famous for its live seafood bazaars.

## Vulnerability to Extreme Weather

Due to its low-lying coastal geography, Lei Yue Mun remains acutely vulnerable to severe typhoons and storm surges. Notably, during **Typhoon Mangkhut** in **2018**, coastal areas suffered severe inundation and structural damage, highlighting ongoing challenges regarding climate change and coastal protection.
