## Introduction
Founded in **1795**, the London Missionary Society (倫敦傳道會) operated as an interdenominational Protestant mission that eventually sent key figures to South China and the nascent colony of Hong Kong. From a British colonial perspective, the Society's educational and medical initiatives were viewed as civilising endeavours that fostered modernisation and administrative utility. Conversely, some Qing-era officials and later critical scholarship viewed these missionary bodies as cultural extensions of British imperialism that undermined traditional Chinese social structures and religious practices.

## Connection to the Hong Kong College of Medicine
The Society is especially significant in Hong Kong history for its institutional and personnel links to the **Opening of the Hong Kong College of Medicine for Chinese** in **1887**. Medical missionaries affiliated with or supported by the network, most notably **Dr. James Cantlie** and **Dr. Ho Kai** (何啟), worked alongside LMS-linked institutions to advance Western medical training. **Dr. Sun Yat-sen** (孫中山), who studied at the College, was notably influenced by the Christian environment of these missionary circles during his formative intellectual years in Hong Kong.

## Historiographical Interpretations
Interpretations of the London Missionary Society's legacy remain multifaceted. Local Hong Kong and New Territories communities often remember the mission through the churches, schools, and hospitals it established, which formed the bedrock of the territory's early social welfare network. Meanwhile, contemporary historians debate the extent to which local converts actively agency-driven indigenised Christianity versus passively absorbed a Western ideological framework. While the precise motivations of every missionary and convert continue to be interpreted differently across British, mainland Chinese, and local historiographies, the Society's institutional footprint on Hong Kong's educational and medical development is undisputed.
