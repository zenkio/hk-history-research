## Strategic Maritime Significance
**Lyemun Pass** (鯉魚門), located between the eastern end of Hong Kong Island and the Kowloon Peninsula, has historically served as one of the primary shipping channels into **Victoria Harbour**. Due to its narrow passage, it has long been recognized for its maritime strategic value. During the **Qing dynasty**, the area was monitored to curb piracy and regulate trade, as reflected in the **Construction of the Fat Tong Mun Coastal Watch** (佛堂門海防), which aimed to secure the waters approaching the Pearl River Delta.

## Differing Perspectives on Development
Historical interpretations of the area vary significantly across different stakeholders. From the perspective of the **British colonial administration**, the pass was a vital defensive asset. The establishment of the **Lyemun Fort** (鯉魚門炮台) in the late **19th century** served as a defensive buffer for the port of Hong Kong. In contrast, local **New Territories** communities, particularly the fishing families who resided there for generations, view the pass as a traditional socio-economic heartland. Their narrative emphasizes the development of the **Sam Ka Tsuen** (三家村) fishing village and the subsequent growth of the area’s renowned seafood industry. 

## Military and Historical Memory
Later scholarship often examines the pass as a point of friction between imperial power and local autonomy. While colonial records highlight the installation of artillery batteries to protect British interests, historians note that these developments often displaced or restricted the traditional livelihoods of local inhabitants. The **Japanese occupation** of Hong Kong in **1941** further altered the landscape, as the fort saw active combat during the Battle of Hong Kong. Today, the site of the former fort serves as the **Museum of Coastal Defence**, though discussions persist regarding how best to balance the conservation of military architecture with the preservation of the indigenous maritime culture of the original village settlers.
