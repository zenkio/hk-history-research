## Geography and Early Development

**Mount Cameron** (**金馬倫山**) is located in the central part of Hong Kong Island, rising approximately 439 meters above sea level. Named after **Major General William Gordon Cameron**, the peak and its surrounding slopes gradually became a prestigious residential area for the colonial elite during the late 19th and early 20th centuries, valued for its cooler climate and sweeping views of Victoria Harbour.

## The Hong Kong Shinto Shrine

During the Japanese occupation of Hong Kong (**1941**–**1945**), Mount Cameron gained significant geopolitical and symbolic importance. In **1942**, the Japanese military administration chose the slopes of Mount Cameron as the site for the **Hong Kong Shinto Shrine** (**香港神社**). The groundbreaking ceremony took place in **May 1942**, intended to establish a central spiritual space that would anchor the 'spiritual-cultural' assimilation of the territory into the Japanese Empire.

## Historiography and Interpretations

Interpretations of the shrine project and its presence on Mount Cameron differ among historians and affected communities:

* **British Colonial and Post-War Perspectives:** Post-war narratives often framed the shrine as a transient imposition of an occupying regime, emphasizing its physical dismantling after the Japanese surrender in **1945** as the restoration of British sovereignty and pre-war urban order.
* **Local and Wartime Perspectives:** For the local Hong Kong population who lived through the occupation, the construction projects on strategic peaks like Mount Cameron were frequently associated with forced labor, economic hardship, and profound social disruption, though contemporary local memory of the specific shrine remains sparse.
* **Later Scholarship:** Modern historians view the shrine project through the lens of wartime imperial geography, noting how the selection of prominent elevations like Mount Cameron mirrored efforts across occupied Asia to visually and spiritually dominate colonized urban landscapes through sacred architecture.
