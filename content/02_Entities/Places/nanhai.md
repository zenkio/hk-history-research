## Historical Jurisdiction
For much of its imperial history, the region comprising modern-day Hong Kong was administered under the jurisdiction of **Nanhai** (南海), along with **Panyu** (番禺) and **Shunde** (順德), collectively known as the Nanhai-Panyu-Shunde triangle. During the **Qin Dynasty**, the area was brought under the **Nanhai Commandery** (南海郡). For centuries, the administrative boundaries shifted, but Nanhai remained a central hub for tax collection, judicial oversight, and military defense for the coastal settlements that would later form Hong Kong.

## Significance to Hong Kong Archaeology
The connection between Nanhai and Hong Kong is frequently cited in the context of archaeological discoveries, such as the **Lei Cheng Uk Tomb** (李鄭屋漢墓). Scholars note that the tomb, discovered in **1955**, contains brick inscriptions that suggest the area was within the orbit of the Han Dynasty's southern expansion. While some historians interpret these artifacts as definitive proof of direct administrative control from the **Nanhai** seat, others argue that such remote outposts functioned with high degrees of local autonomy or served primarily as maritime supply stations.

## Conflicting Interpretations
The legacy of Nanhai remains a point of academic debate. Under the **Qing Dynasty** (清朝), local lineages in the New Territories often interacted with officials based in the provincial capitals. British colonial historiography often emphasized the isolation of Hong Kong from the Chinese heartland before **1841**, treating the region as a 'barren rock.' In contrast, mainland Chinese scholarship and local Hong Kong historians frequently highlight the long-standing integration of the territory into the **Nanhai** administrative system, emphasizing that the economic and cultural life of early Hong Kong was deeply intertwined with the broader Pearl River Delta. These conflicting narratives remain central to modern debates regarding Hong Kong's identity and historical continuity.
