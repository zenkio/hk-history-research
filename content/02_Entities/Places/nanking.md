## Historical Significance
Nanking (南京), the former capital of China, holds pivotal importance in Hong Kong’s history as the location where the **Treaty of Nanking** was signed on **August 29, 1842**. This agreement ended the **First Opium War** between the British Empire and the Qing Dynasty.

## The Treaty and Hong Kong
Under Article III of the treaty, the island of Hong Kong (香港島) was ceded to the British Crown "in perpetuity." For the British colonial administration, this act was viewed as a legal instrument establishing a secure base for trade and maritime operations in the Far East. In contrast, many mainland Chinese perspectives and later scholarship characterize the treaty as the first of the "Unequal Treaties," emphasizing that it was signed under military duress and lacked genuine diplomatic parity.

## Divergent Interpretations
Interpretations of the significance of the event vary significantly. Colonial-era historiography often emphasized the treaty as the catalyst for Hong Kong’s development into a modern global port and a center for law and commerce. Conversely, nationalist interpretations frequently highlight the humiliation inherent in the cession of territory following the defeat of Qing forces. Within local Hong Kong communities, scholarly focus has shifted over time; while early colonial records focused on the legal transfer of land, later academic discourse frequently explores the long-term socio-political impact of the treaty on the identity of the Hong Kong people, who existed outside the direct governance of the Qing state for over a century. The document remains a cornerstone for understanding the legitimacy and eventual conclusion of British rule in the region.
