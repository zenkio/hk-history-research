## Development and History

**Nathan Road** (**彌敦道**) is one of the most prominent thoroughfares in the urban area of Kowloon, Hong Kong. Originally named **Robinson Road** when construction began following the acquisition of the Kowloon Peninsula under the **Convention of Peking** in **1860**, it was later renamed in **1909** to honor Sir **Matthew Nathan**, the 13th Governor of Hong Kong.

From the perspective of **British colonial** administration, the road was envisioned as a backbone for infrastructural expansion, designed to link the southern tip of Tsim Sha Tsui with the growing northern territories. Early photographs and administrative records show the route cutting through largely undeveloped terrain, gradually transforming as settlement expanded.

## Connection to the Kowloon British School

The establishment of the **Kowloon British School** (**九龍英童學校**), later known as King George V School, is closely tied to the urban development along this corridor. Founded in **1902** and relocated to its iconic premises on **Nirmal Street** near Nathan Road in **1902**, the institution catered primarily to the children of the growing expatriate community. While colonial archives framed the school and the surrounding road network as essential modernization projects that brought European educational standards to the peninsula, local and regional perspectives sometimes viewed these developments as physical manifestations of encroaching foreign governance on traditional land uses.

## Historiographical Interpretations

Interpretations of Nathan Road's legacy vary across different historical traditions. **British colonial** historiography typically emphasizes the engineering triumph and economic vitality generated by the avenue, depicting it as a symbol of progress. In contrast, modern **local Hong Kong scholarship** often examines the displacement of indigenous villages and the rapid commercialization that reshaped the cultural landscape of Kowloon. Meanwhile, mainland perspectives from the **Qing and Beijing** traditions view the entire urban layout of Kowloon, including Nathan Road, as a physical reminder of unequal treaties and the loss of sovereign territory during the late 19th century.
