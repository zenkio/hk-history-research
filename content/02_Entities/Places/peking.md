## Role in Colonial Expansion

Peking (北京), the imperial capital of the **Qing dynasty**, was the site of high-level diplomatic negotiations that fundamentally altered Hong Kong's geography. The city functioned as the seat of the Zongli Yamen, the Qing government's foreign affairs body, where British colonial officials and Qing plenipotentiaries drafted agreements that transferred administrative control of Hong Kong territory.

## Key Diplomatic Conventions

Two landmark treaties were formalized in Peking. The **Second Convention of Peking** (1860) was signed following the Second Opium War, resulting in the permanent cession of the Kowloon Peninsula south of Boundary Street to Britain. Decades later, the **Convention for the Extension of Hong Kong** (1898) was signed in the capital, leasing the New Territories and New Kowloon to Britain for 99 years. 

## Interpretations of Sovereignty

Historical interpretations of the treaties signed in Peking vary significantly. From the perspective of British colonial administrators at the time, these agreements were legally binding international instruments that secured the strategic security and economic viability of the Hong Kong colony. In contrast, mainland Chinese historiography and the Qing government often viewed these documents as "unequal treaties" signed under military duress, lacking the moral and sovereign legitimacy that modern interpretations emphasize. 

Later scholarship and the perspectives of local New Territories communities—particularly the indigenous inhabitants whose land rights were affected by the 1898 lease—add layers of complexity. While British law sought to integrate the New Territories into the colonial administrative framework, local land-holding practices often conflicted with colonial regulations. The role of Peking in these transactions remains a central theme in debates regarding the legitimacy of colonial rule, the nature of diplomatic coercion in the 19th century, and the eventual transition of Hong Kong’s sovereignty in 1997.
