## Overview
Located in the northeastern New Territories, **Plover Cove Reservoir** (船灣淡水湖) is a monumental engineering project completed in **1968**. It is significant as the first reservoir in the world to be constructed by damming an ocean bay, effectively isolating a portion of the sea and replacing saltwater with fresh water.

## The Water Crisis Context
During the **1960s**, Hong Kong faced catastrophic water shortages. Severe droughts in **1963** and **1964** necessitated strict water rationing, with some residents receiving water for only four hours every four days. Colonial officials identified the need for large-scale storage solutions that did not rely solely on the precarious rainfall patterns of the territory's limited land area. The choice of Plover Cove was driven by the necessity to bypass the scarcity of inland catchment areas.

## Differing Perspectives
Historical interpretation of the reservoir's construction varies. From the perspective of the **British colonial government**, the project was a triumph of civil engineering and a necessary logistical response to sustain the rapidly industrializing colony and its growing population. Conversely, many local villagers from the indigenous New Territories communities saw the project as a disruptive intrusion. The construction required the forced relocation of several traditional villages, such as **Sam Mun Tsai** (三門仔), permanently altering ancestral land use and maritime livelihoods. Later scholarly analysis often highlights the tension between colonial urban development mandates and the rights of rural, non-urbanized populations.

## Legacy
Following its completion, the reservoir significantly bolstered Hong Kong’s water security. It remains an essential component of the regional water management system, functioning alongside other major projects like the **High Island Reservoir** (萬宜水庫). Its development is widely cited by historians as a turning point in Hong Kong's capacity to support its burgeoning population through centralized utility management.
