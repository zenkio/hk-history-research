## Historical Significance
Located in **Central**, **Queen's Pier** (皇后碼頭) served as a primary landing site for British colonial governors and visiting members of the British royal family. Constructed between **1953** and **1954** to replace the earlier Victorian-era pier, the structure featured an iconic concrete-canopy design characteristic of post-war civic architecture. It was the site of swearing-in ceremonies for governors including **Sir Robert Black** and **Sir David Trench**.

## Heritage and Contested Memory
In the early **21st century**, the pier became the focal point of a prominent heritage preservation movement. Following the land reclamation projects in **Central**, the government proposed the demolition of the pier to accommodate new road infrastructure. 

Perspectives on the site remain diverse. Proponents of preservation emphasize the pier's role as a symbol of Hong Kong's unique colonial identity and its function as a public space for political expression. They argue that the site represents a tangible link to the city's democratic development. Conversely, government authorities and urban planners at the time maintained that the demolition was necessary for essential transport infrastructure improvements and urban modernization. Some academic scholarship notes that while the pier was a colonial construct, local residents adopted it as a "people's pier," shifting its meaning from an instrument of imperial power to a site of local civic identity.

## Current Status
Despite significant protests and occupation of the site in **2007**, the pier was dismantled to facilitate the **Central–Wan Chai Bypass**. While the government has stated an intention to relocate and reconstruct the pier, the project has faced repeated delays. The structural components remain in storage, and the exact timing and final form of its reconstruction remain subjects of ongoing debate between heritage advocates and the **Development Bureau**.
