## Historical Significance
**Sacred Hill** (聖山), formerly located in the **Kowloon City** (九龍城) district, was a distinctive granite outcrop that gained historical prominence due to its connection with the **Southern Song Dynasty** (南宋). According to traditional accounts, the court of the child emperors **Emperor Duanzong** (宋端宗) and **Emperor Bing** (宋帝昺) sought refuge in the Kowloon area around **1277**–**1278** while fleeing the encroaching **Yuan Dynasty** (元朝) forces.

## Interpretations and Legacy
Historians acknowledge that the association between the hill and the Song court remains a subject of ongoing debate. Local lore in the **New Territories** (新界) and historical accounts written centuries later emphasize the site as a location of imperial residence, a narrative that fostered a strong sense of local historical identity. Conversely, critical modern scholarship notes that direct primary source evidence confirming the exact locations of the imperial court remains fragmented. While the hill was historically venerated by local residents and scholars alike, its physical landscape underwent significant transformation during the **British colonial** period, particularly with the expansion of **Kai Tak Airport** (啟德機場).

## The Sung Wong Toi Inscription
The hill was named after the **Sung Wong Toi** (宋皇臺), which translates to 'Terrace of the Song Emperors.' A large boulder featuring the engraved characters "Sung Wong Toi" served as a focal point of this reverence. During the **Japanese Occupation** (1941–1945), much of the original Sacred Hill was leveled to provide materials for airport expansion. After the war, the salvaged portion of the boulder was relocated to the nearby **Sung Wong Toi Garden**, where it remains a site of historical preservation. The site continues to serve as a symbol of Hong Kong’s historical connection to broader Chinese dynastic history, with varying interpretations of its importance shaped by shifting political and cultural perspectives throughout the 20th and 21st centuries.
