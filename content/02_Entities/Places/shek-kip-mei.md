## Origins and the 1953 Fire

Originally a rural area in New Kowloon, **Shek Kip Mei** (石硤尾) was transformed in the post-World War II era by an influx of refugees fleeing the Chinese Civil War. These refugees constructed dense, highly flammable squatter settlements on the hillsides. On **Christmas Eve 1953**, a catastrophic fire broke out, destroying the homes of over **50,000** people and leaving them destitute on the streets.

## Shift in Government Policy

Prior to the disaster, the British colonial government had largely viewed squatter fires as temporary humanitarian crises and resisted direct intervention in mass housing. However, the sheer scale of the **Shek Kip Mei Fire** forced a dramatic policy reversal. Under the leadership of **Governor Alexander Grantham**, the administration established the **Hong Kong Housing Authority** and embarked on an ambitious public housing initiative, constructing multi-story concrete resettlement blocks directly on the site.

## Historiographical Interpretations

Interpretations of the Shek Kip Mei resettlement vary. British colonial historiography traditionally framed the rapid housing construction as a benevolent welfare intervention that laid the foundation for modern social stability and economic growth. In contrast, critical local scholarship and later socio-political analyses often argue that the resettlement program was primarily driven by pragmatic security concerns, economic stabilization, and the urgent need to clear valuable land for industrialization rather than purely humanitarian altruism. Regardless of the interpretation, the establishment of these blocks marked the genesis of Hong Kong's extensive public housing system, which eventually housed a major portion of the territory's population.
