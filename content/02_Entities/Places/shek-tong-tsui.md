## Origins and Development
Located in the Western District of Hong Kong Island, **Shek Tong Tsui** (石塘咀) gained prominence in the late **19th century** and early **20th century** as the primary red-light district of the colony. The area was developed following the relocation of licensed brothels from the Tai Ping Shan (太平山) area to mitigate social issues and improve sanitation. By the early **1900s**, it had become the epicenter of the 'Tangxi' (塘西) social culture, defined by its elaborate brothels, restaurants, and literati gatherings.

## The Banning of Licensed Brothels
For much of the colonial era, the British administration maintained a system of licensed prostitution. However, by the **1920s**, shifting moral tides and the influence of the **1932** International Convention for the Suppression of Traffic in Women and Children led the colonial government to re-evaluate this policy. In **1935**, the Hong Kong government officially banned licensed brothels. 

## Differing Historical Perspectives
Historians offer varying interpretations of the impact of the **1935** ban. From a British colonial perspective, the move is often framed as a progressive alignment with international humanitarian standards. Conversely, some local scholars and community researchers argue the ban did not eradicate the trade but rather pushed it into the informal sector, exacerbating vulnerabilities for sex workers. From the perspective of late Qing-era and early Republican Chinese observers, the district represented both a site of decadent colonial influence and a sanctuary for intellectual exchange, with literary accounts frequently romanticizing the 'Tangxi' era. While the physical landscape of the area has since transformed into a residential and commercial hub, the legacy of the district remains a focal point in discussions regarding Hong Kong’s colonial social policies and the evolution of urban life in the Western District.
