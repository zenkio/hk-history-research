## Historical Significance
Completed in **1936**, the **Shing Mun Reservoir** (城門水塘), often referred to as the Jubilee Reservoir (銀禧水塘) to commemorate the silver jubilee of King George V, represents a critical phase in Hong Kong's water management infrastructure. Located in the **Shing Mun Valley**, the project necessitated the relocation of several long-standing Hakka villages, a point of tension frequently noted in local history and the oral traditions of the New Territories communities.

## Water Security and Rationing
By the early **20th century**, Hong Kong’s rapid population growth consistently outpaced existing water supplies. The reservoir was designed to alleviate the chronic shortages that plagued the colony. Despite its completion, the region continued to face severe challenges, most notably during the **1963–1964** drought. During this period, the colonial government implemented extreme water rationing, limiting supply to four hours every four days. This crisis became a touchstone in Hong Kong’s history, highlighting both the vulnerability of the territory's geography and the strain of colonial administration during resource scarcity.

## Interpretations of Development
Historians offer differing lenses through which to view the reservoir's development. From a British colonial perspective, the project is often cited as a triumph of engineering and long-term urban planning. In contrast, local New Territories historical accounts emphasize the displacement of indigenous populations and the loss of traditional agrarian land. Some modern scholarship examines how these large-scale infrastructure projects integrated the rural New Territories more tightly into the urban colonial state, shifting the region's socio-economic character. Whether viewed as an essential modernizing achievement or a disruptive colonial imposition, the Shing Mun Reservoir remains a central site in the narrative of Hong Kong's struggle for self-sufficiency.
