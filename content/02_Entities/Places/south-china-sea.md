## Geographic and Historical Significance
The **South China Sea** (南海) is a marginal sea of the western Pacific Ocean, forming the maritime gateway through which global trade reaches Hong Kong. Historically, the waters surrounding the territory were populated by Tanka and Hoklo fishing communities who maintained a distinct culture adapted to the maritime environment. For these local communities, the sea was not merely a border but a primary source of livelihood and a space of mobility long before the arrival of colonial authorities.

## Sovereignty and Administrative Perspectives
The legal and historical status of the region has been subject to varying interpretations. During the **Qing Dynasty** (清朝), the sea was governed under an imperial maritime administration, though the efficacy of this control fluctuated. Following the **First Opium War** (1841) and the subsequent treaties, British colonial authorities exercised jurisdiction over the immediate territorial waters of Hong Kong to secure trade routes and maritime safety. Contemporary scholarship and international legal perspectives often diverge on the maritime demarcations of the sea, reflecting the complex interplay between historical claims, the **United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea** (UNCLOS), and modern geopolitical interests. 

## Modern Integration
In the 20th century, the role of the South China Sea shifted toward tourism and urban development. The establishment of **Ocean Park** (海洋公園) in **1977**, located on the southern coast of Hong Kong Island, symbolizes the transformation of the waterfront from purely functional trade and fishing zones into areas of leisure and ecological study. While the harbor remains the heart of Hong Kong’s maritime legacy, the surrounding South China Sea continues to be a focal point for environmental conservation efforts, regional shipping navigation, and the ongoing historical discourse regarding Hong Kong's maritime heritage.
