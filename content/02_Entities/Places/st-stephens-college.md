## Origins and Development
St. Stephen's College (聖士提反書院) was founded in **1903** by the Anglican Church as a boys' school, intended to provide Western-style education to the local Chinese elite. Its location on the Stanley Peninsula was chosen for its relative isolation and coastal position. Over the decades, the school became a symbol of the integration of British colonial education policies and local intellectual development.

## The Battle of Hong Kong and the Massacre
During the **December 1941** invasion of Hong Kong by Imperial Japanese forces, the college buildings were requisitioned to serve as an emergency military hospital. On **December 25, 1941**, the site became the location of the **St. Stephen's College Massacre** (聖士提反書院大屠殺). While historical consensus confirms that wounded British and Canadian soldiers, as well as medical staff, were brutally murdered by Japanese forces, specific details regarding the exact sequence of events remain a subject of rigorous study. Perspectives on the massacre vary; while many colonial-era accounts emphasize the cruelty of the Japanese military, later scholarship has sought to contextualize the event within the broader chaos and breakdown of command during the fall of the colony.

## The Stanley Internment Camp
Following the surrender of Hong Kong, the college grounds and the surrounding area were incorporated into the **Stanley Internment Camp** (赤柱拘留營). The camp housed thousands of British, American, and other Allied nationals. Conditions in the camp are documented through extensive diaries and later research, which highlight the severe malnutrition, disease, and psychological strain experienced by the internees until the liberation in **August 1945**. Post-war accounts from the colonial government often centered on the resilience of the internees, whereas subsequent local and academic historical perspectives have provided a more nuanced look at the camp's administration and the varied experiences of different civilian demographics within the facility.
