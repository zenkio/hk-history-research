## Overview
Established in **January 1942**, the Stanley Internment Camp (赤柱拘留營) was located on the grounds of the Stanley Prison and St. Stephen's College. Following the fall of Hong Kong on **December 25, 1941**, Imperial Japanese authorities confined thousands of British, American, and other Allied nationals here. Conditions were characterized by extreme malnutrition, overcrowding, and a lack of adequate medical supplies, leading to significant mortality rates among the internees.

## Historical Interpretations
Perspectives on the camp vary depending on the historical lens. British colonial records often emphasize the resilience of the internees and the eventual formation of **Gimson’s Provisional British Administration**. Under the leadership of **Franklin Gimson**, the Colonial Secretary, an ad-hoc administrative body began planning for the post-war transition to British authority while still inside the camp. Conversely, some post-war scholarship and local community accounts focus on the civilian experience of occupation, noting that while internees suffered, the local Chinese population faced broader systematic displacement, labor conscription, and starvation outside the camp's walls.

## Post-War Transition
As the Japanese surrender neared in **August 1945**, the camp became a focal point for the restoration of British governance. The internees provided a skeleton staff that helped facilitate the return of the Royal Navy and the transition of power. Historians debate the extent to which these events served as a 'continuity' of colonial power versus an 'interruption' that fundamentally altered the relationship between the British authorities and the local population. Today, the site remains a significant location for understanding the wartime experience and the political maneuvers that defined the immediate post-war recovery of the territory.
