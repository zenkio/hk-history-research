## Historical Context
Following the **Fall of Hong Kong** on **December 25, 1941**, the Japanese military administration established the **Stanley Internment Camp** (赤柱拘留營) on the Stanley Peninsula. The site, which utilized the existing buildings of **Stanley Prison** (赤柱監獄) and the adjacent **St. Stephen's College** (聖士提反書院), was used to house approximately 2,800 civilians, primarily British, American, and other Allied nationals.

## Life in the Camp
Conditions within the camp were severe. Internees faced extreme overcrowding, chronic food shortages, and a lack of adequate medical supplies. While the camp is primarily remembered for the deprivation experienced by the internees, historical accounts highlight the complex social structures that emerged within the fences. Internees organised communal kitchens, makeshift schools for children, and informal governance systems to survive. The site was liberated by the British Royal Navy on **August 30, 1945**.

## Interpretations of Significance
Interpretations of the camp's legacy vary across historical narratives. From the perspective of British colonial history, the site is often depicted as a symbol of endurance and the preservation of British identity under extreme duress. Conversely, some post-war historical scholarship and local voices have noted that while the internees suffered immensely, the occupation period inflicted systemic violence and hardship upon the wider Hong Kong population, particularly local Chinese residents, which at times created complex dynamics between the internees and the local community. There remains academic discussion regarding the extent to which the camp administration reflected the broader goals of the Japanese 'Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere' versus localized military control. The site remains a quiet but significant monument to the wartime trauma that redefined the territory's transition into the late 20th century.
