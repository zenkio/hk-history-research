## Early History and Administration
Prior to the arrival of the British, **Stanley** (赤柱) was a significant fishing village and market center for the Tanka and Punti communities. Following the British acquisition of Hong Kong Island in **1841**, the colonial government initially considered **Stanley** as the administrative capital due to its natural harbor. However, the plan was abandoned in favor of Victoria (Central) due to the difficult terrain and health concerns regarding malaria. Despite this, the area remained a vital military and residential outpost.

## The Battle of Hong Kong and Internment
During the **Battle of Hong Kong** in **December 1941**, **Stanley** was a key defensive position. The region witnessed the **St. Stephen's College Massacre**, a harrowing event in which wounded Allied soldiers and medical staff were killed by invading Japanese forces. Following the fall of Hong Kong, the Japanese military established the **Stanley Internment Camp** on the peninsula. 

## Differing Historical Perspectives
Interpretations of **Stanley**'s wartime history vary. British colonial records emphasize the resilience of the interned civilians and the brutality faced during the occupation. Conversely, local history accounts often highlight the survival strategies of the indigenous villagers who remained in the area, navigating the complex power dynamics between the occupying forces and local life. Later historical scholarship seeks to balance these narratives by examining the civilian experience across all ethnic groups present in the camp and the surrounding village. Today, while **Stanley** is recognized for its colonial architecture and market, it remains a site of profound historical memory regarding the suffering endured during the Pacific War.
