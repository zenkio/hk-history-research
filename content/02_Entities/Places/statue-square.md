## Origins and Colonial Monumentalism

Laid out in the late **19th century** on reclaimed land in Central, **Statue Square (皇后像廣場)** was originally conceived to display bronze monuments dedicated to the British royal family and prominent colonial figures. The space was dominated by a bronze statue of **Queen Victoria**, unveiled in **1896** to mark her diamond jubilee. Over subsequent decades, the square accumulated various memorials celebrating the British Empire's reach and local colonial elites, establishing it as the administrative and ceremonial core of British Hong Kong.

## The Japanese Occupation and Scrap Metal

Following the fall of Hong Kong in **December 1941**, the Japanese military administration sought industrial raw materials to support its war effort. In **1942**, authorities ordered the dismantling of the bronze royal statues erected in Statue Square and other parts of the colony. These monuments, including the statue of Queen Victoria, were shipped to Japan to be melted down for Japanese war scrap. While some statues were recovered after the war and eventually relocated—such as the Queen Victoria statue finding a new home in **Victoria Park** in **1955**—the removal permanently altered the visual landscape of the square.

## Interpretations and Post-War Transformation

Interpretations of Statue Square's history vary depending on political perspective. British colonial narratives historically emphasized the space as a symbol of modernization, civic order, and loyalty to the Crown. Conversely, later scholarship and anti-colonial interpretations view the square's original incarnation as a manifestation of aggressive imperial domination that marginalized local Chinese populations.

Following the post-war removal of the imperial statues, the square lost its monumental character and was redeveloped into a modern urban park. In contemporary decades, Statue Square has gained new significance as the primary gathering place for foreign domestic workers, particularly Indonesian and Filipina women, who congregate there on Sundays to rest, socialize, and hold cultural events. This modern usage has reframed the space from a monument of colonial authority into a vibrant expression of multicultural civil society in Hong Kong.
