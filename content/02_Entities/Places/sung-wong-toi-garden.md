## Historical Significance
Sung Wong Toi, meaning 'Terrace of the Song Kings' (宋王臺), is a memorial marking the location where, according to tradition, the last two boy emperors of the **Southern Song Dynasty**, **Emperor Duanzong** (**趙昰**) and **Emperor Bing** (**趙昺**), sought refuge in the **Kowloon** area in **1277**–**1279** while fleeing the invading **Yuan Dynasty** forces.

## Interpretations and Perspectives
The site has carried diverse meanings throughout its history. To local inhabitants of the **Kowloon** area during the late imperial period, the boulder served as a site of local legend and spiritual reverence. During the **British colonial period**, the colonial administration sought to institutionalize the site as a landmark of heritage, culminating in the formal gazetting of the garden. Conversely, later nationalist historiographies, particularly from the **Republic of China** era, utilized the site to reinforce a narrative of loyalist resistance and connection to mainland Chinese dynastic lineage.

## The Relocation
Originally, the monument was inscribed on a large granite boulder situated on a hill known as **Sacred Hill** (**聖山**). During the **Japanese occupation** of Hong Kong in **1942**, the hill was leveled to expand the **Kai Tak Airport**. The section of the boulder containing the engraved characters was preserved, cut into a smaller block, and moved to its present location in **Sung Wong Toi Garden**, which was officially opened in **1959**. Modern scholarship often emphasizes the irony of the site’s preservation: while the physical monument was rescued, the original geographical context of **Sacred Hill** was destroyed by military infrastructure expansion. Debates continue among historians regarding the precise movements of the imperial court, as primary records from the era are fragmented and occasionally contradictory, leaving the exact duration and specific locations of the emperors' stay in Hong Kong a subject of scholarly inquiry rather than absolute certainty.
