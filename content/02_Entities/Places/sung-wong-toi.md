## Historical Context
Sung Wong Toi (宋王臺), which translates to 'Terrace of the Song Kings,' refers to a rocky hill and memorial stone associated with the **Southern Song** (南宋) dynasty. According to historical tradition, the boy emperors **Emperor Duanzong** (宋端宗) and his brother **Emperor Bing** (宋帝昺) fled south to the Kowloon area in **1277** and **1278** to escape the encroaching **Yuan** (元) forces. It is widely accepted that the royal court utilized the region’s coastal geography as a temporary base.

## Interpretations of the Site
The significance of the site has been interpreted differently across eras. For local **Kowloon** (九龍) communities and nationalist historians, the site became a symbol of loyalty and resistance against invading forces. During the British colonial period, the site was officially recognized as a historical monument, though colonial perspectives often framed it as a curiosity of local topography rather than a center of imperial legacy. Conversely, some scholars of **Qing** (清) and later mainland traditions have debated the precise geographical movements of the court, suggesting that while the royal presence in the Pearl River Delta is well-documented, the specific association of this exact boulder with the emperors rests on long-standing oral and local historical tradition rather than archaeological confirmation.

## Modern Preservation
During the **Japanese occupation** of Hong Kong in the **1940s**, the original larger rock was damaged during the expansion of the Kai Tak Airport. Following the war, the remaining fragment was preserved and relocated to the present **Sung Wong Toi Garden** (宋王臺花園). In recent years, the **MTR Corporation** construction of the **Sung Wong Toi Station** led to significant archaeological findings, revealing remnants of the **Song** (宋) and **Yuan** (元) dynasties, which have provided a new layer of physical evidence regarding the intensity of human activity in the area during that turbulent period. Today, the site remains a focal point for understanding Hong Kong's deep historical ties to the final chapters of the Song dynasty.
