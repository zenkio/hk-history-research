## Early Development and Settlement

Tai Ping Shan (太平山) was one of the earliest areas developed by the British colonial administration for Chinese residence in **1841**. Located on the hillside above the main colonial administrative district, it became a densely populated enclave for laborers and merchants. Historians often emphasize the segregationist nature of the area, noting that while the British favored the Mid-Levels and Central, the Chinese population was largely confined to this steep, poorly ventilated terrain.

## The 1894 Plague Outbreak

In **May 1894**, the district gained international notoriety when the third global bubonic plague pandemic reached Hong Kong. The outbreak began in Tai Ping Shan, where cramped, unsanitary tenements created conditions that allowed the *Yersinia pestis* bacterium to thrive. The colonial government's response, characterized by the forced entry of houses, the use of white-wash, and the eventual demolition of large sections of the district, remains a subject of differing historical interpretations. 

Colonial records typically frame these actions as necessary sanitary interventions to curb a public health emergency. Conversely, local community accounts and later scholarship often highlight the cultural trauma inflicted upon the residents. The forced evictions and the perceived disregard for Chinese funerary customs and property rights led to significant unrest. It was during this period that the bacteriologist **Alexandre Yersin** arrived in the territory, successfully isolating the plague bacillus at the Alice Memorial Hospital. 

## Legacy and Reconfiguration

Following the epidemic, the colonial government enacted the **Tai Ping Shan Resumption Ordinance**, leading to the widespread destruction of existing structures. The area was redeveloped into the **Tai Ping Shan Street** area, known today for its historical temples, such as **Man Mo Temple**, and its eventual evolution into a vibrant, modern residential and commercial neighborhood. While the physical landscape of the 19th-century district has been entirely replaced, the area remains a symbolic site for understanding the intersection of colonial governance, public health policy, and the resilient development of Hong Kong's Chinese community.
