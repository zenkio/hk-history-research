## Geography and Naming
**Tate's Cairn** (大老山) stands at 583 meters above sea level within the **Ma On Shan Country Park**. The name is believed to be derived from the name of a British military officer, **Captain G. P. Tate**, who conducted surveys of the area in the late 19th century. Local oral traditions and topographical maps from the pre-colonial era occasionally refer to the ridge by various regional names, though the English name became standard under the colonial administration.

## Strategic and Infrastructure Significance
Historically, the mountain served as a natural divide separating the urbanized areas of **Kowloon** from the rural **Sha Tin** valley. For much of the early 20th century, the terrain remained largely wild and difficult to traverse. The area gained significant modern importance in the late 20th century due to Hong Kong's rapid suburbanization.

## The Tate's Cairn Tunnel
The **Tate's Cairn Tunnel** (大老山隧道) represents a major turning point in the site's history. Completed in **1991**, the tunnel was the longest road tunnel in Hong Kong at the time of its opening. Its construction was driven by the need to alleviate severe traffic congestion on the **Lion Rock Tunnel**. While the government emphasized the project’s role in regional economic integration, local community groups and historical scholars have noted that the construction projects in the area permanently altered the ecological landscape and the traditional access routes used by villagers for generations.

Interpretations of the site’s development vary. British colonial records emphasize the tunnel as a triumph of civil engineering necessary for urban connectivity. Conversely, some local historians argue that the development reflects a period of top-down planning that prioritized industrial efficiency over the preservation of the New Territories' historical topography. Today, the mountain remains a popular destination for hikers, balancing its role as a logistical artery with its function as a preserved natural space.
