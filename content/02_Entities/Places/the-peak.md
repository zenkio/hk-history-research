## Geography and Early Settlement

Rising in the western half of Hong Kong Island, The Peak (**Victoria Peak** or **Tai Ping Shan**) historically served as a navigational landmark for mariners entering the harbor. Following the British colonization of Hong Kong in **1841**, the area's cooler climate quickly attracted colonial administrators seeking relief from the humid subtropical heat of the coastal settlement below.

## Colonial Segregation and Ordinance No. 1 of 1888

By the late nineteenth century, The Peak became an exclusive European residential district, physically and socially separated from the densely populated Chinese communities in the lower districts. This spatial divide was legally reinforced through measures such as **Eitel's Ordinance No. 1 of 1888** (**The Peak Reservation Ordinance**), which prohibited non-Europeans, including wealthy Chinese merchants, from residing on The Peak without the explicit permission of the Governor. 

## Historiographical Perspectives

Interpretations of The Peak's history diverge significantly across different perspectives:

* **British Colonial Administration:** Contemporary colonial officials framed the segregation policies as sanitary necessities required to protect Europeans from prevalent tropical diseases.
* **Local Hong Kong Communities:** Local Chinese elites and residents viewed the reservation laws as overt institutional racism that humiliated the local population and entrenched colonial dominance.
* **Later Scholarship:** Modern historians generally analyze The Peak as a prime spatial manifestation of British imperial hegemony, where geography was utilized to enforce racial hierarchies and socio-economic exclusion.

## Infrastructure and Accessibility

To facilitate travel for its elite residents, the **Peak Tram** was opened in **1888**, drastically altering the accessibility of the mountainous terrain and shaping the area's development into a major urban node.
