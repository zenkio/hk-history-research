## Origins and Early Settlement
Originally known as **Rennie’s Mill** (照鏡環), the area took its English name from **Alfred Herbert Rennie**, a Canadian businessman who attempted to establish a flour mill there in the early **20th century**. The venture failed, and following **Rennie’s** death in **1908**, the site remained largely undeveloped until the mid-century political upheaval.

## The Refugee Camp Era
Following the establishment of the **People’s Republic of China** in **1949**, thousands of former **Kuomintang** (KMT) soldiers and their families fled to Hong Kong. In **1950**, the colonial government designated the area as a temporary refugee camp. For decades, the site was characterized by its strong political identity; it was frequently referred to as a 'Little Taiwan' due to the widespread display of the **Republic of China** flag and the influence of pro-KMT sentiments. While the **British colonial government** initially viewed the settlement as a necessary humanitarian measure, local community reports and later scholarship highlight the complex tensions between the camp’s residents, who often lived in precarious conditions, and the colonial administration’s cautious approach toward political activities within the territory.

## Redevelopment and Modernity
By the **1990s**, as part of the **Junk Bay New Town** (將軍澳新市鎮) development plan, the government began clearing the refugee camp to make way for public housing and high-density residential projects. This process involved the demolition of the hillside shanties, leading to the relocation of residents to nearby public housing estates. The redevelopment remains a subject of historical interest; some scholars point to the erasure of the area’s unique political history as a byproduct of modernization, while others emphasize the necessary improvement in living standards and public infrastructure. Today, **Tiu Keng Leng** serves as a major transit hub connected to the **MTR** network, marking its complete integration into the fabric of contemporary Hong Kong.
