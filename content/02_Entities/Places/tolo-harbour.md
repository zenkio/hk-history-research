## Geographic and Historical Significance
**Tolo Harbour** (吐露港) is a major body of water in the northeastern New Territories, connecting to the South China Sea through the Tolo Channel (赤門海峽). It is bordered by the districts of **Tai Po** (大埔) and **Sha Tin** (沙田). Historically, the harbour was a critical hub for the local maritime economy and the site of significant state-sponsored industries.

## The Pearl Harvesting Industry
One of the most notable historical aspects of the harbour is the **Tai Po** pearl fishery. According to historical records such as the *Guangdong Tongzhi* (廣東通志), the waters were a source of high-quality pearls as early as the **Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms** period. 

Interpretations regarding the pearl industry vary. Imperial Chinese records often depict the harvesting as a state-sanctioned activity that brought prestige to the region, though they also note the immense hardship and fatalities suffered by local divers. In contrast, local **Tanka** (疍家) community traditions emphasize the exploitation inherent in the imperial tribute system. Later scholarship suggests that the intensity of pearl harvesting likely contributed to the eventual depletion of oyster beds in the area by the **Ming Dynasty** (明朝). 

## Colonial and Modern Development
Following the **1898** Convention for the Extension of Hong Kong Territory, the harbour came under British administration. While the British colonial government initially prioritized the harbour for navigation and maritime defense, the later 20th century saw a shift toward major reclamation projects. The development of **Sha Tin** and **Tai Po** as new towns transformed the coastline, moving the shoreline significantly and altering the harbour's ecological landscape. Whether these reclamation efforts were a triumph of modern planning or an irreparable loss of traditional coastal heritage remains a subject of ongoing debate among local historians and environmental advocates.
