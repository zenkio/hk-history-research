## Foundation and Early Years

Established in **1911** under the administration of Governor **Sir Frederick Lugard**, the University of Hong Kong (香港大學) was founded to project British cultural and scientific influence in East Asia while serving local elite training needs. Early milestones include the historic **1923** address by **Sun Yat-sen** (孫中山) at Loke Yew Hall, reflecting the institution's entanglement with modern Chinese nationalism. British colonial authorities initially viewed the university as an instrument of British prestige, whereas local Chinese communities and donors saw it as a crucial avenue for advancement.

## Cultural Studies and Political Shifts

During the interwar period under figures like Governor **Sir Cecil Clementi**, the university became a focal point for promoting traditional Chinese cultural studies alongside Western curricula. This policy aimed to appease local elites and scholar-officials connected to late Qing and Republican networks, though interpretations differ on whether this was genuine cultural preservation or a divide-and-rule colonial strategy.

## Contemporary Controversies

In recent decades, HKU has transitioned into a prominent battleground for freedom of expression and historical memory in Hong Kong. In **December 2021**, the university administration ordered the abrupt removal of the **Pillar of Shame** (國殤之柱), a monument by Danish sculptor **Jens Galschiøt** commemorating the Tiananmen Square crackdown. Pro-Beijing circles and university management framed the removal as a routine legal and property management decision, while critics, human rights advocates, and international observers viewed it as a stark erosion of academic freedom and historical remembrance.

More recently, in **late 2022**, the campus witnessed **A4 Paper Protests** in solidarity with mainland demonstrations, illustrating how students continue to use the grounds to voice broader geopolitical grievances despite tightening security under the **National Security Law**.
