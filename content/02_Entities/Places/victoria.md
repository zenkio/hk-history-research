## Establishment and Urban Center

Founded in the early years of British colonial rule, **Victoria** (維多利亞城), often referred to as the City of Victoria, was designated as the capital and central urban area of Hong Kong Island. British colonial authorities laid out the city's districts, naming them after British monarchs and figures, establishing it as the administrative heart where institutions like **The China Mail** newspaper operated to serve the expatriate and mercantile communities.

## Colonial Administration and Security

As the seat of power, Victoria was the focal point of both governance and security challenges. In **1912**, the city was shaken by the **Assassination Attempt on Governor Sir Francis May** (梅含理), reflecting the intense political unrest and spillover of mainland revolutionary sentiments into the urban center. Later, during the Japanese occupation, the administrative structures based in Victoria were temporarily dismantled, leading to the eventual **Formation of Gimson's Provisional British Administration** in **1945** to restore order.

## Historiographical Interpretations

Traditional British colonial accounts often framed the development of Victoria as a triumph of modern urban planning and governance over a barren landscape. In contrast, local Hong Kong and later critical scholarship emphasize how this urban core was built upon the displacement of local indigenous communities and the imposition of a rigid racial and social hierarchy. Qing and mainland Chinese historiography viewed Victoria primarily as a symbol of British imperial encroachment and the base from which extraterritorial influence was projected into Southern China.
