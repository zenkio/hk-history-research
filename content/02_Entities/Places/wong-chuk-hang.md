## Early History and Settlement
Before extensive urban development, **Wong Chuk Hang** (黃竹坑), literally meaning 'Yellow Bamboo Hang', was a valley primarily occupied by small villages and agricultural plots. Historical records suggest the area was inhabited by local communities long before the British colonial period. During the early colonial era, the landscape remained largely pastoral. Historians note that the valley served as a crucial water catchment area and a route connecting the southern coastal settlements to the northern parts of Hong Kong Island.

## Industrial Transition and Ocean Park
The character of the region shifted significantly in the mid-20th century. Following the post-war industrial boom, **Wong Chuk Hang** was designated as one of the major industrial zones on the south side of the island, with many multi-story industrial buildings constructed in the 1960s and 1970s. 

A pivotal moment in the area's development was the **1977** opening of **Ocean Park** (海洋公園). The park's establishment represented a shift in Hong Kong’s economic landscape toward tourism and leisure. While British colonial authorities viewed the project as a vital step in modernizing the territory's recreational infrastructure, local community discussions have occasionally contrasted this rapid development with the displacement of historical village land use. Later scholarship often highlights this era as a microcosm of Hong Kong's rapid transformation from a manufacturing base to a service-oriented economy.

## Contemporary Status
Today, the area is undergoing a process of urban renewal. Following the opening of the **MTR South Island Line** in **2016**, the former industrial district has seen an influx of commercial developments, art galleries, and residential projects. While the physical landscape of old factories is being replaced by modern skyscrapers, the area remains a focal point for understanding the interplay between Hong Kong's industrial past and its modern global city identity.
