## Archaeological Significance

Located on Lamma Island (南丫島), **Wong Tei Tung** (黃地峒) is recognized as one of the most significant Paleolithic sites in Hong Kong. Archaeological investigations at the site have yielded numerous chipped stone tools, including scrapers and choppers, made primarily from local igneous rocks. These artifacts indicate that human toolmakers inhabited or frequented the coastal areas of the Pearl River Delta thousands of years before the Bronze and Neolithic ages.

## Historiographical Interpretations

While colonial-era surveys occasionally noted early stone implements, systematic excavations in the late 20th and early 21st centuries by local and mainland scholars established the site's antiquity. Modern scholarship generally views Wong Tei Tung alongside other regional discoveries to understand early maritime adaptation and lithic industry in southern China. However, precise dating remains a subject of ongoing academic discussion due to the surface context of many disturbed artifacts, leading researchers to exercise caution when linking these tools to specific hominid timelines or distinct cultural phases.

## Connection to Hong Kong History

For the history of Hong Kong, Wong Tei Tung shifts the narrative of human habitation significantly deeper into prehistory. While popular histories often emphasize Hong Kong's role as a later imperial maritime outpost—such as during the global trade of **Tang ceramics** (唐代陶瓷) and subsequent dynasties—Wong Tei Tung demonstrates a deep-root Pleistocene human presence long before written records or formalized trade networks emerged in the region.
