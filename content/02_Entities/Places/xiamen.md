## Historical Significance
**Xiamen** (廈門), historically known to Westerners as Amoy, served as a vital node in the maritime trade networks connecting the Fujian coast to Southeast Asia and, eventually, the British **Canton Trade System** (廣州貿易制度). While Hong Kong’s development is often centralized around the **Pearl River Delta**, the socio-economic ties between the Fujianese diaspora and the British trade interests in **Canton** (廣州) were inextricable. 

## Connection to the Canton Trade System
Before the **First Opium War** (第一次鴉片戰爭), the **Canton System** restricted foreign maritime trade to the port of Guangzhou. **Xiamen** remained a secondary port, yet it was a critical transshipment hub for tea and silk. British merchants, frustrated by the bureaucratic limitations of the **Cohong** (公行) system in Canton, frequently looked toward other coastal ports. The eventual forced opening of **Xiamen** as a treaty port under the **Treaty of Nanking** (南京條約) in **1842** marked a turning point. 

## Interpretations of Trade Expansion
Historical interpretations of these events remain divided. **British colonial** perspectives often framed the opening of treaty ports like **Xiamen** as an inevitable expansion of "free trade" and a necessary corrective to Qing isolationism. Conversely, **Qing-era** and subsequent **Beijing-centric** historiography frequently emphasize the trauma of the "Century of Humiliation," viewing the forced opening of these ports as the onset of foreign economic encroachment. 

Local **Hong Kong** perspectives note that the opening of secondary ports shifted migration patterns. As trade moved from the strict monopoly of Canton to a broader network, the movement of merchants and laborers between **Xiamen**, the **Fujian** region, and the burgeoning **Hong Kong** colony intensified. This mobility contributed to the demographic and commercial heterogeneity that defined the early colonial period. While the exact economic impact on individual maritime communities is difficult to quantify due to fragmented records, it is widely accepted that the diversification of ports undermined the traditional power structures of the Canton Cohong, indirectly bolstering the strategic value of the newly established **Hong Kong** port.
