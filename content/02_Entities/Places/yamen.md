## Overview and Geographical Context

Yamen (崖門), located at the mouth of the Pearl River in modern-day Guangdong province, is historically significant as the final defensive stronghold and graveyard of the Southern Song dynasty (南宋). Following the relentless Mongol advance, the remnant imperial court—including the young child emperors **Emperor Duanzong** (宋端宗) and later **Emperor Huaizong** (宋懷宗, commonly known as **Emperor Bing**)—relied heavily on coastal waters and islands within the greater Pearl River delta, including areas that now comprise the territory of Hong Kong, before making their final stand at Yamen.

## Connection to Hong Kong

The history of Yamen is inextricably linked to Hong Kong through the flight of the Song court. Prior to the fatal battle at Yamen, the imperial entourage sojourned in various parts of Hong Kong waters, such as **Mui Wo** (梅窩) and **Kowloon** (九龍), leaving behind strong oral traditions and cultural footprints. The eventual destruction of the Song fleet at Yamen in **March 1279** directly triggered local veneration practices in Hong Kong. Local communities and subsequent generations established shrines dedicated to loyal officials and figures associated with the loyalist resistance, most notably **Hau Wong** (侯王), whom local tradition often connects to **Yeung Ngai** (楊亮節), an uncle of the last child emperors who protected them during their flight through the region.

## Historiographical Interpretations

Interpretations of the events at Yamen vary across different historical perspectives. Traditional Qing and imperial Chinese historiography heavily romanticized the loyalist sacrifice at Yamen, epitomized by Prime Minister **Wen Tianxiang** (文天祥) and **Lu Xiufu** (陸秀夫) carrying the young emperor into the sea, framing it as the ultimate expression of Confucian loyalty and righteousness. Later scholarship, including modern regional studies, tends to place greater emphasis on the geopolitical collapse of the Song maritime economy and the broader demographic shifts that pushed refugees southward into the Pearl River delta, including the New Territories. British colonial historiography generally treated these late Song events as peripheral folklore, focusing instead on post-1841 maritime trade and administrative development, though colonial-era gazetteers occasionally documented the enduring local rituals and temple dedications tied to the Yamen tragedy.
