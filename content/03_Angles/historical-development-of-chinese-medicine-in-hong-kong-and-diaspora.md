The research conducted by **Ania Mah Gricuk**, a PhD candidate at the **University of California, Santa Cruz**, investigates the historical trajectory of medicine, food, and healing practices within overseas Chinese communities. Her doctoral dissertation, titled *Healing in Diaspora: The Making of Chinese Medicine in Hong Kong, Malay(si)a, and Singapore, 1860s-1980s*, explores the development and evolution of these practices over a span of more than a century. A central focus of the work is the co-creation of the category of 'Chinese Medicine' and the institutional rise of Chinese charitable hospitals in **Hong Kong** and **Southeast Asia**.

According to the source, the research seeks to deconstruct the established narrative that equates the identity of 'Chinese medicine' as being strictly and innately tethered to the mainland of **China**. Furthermore, **Gricuk** challenges traditional academic understandings that frame the history of medicine exclusively through the lens of **Western biomedicine**. By analyzing the production and consumption of medicine within these diaspora settings from the **1860s** through the **1980s**, the study illuminates how localized healing practices were adapted, exchanged, and institutionalized across different geopolitical contexts. The research highlights the critical role of philanthropic medical institutions in shaping the public health landscape for Chinese populations during the colonial and post-colonial eras.

## Historical context

> [!note] General background (AI, not from the source)

Chinese medicine has long played a vital role in Hong Kong's healthcare system, functioning alongside Western medical models introduced during British colonial rule. Charitable hospitals, often established by early Chinese merchant organizations, served as foundational institutions for the provision of traditional healing, helping to maintain cultural ties while serving the practical needs of the local population and the broader Chinese diaspora.
