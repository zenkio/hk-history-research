During the **1920s**, Hong Kong experienced a significant speculative land boom that precipitated a severe housing crisis. According to **Prof. Cecilia Chu** of The Chinese University of Hong Kong, the rapid escalation of land prices and frequent property transactions resulted in the mass eviction of existing tenants, triggering widespread public dissatisfaction. These tensions affected both middle-income European and Chinese populations, who were perceived as victims of wealthy, newly arrived mainland immigrants utilizing investment capital to dominate the market.

Public anger intensified toward large investment firms engaged in 'rent racking'—the systematic farming out of tenement properties to middleman 'rentier lords'—which reached a focal point in **1921** following accusations against a major land investment company. In response to public protest and the subsequent demand for government intervention, the colonial administration took the unprecedented step of enacting the first **Rent Control Ordinance** in **1921**. **Chu** notes that while this policy was welcomed by tenants, it was vehemently criticized by landlords and contained notable limitations, such as its exemption of new building projects, intended to maintain incentives for housing supply. The legislation ultimately proved ineffective at lowering rents or preventing evictions, as landlords exploited loopholes, including the intentional creation of domestic disturbances or the removal of architectural features to force vacancies. Despite these challenges, **Chu** observes that the working-class response was not a rejection of the colonial capitalist system, but rather a demand for protections that would safeguard worker livelihoods and ensure the territory's economic stability.

## Historical context

The 1920s in Hong Kong were characterized by rapid urban expansion and an influx of capital from mainland China, which frequently clashed with the needs of the local working class residing in overcrowded tenement housing. This era marked a transition in colonial governance, as the administration began to experiment with limited legislative interventions in the private housing market to maintain social order.
