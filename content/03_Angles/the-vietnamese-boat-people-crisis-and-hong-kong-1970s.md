During the 1970s, **Hong Kong** experienced a severe humanitarian crisis caused by a massive influx of refugees fleeing **Vietnam**. According to **Ray Yep** of the **Hong Kong History Centre**, the colonial administration faced immense pressure while receiving minimal support from the British government in **London**. **Yep** argues that the British response was characterized by indifference, influenced by post-independence anti-immigrant sentiment and a desire to maintain close ties with the **United States**. Two critical events highlighted these logistical and political challenges: the arrival of the **Maersk** vessel on **May 4, 1975**, carrying over 3,700 refugees, and an incident on **July 6, 1976**, involving a **Burmese** vessel carrying 99 refugees. In the former case, the government initially adopted a humanitarian approach, though it received insufficient financial aid from Britain. In the latter, entry was refused due to fears regarding the long-term settlement of refugees in the territory.

**Yep** asserts that the British government, acting under pressure from the **United States**, effectively forced **Hong Kong** into the role of a first-reception center. This policy aligned with an American strategy to vet refugees in specific **Asian** detention centers before final admission to the **US**. Consequently, the **Hong Kong** government was tasked with bearing the significant financial and social burdens of the crisis, a role that **Yep** characterizes as the outsourcing of British humanitarian responsibilities to the colony.

## Historical context

> [!note] General background (AI, not from the source)

Following the end of the Vietnam War and the reunification of the country in 1975, hundreds of thousands of people fled the new regime by sea. Hong Kong, as a nearby British colony, became a primary destination for these refugees, leading to a decade-long struggle involving international diplomacy, local containment policies, and long-term settlement programs.
