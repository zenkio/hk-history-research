According to **Ray Yep** of the **Hong Kong History Centre** at the **University of Bristol**, presenting in Lecture 8 of the **Hong Kong History Academy** online lecture series, **Hong Kong** faced a major humanitarian crisis starting in the **1970s** with the massive influx of Vietnamese boat people. Between **1975** and **1997**, over **200,000** Vietnamese boat people arrived in the territory. This influx coincided with a massive wave of illegal immigrants from mainland China, which sometimes saw up to 3,000 daily arrivals per district. **Ray Yep** notes that local citizens often questioned why authorities cracked down on mainland immigrants who shared familial ties while welcoming Vietnamese boat people with whom they had no connection.

The crisis was further complicated by international geopolitics. Following the **1975** **United States** defeat in the Vietnam War, the **US** government felt primary moral responsibility for the crisis but expected Asian countries to serve as "first asylum" nations where refugees had to land and undergo screening before entering Western countries. Consequently, **Hong Kong** was designated as a port of first asylum, despite receiving relatively limited aid and support from the international community and the British government. **Ray Yep** highlights how these overlapping local immigration pressures, geopolitical shifts, and international policies shaped the complex humanitarian challenge faced by the local administration during the **1970s**.

## Historical context

> [!note] General background (AI, not from the source)

During the late 20th century, Hong Kong frequently found itself on the frontline of geopolitical upheavals in East and Southeast Asia. The arrival of hundreds of thousands of Vietnamese refugees strained local resources, housing, and social services, leading to the establishment of closed and open detention camps across the territory that lasted for decades.
