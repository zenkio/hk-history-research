During the late **19th** and early **20th centuries**, the Hong Kong colonial government grappled with balancing housing needs, public safety, and economic imperatives. According to **Prof. Cecilia Chu** of **The Chinese University of Hong Kong**, urban planning during this period was fundamentally driven by colonial land policies and economic interests, which frequently prioritized the profits of landlords over the health and living conditions of the population. A pivotal moment occurred following the **1894** bubonic plague, when the government demolished the **Taiping Shan** district. This action met significant resistance from landlords, who argued that the state's intervention violated the principles of a colonial capitalist economy and that minor improvements could have addressed sanitary concerns. **Dr. Chu** identifies this era as witnessing the emergence of a new 'planning rationality' where the government sought to bolster its own legitimacy and maximize land revenue rather than strictly focus on public health. The period was marked by repeated urban crises, including a building collapse in Central in **1901** and a severe housing crisis in the early **1920s**. Throughout these decades, the prevalence of *táng lóu* (Chinese tenements) became a focal point of contention. These structures were widely criticized for their poor quality, yet they remained highly sought after by landlords of various ethnic backgrounds because their capacity for subdivision allowed for superior investment returns compared to traditional European housing designs.

## Historical context

> [!note] General background (AI, not from the source)

The **1894** bubonic plague was a transformative event for colonial Hong Kong, leading to the enactment of the Public Health Ordinance of **1894** and significantly increased government intervention in building regulations and land use. This period signaled a shift from laissez-faire governance toward more proactive urban management to secure the colony's stability as a global trade hub.

This lecture, presented by **Prof. Ray Yep** and delivered by **Associate Professor Cecilia Chu**, examines the history of land and housing policies in **Hong Kong** during the late **19th** and early **20th centuries**. **Dr. Chu** focuses on how urban crises and their aftermaths influenced the city's development, specifically citing the **1894** bubonic plague, the **1901** building collapse in Central, and a housing crisis in the early **1920s**. She explores how the government balanced competing interests, including public housing needs and the regulation of the property market. **Dr. Chu** argues that early urban planning in **Hong Kong** was heavily driven by economic interests and colonial land policies, which often favored the profits of landlords over public health and living conditions. For example, she explains that **Chinese** tenements, or *táng lóu*, were frequently criticized for poor quality, yet landlords of various ethnic backgrounds prioritized them because their ability to be subdivided yielded better investment returns than European houses. Furthermore, she highlights that after the **1894** plague, the government’s decision to demolish the **Taiping Shan** district was controversial, as many landlords argued it violated the fundamental principles of the colonial capitalist economy and that the land could have been made healthy through minor improvements. **Dr. Chu** concludes that this period demonstrates the emergence of a new planning rationality that sought to strengthen government legitimacy and maximize land revenue rather than solely improve public health.
