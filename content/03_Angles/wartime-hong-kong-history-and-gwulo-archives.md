According to source materials from the **Gwulo** website presented by **David Bellis**, historical discussions and archival documentation highlight the impact of war on Hong Kong. In a talk given to the Royal Geographical Society, **Bellis** utilized photographs taken by **Harrison Forman** between **1940** and **1941**—currently preserved at the University of Wisconsin—to illustrate British wartime preparations. These preparations included the construction of pillboxes, pen shelters, and the excavation of air-raid shelter tunnels. The records also document the subsequent damage inflicted during the Japanese occupation and American bombing raids. While many British-built defense structures were cleared after the war, physical remnants such as ventilation shafts, concrete platforms under Mount Cameron Mansions, and tunnels remain visible today.

Additional historical materials detailed in the Gwulo updates encompass World War II resistance and internment experiences. **Phyllis Nolasco Da Silva** is noted for her biography, *Courage in the Shadows*, which chronicles her three dangerous years working for the British Army Aid Group (**BAAG**) in Kwongchowwan, where she managed agents and survived Japanese interrogation and blackmail. Furthermore, wartime diaries—including those of **Leo Landau** and a newly scanned diary by the father of **Barbara Harding**—provide firsthand accounts of the Shamshuipo POW camp, the Argyle Street POW Camp, and the liberation of Hong Kong on **1945-08-16**. The archives also reference historical figures and personnel connected to the Hong Kong Volunteer Defence Corps (**HKVDC**) and other armed forces, such as **Robert Drude**, **Alfred Floisand**, **John Mayo HOUGHTON**, **Lawrence JACK**, **Alfred Cecil Houghton**, and **Kenneth Albert Watson**, alongside genealogical and institutional studies concerning early Hong Kong Protestant elites, the To Tsai Church registries, and King's College expenditures.

## Historical context

> [!note] General background (AI, not from the source)

During the **1940**–**1941** period, British authorities rapidly militarized Hong Kong in anticipation of a Japanese assault, constructing extensive civil defense infrastructure. Following the fierce Battle of Hong Kong in December 1941, the territory endured three years and eight months of harsh Japanese military occupation before its liberation in August 1945, leaving a profound legacy preserved through personal diaries, photographs, and postwar historical research.

Bellis concluded by inviting viewers to consult the Gwulo website and his book series, **Old Hong Kong Photos and the Tales They Tell**, for further information.
