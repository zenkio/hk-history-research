This document provides a rigorous **evidence-based framework** for understanding the pivotal two decades in Hong Kong following the **1898 New Territories lease**. By synthesising archival records and academic scholarship, the text outlines how the colonial state expanded its **administrative and spatial control** through legislative enactments, infrastructure projects, and the management of public health crises. It specifically highlights the tension between British governance and local populations, documenting instances of **indigenous armed resistance** and the implementation of **racial segregation** policies. Ultimately, the source serves as a scholarly guide to the **socio-political reconfiguration** of the region, while also identifying significant **gaps in the historical record** regarding the lives of women and non-elite ethnic minorities.
# Evidence Base for Hong Kong History: The New Territories Lease and Early Twentieth Century (1898–1918)

The era from the lease of the New Territories in 1898 through the conclusion of the First World War in 1918 represents a formative period in Hong Kong history [cite: 1, 2]. During these two decades, the colonial state expanded beyond its maritime urban core on Hong Kong Island and the Kowloon Peninsula into a larger hinterland [cite: 1, 3]. This spatial expansion triggered significant administrative, social, and economic reconfigurations [cite: 4, 5]. The transition was marked by indigenous rural armed resistance, structural spatial and racial segregation, severe public health crises, political anti-colonial boycotts, major infrastructure investments, and the global disruptions of World War I [cite: 1, 4, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10].

To construct a verifiable evidence base for public history, each statement and historical event must be linked directly to primary archival documentation, peer-reviewed scholarship, and reference repositories [cite: 2, 4, 6, 9, 11]. The synthesized data below details twenty-five key events, legislative enactments, public health crises, and social shifts that defined this period [cite: 1, 7, 8, 9, 10, 12].

## Key sources for this era: the 5–10 most useful collections, books or archives, with URLs.

* **UK National Archives Colonial Office Series (CO 129: War and Colonial Department and Colonial Office: Hong Kong Original Correspondence)**
  * *Description*: The primary archival record for colonial administration in Hong Kong, containing official despatches, draft legislation, minutes, confidential reports, and administrative communications between Governors and the Colonial Office [cite: 2, 5, 6, 9, 17].
  * *URL*: https://discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk/details/r/C1027
* **Hong Kong Government Reports Online (HKGRO) &amp; Sessional Papers Archive**
  * *Description*: A digitized collection hosted by the University of Hong Kong Libraries, providing open access to Sessional Papers, Administrative Reports, Blue Books, and Public Health Reports from 1842 to 1941 [cite: 4, 5, 9, 10, 15].
  * *URL*: https://hkgro.lib.hku.hk
* **Hong Kong Legislative Council Hansard &amp; Historical Ordinances Archive**
  * *Description*: The official record of proceedings, debates, and statutory enactments of the Legislative Council of Hong Kong, crucial for tracking legislative intent and economic policy [cite: 8, 12, 14, 20].
  * *URL*: https://www.legco.gov.hk/general/english/library/hist\_lp.html
* **Journal of the Royal Asiatic Society Hong Kong Branch (JRASHKB Archive)**
  * *Description*: The primary peer-reviewed academic journal focused on Hong Kong local history, rural New Territories studies, demographic analysis, and social development [cite: 4, 11, 15].
  * *URL*: https://www.jstor.org/journal/jroyaasiatsocp2
* **Hong Kong Public Libraries Multimedia Information System (MMIS) – Historical Newspapers**
  * *Description*: A comprehensive repository of digitised historic newspapers, including the *Hong Kong Daily Press*, *China Mail*, and Chinese-language periodicals documenting daily urban life and political movements.
  * *URL*: https://mmis.hkpl.gov.hk
* **Patrick H. Hase,** **The Six-Day War of 1899: Hong Kong in the Age of Imperialism** **(Hong Kong University Press, 2008)**
  * *Description*: Monograph detailing the British acquisition of the New Territories and the indigenous rural armed resistance, drawing from local lineage genealogies and British military records [cite: 1, 4, 11].
  * *URL*: https://hkupress.hku.hk/pro/175.php
* **John M. Carroll,** **A Concise History of Hong Kong** **(Hong Kong University Press, 2013)**
  * *Description*: A scholarly monograph summarizing socio-economic shifts, colonial governance strategies, elite compradors, and political relations between Britain, Hong Kong, and Mainland China [cite: 1, 13].
  * *URL*: https://hkupress.hku.hk/image/catalog/pdf-preview/9789888139286.pdf
* **Ka-che Yip,** **A Medical History of Hong Kong: 1842–1941** **(Hong Kong University Press, 2016)**
  * *Description*: The principal scholarly study detailing the institutional history of public health, epidemic prevention, plague control, and sanitary legislation in early colonial Hong Kong [cite: 9].
  * *URL*: https://hkupress.hku.hk/pro/1261.php

## Gaps: important topics where you could not find good evidence.

* **Informal Female Labor Networks and Domestic Service Records**: Primary institutional archives (such as CO 129 despatches and Legislative Council Sessional Papers) preserve administrative and legislative data on male coolie labor, construction gangs, and merchant guilds [cite: 5, 6, 9]. However, primary documentation detailing the informal economy of working-class Chinese women—such as domestic servants (*mui tsai*), laundresses, and street hawkers—remains limited outside of occasional missionary tracts and police court records [cite: 6, 16].
* **Indigenous New Territories Female Oral Histories (1898–1905)**: While the Six-Day War and Land Court title registrations are documented through British military despatches and village land deeds [cite: 1, 2, 4], the lived experiences and economic contributions of indigenous rural women during the political transition to British colonial rule are largely unrecorded in official government files.
* **Informal Chinese Silver Draft and Remittance Networks**: Major British financial institutions leave detailed corporate records, but non-institutional native Chinese draft shops (*yinhao*) and native remittance channels that handled trans-Pacific capital flows from overseas Chinese laborers through Hong Kong to Guangdong lack systematic archival documentation for the 1898–1918 era.
* **Daily Life and Informal Social Organizations of Non-Elite South Asian Personnel**: Official archives contain personnel rosters for European officers and high-level administrators [cite: 9]. However, primary evidence regarding the daily lives, informal social clubs, and sub-inspectoral networks of Indian police officers, Sikh guards, and Muslim marine workers remains under-represented in accessible digital public repositories.
